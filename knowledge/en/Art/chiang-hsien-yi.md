# Jiang Xian-er: Fled Taiwan for Forty-Five Years, Only After Returning Home Could He Paint the Best Works of His Life

> **30-Second Overview:** Jiang Xian-er was born in Taichung in 1942. After graduating from the Department of Art at National Taiwan Normal University, he went alone to Paris in the winter of 1967 hoping to study under Giacometti, only to learn upon arrival that his idol had passed away the previous year. For the next forty-five years, whether painting in Paris, New York, or Taipei, he habitually sealed all the windows shut: "I didn't want to see the natural light outside; I wanted to find the light inside my own heart." [^1] His first solo exhibition at New York's Lamagna Gallery in 1975 didn't sell a single painting. In the mid-1990s, after his father fell and was injured, he returned to Taipei and painted the "Century Temple" series amid the incense smoke of Longshan Temple and Baoan Temple. In 2007, he and his wife Fan Xiang-lan drove to Jinzun in Taitung; they settled there in 2008 and opened the windows — he was sixty-five years old. On March 15, 2025, the Jiang Xian-er Art Park officially opened in Jinzun; he was eighty-three.

An artist's most important body of work often isn't created when they're striving to become a master. It comes after they've given up on becoming one.

Jiang Xian-er lived outside Taiwan for forty-five years. For thirty of those years, the first thing he did upon moving into a new studio in a new city was seal the windows. The garret in Paris's Latin Quarter, the SoHo rental in New York, the leaky empty house on Jinzhou Street in Taipei [^2] — sealed until not a sliver of outside light could enter.

Then, in that pitch-black room, he searched for the light within his own heart.

He returned to Taiwan to settle down at age sixty-five. Seventeen years later, at eighty-three, the art park on the Taitung Jinzun coastline — designed by him and architect Lin You-han, funded over twelve years by a team led by Public Platform Cultural Foundation Chairman Yan Chang-shou — officially opened on March 15, 2025 [^3].

What opened wasn't just the windows.

## In the winter of 1967, when he arrived in Paris, Giacometti had been dead for a year

Jiang Xian-er was born in Taichung on February 1, 1942[^4]. His mother passed away when he was young, and his father did business overseas year-round. A sensitive child, his most constant companion was the classical music from the radio[^5]. He especially loved Mahler, the composer who wrote death and loneliness in music.

He studied painting under Li Shih-chiao at the Normal University Affiliated High School, and in 1960 entered the Art Department of Taiwan Provincial Normal University (now the Department of Fine Arts at National Taiwan Normal University)[^6]. At university he was guided by Liao Chi-chun and Chen Hui-kun. Once in a sketching class, Chen Hui-kun looked at his plaster cast drawing and said: "You don't need to draw plaster casts anymore!" Liao Chi-chun examined his painting in oil painting class and gave this comment: "This is a work of genius."[^7]

In January 1965, Jiang Xian-er was a senior. Together with classmates Yao Ching-chang and Ku Chung-kuang, they held the "Era Painting Society" first exhibition at the Taiwan Provincial Museum (now the National Taiwan Museum)[^8], a small starting point at the tail end of Taiwan's abstract painting movement in the 1960s. That June he graduated, and in September his work was selected for the São Paulo Biennial in Brazil[^8].

It was the best start a Taiwan art student could imagine.

But Jiang Xian-er had a greater obsession in his heart. He wanted to go to Paris to find Giacometti, the Swiss sculptor who expressed human loneliness, anxiety, and despair through slender human figures, who was his idol[^9].

In the winter of 1967, he flew to Paris alone. His wife Fan Hsiang-lan flew over to join him later[^10].

Not long after arriving in Paris, he learned a piece of news: Giacometti had passed away the previous year (1966)[^9].

"My heart was hollowed out."[^11]

He stayed in Paris, but his idol was gone. What to learn? How to learn? From May to June 1968, the student movement erupted in Paris, a nationwide general strike, even survival became a problem[^12]. Jiang Xian-er and Fan Hsiang-lan left Paris and turned to New York.

That year he was twenty-six. He never imagined that this departure from Taiwan would last forty-five years.

## Sealing the Windows, Finding the Light Within

In the 1970s, New York's SoHo was an artists' paradise before boutique stores moved in: warehouses, factories, cheap loft rents, streets full of contemporary abstract expressionist painters. Andy Warhol opened the Factory in the neighboring district, and Pop Art was at its peak. Jiang Xian-er moved there, but what he did was completely opposite to the art trends of that era in New York.

He sealed the windows.

Cardboard, fabric curtains, newspapers — whatever worked. They had to be sealed until not a sliver of natural light entered. Then he painted in that pitch-black room.

Forty years later, he explained this habit himself: "For thirty-some years in Paris, New York, even Taipei, no matter how large the windows, I sealed them all the same. The reason is simple: I didn't want to see the natural light outside. I wanted to find the light inside my own heart."[^1]

Works from that period are called the "Sealed Window Works"[^13]. Gloomy, minimalist, predominantly gray and black; sometimes only a single white line of light emerges from the darkness. Themes repeatedly invoke a few words: "emptiness, loneliness, hopelessness, death, distant funerals."[^13]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The common narrative says: Jiang Xian-er's thirty years in New York were a "period of immersion," "cultivation," "waiting for the right moment." This narrative is convenient, but it gets one thing backwards. He truly believed art shouldn't look outward, which is why he was willing to spend thirty years immersed. While other painters were depicting this world, he used thirty whole years to tell himself that the light outside this world doesn't matter. What matters is that beam inside.

## 1975 New York: Not a Single Painting Sold

In 1975, Jiang Xian-er was thirty-three years old and had already been in New York for seven years. Andy Warhol's agent, Ivan Karp, founder of the O.K. Harris Gallery, noticed him and recommended him for a solo exhibition at the Lamagna Gallery in SoHo[^14].

It was his first solo exhibition in New York.

The entire exhibition ended without a single painting sold[^14].

For the next nineteen years, he did not hold another solo exhibition in New York. It was not until December 1994 that Ivan Karp brought him back to his own O.K. Harris Gallery for a second solo exhibition[^15]. How did he survive during those nineteen years in between? How did he maintain his studio? How did he afford canvas and oil paints? These details are rarely mentioned in his own interviews. He only ever said one thing: "There are far too many people who want to paint, but far too few who become professional painters. You need patience, persistence, and you have to sacrifice a great deal to achieve it."[^16]

In the summer of 1982, at age forty, he briefly returned to Paris from New York and rented a garret above a cinema in the Latin Quarter[^17]. He boarded up the windows of that garret as well, and painted a series: "Notre-Dame de Paris."

Dark, heavy canvases, a few white lines tracing the arches, heavy brushstrokes picking out pure white light[^17]. What Jiang Xian-er wanted to express with paint was "the spiritually devout air": the part that remains after a popular tourist spot is stripped of its noisy reality.

Many years later, looking back at this series, he said: "Not until I painted the 'Notre-Dame de Paris' series did I feel I had achieved something, did I feel that art was a path I could walk, that I was qualified to be a painter in this lifetime."[^18]

It was at age forty that he finally felt qualified to be a painter. Before that, he had already been painting in Paris and New York for fifteen years.

## Longshan Temple's Incense, Baoan Temple's Pillars: The Year Father Fell

One day in the mid-1990s, Jiang Xian-er received a call from home in New York: his father had fallen in Taipei, was injured, and could no longer take a long-haul flight to New York to see him[^19].

That was the distance that had always existed between father and son. His father was a businessman, away overseas year-round; father and son interacted little, their connection tenuous. Jiang Xian-er himself had chosen a path that placed his home on the other side of the globe. But his father had grown old, had fallen, and could no longer fly.

Jiang Xian-er began shuttling between Taipei and New York.

In Taipei he found a dilapidated empty house on Jinzhou Street (it leaked when it rained, had mosquitoes in summer, no hot water in winter)[^2]. Living there, what he did was go to temples.

Longshan Temple, Baoan Temple, Mazu temples — he went to every one[^20]. He wasn't there to worship anything. He was there to look at the temples.

Those temple pillars, those plaque inscriptions, that incense smoke, those red papers with black characters written over a hundred years ago — things he hadn't seen in thirty years in Paris and New York — only now entered his vision.

The "Hundred-Year Temples" series grew out of that leaky empty house on Jinzhou Street[^20].

Like _Notre-Dame de Paris_, the paintings also used a somber black as ground, but there was something more in the black. Several streaks of bronze-gold light emerged from the depths of the canvas. It was the color that slowly grew on temple pillars touched by countless hands, smoked by centuries of incense, illuminated by sunlight slanting in from skywells.

In March 1997, Jiang Xian-er held a solo exhibition at Eslite Gallery in Taipei. The exhibition subtitle read: "First solo exhibition upon returning to Taiwan after 30 years apart."[^21]

He left Taiwan at twenty-five, held his first exhibition back at fifty-five. In those thirty years in between, his Taiwan classmates lost touch with him, Taiwan's art circle didn't know him, Taiwan's audiences had never seen his paintings.

In November 1998, he held his second solo exhibition at Eslite, formally debuting the "Hundred-Year Temples" series[^21].

It was the first time he let Taiwan's audiences see the paintings he had painted in Taiwan.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> An artist stays outside for thirty years, returns to Taiwan to exhibit — how would Taiwan's art critics view him? Probably not with the interest they'd show a newly debuted young artist, nor with the reverence they'd show a master already famous internationally for twenty years. When Jiang Xian-er exhibited in Taiwan, he was in an awkward position: neither a newcomer nor an international superstar, just a middle-aged man who had spent thirty years in New York without making it big, and whom few in Taipei recognized.
>
> The ones who caught him first were galleries and curators. In the 1999 Eslite Gallery 10th Anniversary curated exhibition, Wang Chia-chi selected "Hundred-Year Temples" for inclusion; from 1997 to 2005, Jiang Xian-er held a total of six solo exhibitions at Eslite, entered the 2008 Taiwan Biennial at the National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts, and the 2012 "Form of the Formless: Taiwan Abstract Art" at the Taipei Fine Arts Museum[^44]. What truly let him be seen by Taiwan's general audience was the 2020 retrospective at TFAM, curated again by Wang Chia-chi; he was seventy-eight that year.

## One Afternoon in 2007, They Drove Up to Jinzun

In 2007, Jiang Xian-er and his wife Fan Xiang-lan drove along Taitung's east coast. Provincial Highway 11 winds between mountains and sea, past Changbin and Sanxiantai, until the car reached a small village in Donghe Township called Jinzun, an old Amis place name.

They parked the car and walked up a small hill. The Pacific Ocean lay before them, the tail ridge of the Coastal Mountain Range behind, and on clear days Green Island could be seen in the distance[^22].

"But we felt this was it," Jiang Xian-er recalled of that afternoon[^23].

He was sixty-five.

That year, he broke ground in Jinzun, Donghe Township, Taitung County, and built a mountain-backed, sea-facing residence and studio[^24]. The following year (2008), the studio was completed, and he and Fan Xiang-lan moved down from Taipei to settle there permanently[^24].

From Paris, New York, Taipei, to Taitung's Jinzun, this was his fourth studio.

It was also the first studio where he didn't board up the windows.

> **💡 Did You Know**
> The stretch of coast next to the Jinzun studio is called _Pisirian_ in Amis, meaning "place where sheep are grazed": _pi_ is the verb "to pasture," _sili_ is "sheep," _an_ is "place"[^25]. Jiang Xian-er's first Taitung-period series was titled "Dreams of Pisirian." A New York painter who painted in black for thirty years suddenly saw colors explode onto his canvases after arriving at the "place where sheep are grazed."

## Gould's "Goldberg", and the Opened Window

"I've never used these colors before, never in my life imagined I could paint works like this." After moving to Taitung, Jiang Xian-er described the transformation of his paintings this way[^26].

The "Bisirian Dreams," "Wings on Song," "Jin Zun," and "Silver Lake" series emerged one after another after 2008[^27]. Blues, yellows, oranges, pinks began to appear. The sea, song, dreams began to appear[^27]. "The colors I use, the scale of my works, and the freedom — all came after moving to Taitung."[^28]

His daily routine became this:

Wake between 4 and 5 AM, brew a cup of coffee, play Gould's Bach _Goldberg Variations_, then wait for the sun to slowly climb up from behind Green Island, illuminating the slopes of Jin Zun and the Pacific.

> "Coffee is a must, then Bach is a must — I always play Gould's _Goldberg Variations_. You can see Green Island from here, the light slowly brightens, Bach piano music beside you, that state of being is really hard to describe."[^29]

> "After finishing the day's work, I sit down with a cup of coffee, watching the sun on the horizon use its last warmth to reflect the sea in orange-red and gold."[^30]

From morning Bach to afternoon sun, this is his every day after turning eighty.

The studio's open window faces the Pacific.

In a 2025 _Outstanding Figures_ interview, he said something weighty: "Taitung gave me a second artistic life."[^31]

A second. Meaning there was a first.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The first artistic life spanned forty-two years from 1965 to 2007: Paris, New York, Taipei. Thirty years with windows sealed. Thirty years searching for the light within. Thirty years without fame. The second artistic life is Taitung from 2008 to now. In these seventeen years of his seventies and eighties, he painted works better than the "best paintings" of his twenties, thirties, forties, and fifties combined. This itself is counterintuitive: artists typically peak in their fifties or sixties, but Jiang Xian-er's peak came this late — so late even he was surprised. The surprise is that he knows why it came late. He himself used the word "gave": "Taitung gave me." For a painter to acknowledge being nurtured by a piece of land — this is exceedingly rare in the traditional artist's self-mythology.

## From a Studio to an Art Park: Lin You-han, Yan Chang-shou, and Twelve Years

In the spring of 2020, seventy-eight-year-old Jiang Xian-er held his first retrospective at the Taipei Fine Arts Museum.

Art critic Wang Chia-chi served as guest curator for "Jiang Xian-er: Retrospective," which exhibited nearly two hundred works, from the 1960s to the new "Jinzun" series of 2020[^32]. The exhibition ran from March 28 to June 28, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, when many galleries and museums suspended operations, and the Taipei Fine Arts Museum also limited visitor flow. Even so, the exhibition attracted approximately 125,000 visits, setting a record for a solo artist exhibition during the pandemic[^32].

That was the first time Taiwan's art world fully saw him.

Wang Chia-chi's curation employed a reverse-chronological approach: the exhibition traced back from the colors of Jinzun to the darkness of New York, and further back to the young man who had just graduated from Shida (National Taiwan Normal University) in 1965[^33]. Walking through that path, viewers saw not "how a painter grew stronger," but "how a painter returned home."

After the retrospective, Public Welfare Platform Cultural Foundation Chairman Yan Chang-shou sought him out[^34].

Yan Chang-shou had been in Taitung for over a decade, accomplishing several things: establishing the Public Welfare Platform, founding the Junyi International School, promoting Hualien-Taitung education, and advancing the Taiwan Connection chamber music ensemble. He believed art could transform remote areas; he believed Hualien-Taitung should not be merely a rest stop for tourists, but an international-class cultural landmark. Jiang Xian-er had already lived in Jinzun for twelve years; he believed art could purify the human heart, and that a painter should not merely be a painter, but could be part of an art park[^35].

Their combined ages exceeded one hundred years.

They began a project: building an art park in Jinzun to exhibit Jiang Xian-er's paintings, allow creators to stay for short-term residencies and exchanges, and give Taitung's children a place to experience contemporary art from a young age.

The park was built on land donated by Jiang Xian-er himself, including his original studio and surrounding property[^36]. The architect chosen was Lin You-han, based in Germany[^37]. Though less known, he shared a deep tacit understanding with Jiang Xian-er on the principle that "architecture and nature should not oppress each other."

Lin You-han and Jiang Xian-er designed the park together. They did not want a single massive volume dominating the hilltop; instead, they divided the space into five small buildings, scattered like pebbles across the landscape[^37]:

- **Gongyuan Reception Center**: The first stop upon entering the park, handling ticketing and baggage storage[^43]
- **Xinyi Hall** (First Exhibition Hall): Before the park's full completion, this served as Jiang Xian-er's temporary studio[^43]
- **Qincheng Hall** (Second Exhibition Hall): Expanded from Jiang Xian-er's old studio; its "Meditation Space" is a permanent installation of the park[^43]
- **Chenghan Hall** (Third Exhibition Hall): Built based on Jiang Xian-er's sculpture "13.5 Ping," nicknamed "the Chapel"[^43]
- **Zhuhongting Hall**: Residence for the honorary director and visiting exchange artists; the first floor houses Debussy Café, the only dining area in the entire park[^43]

In his design, Lin You-han placed "light" as the top priority. In the galleries displaying Jiang Xian-er's Paris and New York period works, light is guided to an almost sacred quality; in the galleries showing his Taitung period works, light is relaxed and free-flowing, carrying vibrant colors and nature's rhythms[^37]. The light of sealed windows and the light of open windows coexist within the same park.

The park did not apply for government subsidies—funding came entirely from Jiang Xian-er himself and art-supporting entrepreneurs[^38]. At the opening, Yan Chang-shou said: "We don't want to seize social resources, but to use our own strength to realize a dream."[^39]

From the first conversation to the opening, they spent twelve years[^40].

On March 15, 2025, the Jiang Xian-er Art Park officially opened to the public[^41]. The inaugural exhibition was titled "Light, Beauty, and Purification"[^41].

That day, Jiang Xian-er was eighty-three years old.

## On His Eighty-Third Birthday, He Opened More Than a Window

Rewind to the winter of 1967——he was twenty-five, two years out of Normal University, flying to Paris on a pilgrimage to Giacometti, only to discover his idol had died the year before.

That day, he felt "hollowed out inside."

From that day to the opening of the Jinzun Art Park on March 15, 2025——fifty-eight years.

What happened in between:

He lived in Paris, New York, and Taipei for forty-five years; for thirty of those years he sealed every window to search for the light inside. His 1975 debut solo show in New York didn't sell a single painting. In 1982, in a Paris garret, he painted the "Notre-Dame de Paris" series——the first time he felt qualified to call himself a painter. A second New York solo show followed in 1994. In the mid-1990s his father fell; he returned to Taipei, moving into a leaking vacant house on Jinzhou Street, visiting Longshan Temple to study its pillars, and producing the "Centennial Temple" series. In 1997 he held his first Taiwan exhibition in thirty years, but no one in Taiwan knew him. At sixty-five, in 2008, he moved to Jinzun, Taitung, and opened a window for the first time. At seventy-eight, in 2020, the North America Museum mounted a retrospective——Taiwan saw him for the first time.

In 2025, at eighty-three, the Jinzun Art Park he designed with Lin You-han and built by Yan Chang-shou opened its doors.

He spent fifty-eight years painting the best works of his life. He spent the same fifty-eight years becoming a person willing to open the window.

In his later years he was often asked: What do you still want to do?

"I don't know how much longer I can paint, but what I want to do most now, and what I feel is most important, is to leave everything I've experienced in this life to the next generation."[^42]

"I'm willing to devote my whole life to creating until the end. I believe art can purify the human heart."[^35]

Art can purify the human heart.

Not a single word in that sentence is difficult. The difficulty lies in the person saying it——someone who had to live through fifty-eight years, seal windows for thirty, come home once, open a window, watch a stretch of Pacific turn from black to blue to orange, and listen to Bach's _Goldberg Variations_ repeat a thousand times.

> **✦** "Taitung gave me a second artistic life."

4:30 a.m., Jinzun. Jiang Xian-er brews a cup of coffee, drops the needle——the opening low G of Gould's _Goldberg Variations_ sounds; the Pacific is still black, Green Island's silhouette still in the mist. Coffee aroma and Bach's piano flow together beside the unsealed window.

At eighty-three, he watches the sea slowly turn from black to blue.

Another hour before the sun climbs up from behind Green Island.
