# The Misjudgment of "Zhiyu": Why a Single Word Can Be Both Taiwanese and Chinese Usage

> **30-second overview:** The Ministry of Education's _Revised Mandarin Chinese Dictionary_ entry for "tudou" gives three meanings at once: an alternate name for peanuts, the mainland term for potatoes, and a plant called hulucha. Meanwhile, the _Concise Mandarin Chinese Dictionary_ appendix "Cross-Strait Common Vocabulary Comparison Table" places "tudou" under the "potato" entry as a mainland usage, listing "yangyu" and "shanyao dan" in the same cell. Put these two official sources together and they suffice to explain why "is this zhiyu" rarely has a single answer.

---

## One Word, Three Meanings

First, let's see how the dictionary defines it. The Ministry of Education's _Revised Mandarin Chinese Dictionary_ entry for "tudou" reads: "Alternate name for 'peanut.' See 'peanut' entry. In mainland regions refers to potato. Alternate name for 'hulucha.' Perennial subshrub. Odd-pinnate compound leaves. Racemes, flowers pale purple. Pod fruit with five to eight segments." [^1]

Three things hold true simultaneously in this single entry. In Taiwan, "tudou" refers to peanuts — this is everyday usage. Across the strait, "tudou" refers to potatoes. And in botanical classification, "tudou" is again an alternate name for hulucha.

In other words, the same word occupies a position in three different referential systems. Any list that omits a "under what context" column risks mistaking one of those meanings for the whole.

## What the Official Comparison Table Looks Like

The other official source is the appendix "Cross-Strait Common Vocabulary Comparison Table" in the Ministry of Education's _Concise Mandarin Chinese Dictionary_. Its data sources are clearly stated: first, the Chinese Language Knowledge Base's "Cross-Strait Differing Vocabulary"; second, the _Cross-Strait Daily Word • General Noun Comparison Table_, both provided under authorization from the Chinese Culture Association. [^2]

The table has a two-column format: left column "Taiwan Vocabulary," right column "Mainland Vocabulary." Page 1 shows examples including:

| Taiwan Vocabulary | Mainland Vocabulary                                |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Biandang          | Hefan                                              |
| Bu luo ge         | Boke                                               |
| Bi dian           | Shou ti dian nao / Bi ji ben / Bi ji xing dian nao |
| Bao gu            | Bao xiu                                            |
| Ma ling shu       | Yang yu / Shan yao dan / Ma ling shu / Tu dou      |
| Mao xiong         | Xiong mao / Mao xiong / Da xiong mao               |
| Bao ling qiu      | Bao ling qiu / Di gun qiu                          |
| Mang guo          | Mang guo / Meng guo / Mang guo                     |

Understanding this format reveals its purpose: "comparison," not "prohibition." It assumes each side has its own set of usages, places them side by side, so the reader knows how the other side might speak. This is a completely different thing from a list that says "seeing these words means you've been subjected to united front work." [^2]

## Same Entry, Multiple Usages

The "potato" cell in the table lists four mainland usages: yangyu, shanyao dan, ma ling shu, tu dou. This means even on the mainland, the same thing has more than one name, and one of them — "ma ling shu" — is equally common in Taiwan.

The "mao xiong" (giant panda) cell illustrates the problem even more clearly. The mainland column lists "xiong mao / mao xiong / da xiong mao," three terms coexisting, among which "mao xiong" is identical to Taiwan's usage. If a list picks only "xiong mao" as the Chinese term and reserves "mao xiong" for Taiwan, it manufactures a boundary that does not exist within the same entry. [^2]

The "mang guo" (mango) cell lists "mang guo / meng guo / mang guo" — three variant-character forms of the same fruit, differing in orthography and regional habit, not political stance. The "bao ling qiu" (bowling) cell brings in "di gun qiu," a usage in some mainland regions, listed alongside Taiwan's "bao ling qiu." [^2]

These examples point to the same conclusion: the comparison table records "differences," not "us versus them." Treating it as a judgment tool runs into massive one-cell-multiple-terms situations, and each cell then demands a second judgment: "which one actually counts."

## Why Judgment Goes Awry

From the two sources above, three mechanisms that destabilize judgment can be identified — and none requires citing internet disputes to verify.

The first is polysemy. "Tu dou" in Taiwan means peanuts; on the mainland it means potatoes. The dictionary puts both meanings in the same entry. To see this word and declare "this is zhiyu" is to ignore its original Taiwan usage. [^1]

The second is multiple names for a single concept. The "potato" entry lists four; any list that recognizes only one will misjudge the other three as "not mainland usage." Conversely, it may also misjudge "ma ling shu" — which Taiwan has always used — as a mainland term. [^2]

The third is variant characters and regional variants. "Mang guo / meng guo / mang guo," "mao xiong / xiong mao / da xiong mao" are all different writings or names for the same thing. Splitting them into two camps makes the list longer and longer, yet harder and harder to use. [^2]

These three mechanisms share a common feature: they all occur below the level of the "word." To judge a word, you must first know its context, its referent, and who is using it — and that information is not in the list.

## Replace "Is It" with "How Is It Used"

This article does not argue that "zhiyu does not exist" or that "using zhiyu doesn't matter." It argues something else: using a context-free list to judge a word yields a high error rate, and the errors do not necessarily favor either side.

What official sources can do is provide a verifiable starting point. The MOE dictionary tells you how many meanings a word has; the comparison table tells you how both sides might say it. They cannot tell you "should you use it," because that has never been the dictionary's job.

The truly actionable approach is to swap the question from "is this zhiyu" to "how is this word used in Taiwan, where does it come from, is there a more precise way to say it." The answer to the first question changes with the list version; the answer to the second can be verified entry by entry, and once verified, you will know what you are saying.
