> **30-second overview:** The development of Christianity in Taiwan is a long marathon from "misunderstanding" to "symbiosis." 19th-century missionaries pried open society's doors with "a Bible in one hand, forceps in the other"—George Leslie Mackay alone extracted over 20,000 teeth. Having endured Japanese-era pressure to worship at Shinto shrines and the post-war "flour church" years of US aid relief, this force ultimately turned political during the martial law era. The Presbyterian Church risked everything to issue its Human Rights Declaration, becoming a voice that could not be ignored in Taiwan's democratization process. Today, Mormon missionaries in white shirts riding bicycles have become another distinctive sight on Taiwan's streets.

On June 16, 1865, Scottish physician James Laidlaw Maxwell rented a one-story house on Kanxi Street in Tainan. The front half served for preaching, the back half for treating patients. This was Taiwan's first Western medical clinic, yet it survived only 23 days. At the time, rumors spread through the prefectural city that these "red-haired barbarians" would gouge out people's eyes and hearts to make medicine. Enraged crowds surrounded the clinic, and Maxwell was forced to flee to Takow (modern-day Kaohsiung). [^1]

This "eye-gouging and heart-excavating" farce was the true opening act of Christianity taking root in Taiwan.

### A Bible in One Hand, Dental Forceps in the Other

If Maxwell used a medical scalpel to open the way in southern Taiwan, then the pioneer of the north, George Leslie Mackay, relied on a pair of dental forceps. Arriving in Tamsui in 1872, Mackay discovered that Taiwanese people suffered terribly from toothaches yet had nowhere to seek treatment. He began his itinerant preaching by singing hymns in open spaces, then extracting teeth for the populace free of charge. [^2]

According to Mackay's 1895 autobiographical memoir, over his thirty years in Taiwan he personally extracted more than 21,000 teeth. [^3] This strategy of "first relieve physical suffering, then deliver the gospel of the soul" proved remarkably successful. The dental instruments themselves were even forged by local Taiwanese blacksmiths following Mackay's specifications. Medical services not only reduced popular rejection of the "foreign religion," but also allowed Christianity to establish deep trust at the grassroots level.

📝 Curator's note: In an era without anesthesia, 20,000 teeth are not merely a statistic—they represent 20,000 direct, painful, and profound connections with local people.

### The Japanese Colonial "Fumi-e": Conflict Between Cross and Rising Sun Flag

Entering the Japanese colonial period, Christianity faced institutionalized challenges. In the 1930s, as Japanese militarism rose, the Governor-General's Office promoted the _Kominka_ (Japanization) movement, requiring all religious organizations in Taiwan to worship at Shinto shrines. For Christians who insisted "you shall have no other gods besides God," this was an extreme test of faith.

At the time, Tainan Presbyterian Middle School (today's Chang Jung High School) faced the dismissal of its principal and the expulsion of its missionaries because it refused to lead students in Shinto worship. [^11] To survive, some churches had no choice but to define shrine worship as a "non-religious" "national ritual"—a compromise later scholars have described as a modern-day _fumi-e_. [^12] During this period, the church survived in the cracks, yet also honed the resilience that would later enable it to resist authoritarian rule.

### US Aid and the "Flour Church": The Gospel That Filled Bellies

In the early post-war years, Taiwan suffered extreme material shortages. During the US aid period from 1951 to 1965, Protestant and Catholic churches became crucial channels for distributing relief supplies. Many Taiwanese went to church not to hear sermons, but to receive flour, powdered milk, soybean oil, and used clothing bearing the "Sino-American Cooperation" handshake logo. [^13]

"Attend service, get flour" became a shared memory of that generation, and Christianity was consequently nicknamed the "flour church." [^14] Although some believers left the church once material distribution ceased, this period undeniably embedded Christianity deep within Taiwan's grassroots families, even altering Taiwanese dietary habits (such as the popularization of wheat-based food culture).

### Bicycle Silhouettes on Country Lanes

In an era of inconvenient transportation, the bicycle was the "gospel chariot" that carried missionaries into remote villages. In Taitung, Dr. Frank Dennis, founder of Taitung Christian Hospital, frequently rode a "second-hand motorcycle" or bicycle along the East Coast in the early days. Locals recall that when Dr. Dennis switched from motorcycle to bicycle, it was usually because he had saved the gasoline money to help the poor. [^9]

In Puli, Nantou, the "Puli Grandpa" from Norway, Bjarne Gislefoss, became the exemplar of bicycle ministry. He often cycled along Puli's country paths, caring for children with polio and orphans. [^10] For many older Taiwanese, these tall, blond, blue-eyed foreigners—riding bicycles, speaking halting Taiwanese or Indigenous languages—were the warmest embodiment of Christianity.

Additionally, on Taiwan's streets there is another group: young foreign missionaries in white shirts, black trousers, wearing helmets, riding bicycles in pairs through city streets and country lanes. They are missionaries of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (commonly known as the Mormon Church). [^15] They typically come from Utah, USA, volunteer for missions in Taiwan after high school, and work hard to learn fluent Mandarin, becoming a unique feature of Taiwan's urban and rural landscapes. [^16]

### From Altar to Street: The Political Thunderclap of 1977

In the latter half of the 20th century, the complexion of Taiwanese Christianity underwent a dramatic shift. After 1945, the "Mandarin-speaking churches" that arrived with the KMT largely supported the party-state system; however, the Presbyterian Church, which had cultivated Taiwan for a century using mother tongues and working at the grassroots, gradually fused with Taiwan's indigenous destiny. [^5]

On August 16, 1977, amid panic over the possibility of the United States breaking diplomatic relations with the Republic of China, the Presbyterian Church in Taiwan issued the shocking Human Rights Declaration. The declaration explicitly asserted: "Taiwan's future should be determined by Taiwan's 17 million residents," and urged the government to "make Taiwan a new and independent nation." [^6] This was the first time during the martial law era that a local organization publicly advocated Taiwan independence. Leaders such as Rev. Kao Chun-ming were subsequently imprisoned.

### Modern Challenges: When Conservative and Progressive Meet in the Sanctuary

Entering the 21st century, Christianity in Taiwan faces new internal fractures. A 2024 church census report shows Taiwan's Christian proportion at approximately 3% to 4%, and declining. [^7]

An even more striking challenge comes from social issues. Over the past decade, Taiwan's churches have been embroiled in fierce debate over "marriage equality." Although there are voices within the Presbyterian Church supporting gender equality, most conservative churches (such as Living Bread Church, Mandarin Worship Halls, etc.) became the core force opposing same-sex marriage. [^8] This image shift from "democracy's driving force" to "cultural conservative power" has also triggered a re-examination of the church's role by the younger generation.

📝 Curator's note: The history of Christianity in Taiwan is an evolutionary history of constant tension between "foreign" and "indigenous," "medical" and "political," "conservative" and "progressive."
