# Market Names

> **30-Second Overview:**
> "Market names" refer to those names that, when shouted in a traditional market, cause countless people to turn around simultaneously. This is no joke — according to the Ministry of the Interior's 2024 latest statistics, "Shu-fen" has over 30,000 people nationwide, while "Chia-hao" has more than 14,000. They have respectively held the top two spots for men's and women's market names for over a decade.[^1] These names reflect not parental laziness, but Taiwan society's collective consensus across different eras on "what makes a good name."

---

## Shu-fen and Chih-ming: The Fifth- and Sixth-Grade Standard

If you have an elder named "Shu-fen," "Shu-hui," or "Mei-ling" around you, that is perfectly normal.

In the 1960s to 1970s (Republic of China years 50s and 60s), Taiwan parents' expectations for daughters were remarkably consistent: to be virtuous (shu), beautiful (mei), and blessed with grace (hui). The name "Shu-fen" peaked in that era, and even as of 2026, the number of people named "Shu-fen" in Taiwan still ranks first nationwide, with the vast majority concentrated in the 50-and-above age group.[^2]

For men, it was the world of "Chih-ming," "Chih-cheng," and "Wen-hsiung." The reason Mayday's hit song "Chih-ming and Chun-chiao" resonated so deeply is precisely because these two names were the synonyms for "ordinary Taiwanese man and woman" at the time.[^3]

---

## Ya-ting and Yi-chun: The Seventh- and Eighth-Grade "Ya, Yi" War

By the 1980s (Republic of China year 70s), Taiwan's economy took off, and naming began to become more "refined."

This generation was the battleground of "Ya-ting" and "Yi-chun." These two names were extremely common among women born between 1980 and 1995. Statistics once noted that on certain university admission lists, the number of "Ya-ting"s was so high they had to be numbered.[^4] Unlike the previous generation's pursuit of traditional virtues, this generation's names added a wish for "poise" and "joy" (ya, yi).

Men entered the era of "Chia-hao" and "Chih-hao." The character "hao" symbolized parents' hope that their sons would become heroes and achieve career success.

---

## Cheng-en and Tzu-ching: Post-Ninth-Grade "Fantasy Novel Style"

If you look at current elementary and junior high school rosters, you will find "Shu-fen" has completely disappeared.

After 2010 (Republic of China year 100), Taiwan's naming trends underwent a dramatic shift. The new generation of parents, deeply influenced by pop culture and web fiction, chose names that read more like romance novels or fantasy dramas:

- **Men**: Cheng-en, Yu-ting, Pin-jui.
- **Women**: Yung-ching, Tzu-ching, Pin-yen.[^5]

"En," "jui," and "pin" became the new generation of parents' keywords. This shift shows that Taiwanese people's definition of a "good name" has moved from traditional character expectations toward greater emphasis on euphony and individual uniqueness (even though the results still end up looking very similar).

---

## Reduplication Trend: Pin-pin and Ting-ting

In 2023, the Ministry of the Interior released statistics on "reduplicative names" for the first time.

Over 60,000 people in Taiwan have reduplicative names, with "Pin-pin" leading for men and "Ting-ting" for women.[^6] This naming style was common in early film and TV works (such as Lin Pin-pin) or stage names, but some parents also choose it hoping their child will sound more affectionate and cute.

---

## The Salmon Chaos: Taiwanese People's "Flexibility" with Names

In March 2021, Taiwan experienced the "Salmon Chaos" that shocked the international community.

Because the conveyor-belt sushi chain Sushiro launched a promotion offering free meals to anyone whose name contained the characters for "salmon" (gui-yu), a staggering 331 people across Taiwan rushed to household registration offices to change their names within two days. Some changed to "Chang-chien Kuei-yu," others to bizarre names up to 36 characters long.[^7]

Behind this "chaos" lies the uniqueness of Taiwan's _Name Act_: Taiwanese people have the right to change their names three times in their lives. This makes a name in Taiwan not just a parental imprint, but more like an administrative tool that can be "corrected" or "exchanged for benefits" at any time. As of 2025, statistics showed that about 20% of that year's "salmon clan" still had not changed their names back.[^8]

---

## Why This Matters

A name is an island's collective memory.

When you call out "Ya-ting," you are summoning not just a woman, but a generation that lived through Taiwan's economic miracle, exam pressure, and the rise of the internet. When you see a child named "Cheng-en," you see a group of modern parents wanting to give their children more "ethereal" names.

Market names are nothing to be ashamed of; they are evidence of our shared lives.
