# Wet Market Names

> **30-Second Overview:**
> "Wet market names" (菜市場名) refers to those names that, if shouted in a crowded wet market, would cause countless people to turn around simultaneously. This is no joke; according to the latest 2024 statistics from the Ministry of the Interior, there are over 30,000 people named "Shufen" (淑芬) and over 14,000 named "Jia-hao" (家豪) across Taiwan. They have respectively held the top and runner-up spots for male and female wet market names for over a decade. [^1] These names do not reflect parental laziness, but rather the collective consensus in Taiwanese society regarding "what constitutes a good name" across different eras.

## Shufen and Zhiming: The Standard for the 5th and 6th Generations

If you have an elder around you named "Shufen" (淑芬), "Shuhui" (淑惠), or "Meiling" (美玲), this is perfectly normal.

During the 1960s and 1970s (the 50s and 60s of the Minguo era), Taiwanese parents' expectations for daughters were very consistent: they should be virtuous (_shu_ 淑), beautiful (_mei_ 美), and gracious (_hui_ 惠). The name "Shufen" reached its peak during that era; even today in 2026, the number of people named "Shufen" in Taiwan remains the highest, with the vast majority concentrated in the population aged 50 and above. [^2]

Meanwhile, men's names were dominated by "Zhiming" (志明), "Zhicheng" (志成), and "Wenxiong" (文雄). The reason Mayday's famous song 〈Zhiming and Chunjiao〉 resonates so deeply is that these two names were synonymous with "ordinary Taiwanese men and women" at the time. [^3]

---

## Ya-ting and Yi-jun: The "Ya and Yi" War of the 7th and 8th Generations

By the 1980s (the 70s of the Minguo era), as Taiwan's economy took off, naming conventions began to become a bit more "elegant."

This generation saw the war between "Ya-ting" (雅婷) and "Yi-jun" (怡君). These two names are extremely common among women born between 1980 and 1995. Statistics have noted that in some university enrollment lists, there were so many "Ya-tings" that they had to be assigned numbers. [^4] Unlike the naming style of their elders, which pursued traditional virtues, this generation's names added a wish for "elegance" (_ya_ 雅) and "happiness" (_yi_ 怡).

For males, the era of "Jia-hao" (家豪) and "Zhi-hao" (志豪) arrived. The character _hao_ (豪) symbolizes parents' hopes that their sons will become heroes or successful in their careers.

---

## Cheng-en and Zi-qing: The "Fantasy Novel Style" After the 9th Generation

If you look at the rosters of current elementary and junior high school students, you will find that "Shufen" has completely disappeared.

After 2010 (the centennial year of the Minguo era), naming trends in Taiwan underwent a drastic shift. New-generation parents are heavily influenced by popular culture and web literature, making names sound more like those from romance novels or fantasy dramas:

- **Males**: Cheng-en (承恩), You-ting (宥廷), Pin-rui (品睿).
- **Females**: Yong-qing (詠晴), Zi-qing (子晴), Pin-yan (品妍). [^5]

"En" (恩), "Rui" (睿), and "Pin" (品) have become keywords for the new generation of parents. This shift shows that the Taiwanese definition of a "good name" has moved from traditional expectations of character toward an emphasis on phonetic beauty and individual uniqueness (though the end results often still look quite similar).

---

## The Reduplication Trend: Bin-bin and Ting-ting

In 2023, the Ministry of the Interior conducted its first statistics on "reduplicated names" (疊字名字).

Over 60,000 people in Taiwan have reduplicated names; among males, "Bin-bin" (彬彬) is the most common, while among females, "Ting-ting" (婷婷) takes the lead. [^6] This naming style is common in early film and television works (such as Lin Bin-bin) or stage names, but it is also a choice made by parents who want their children to sound more affectionate and cute.

---

## Salmon Chaos: Taiwanese "Flexibility" Regarding Names

In March 2021, the "Salmon Chaos" (鮭魚之亂) occurred in Taiwan, shocking the international community.

Because Sushiro launched a promotion where customers with "salmon" in their names could eat for free, 331 people across Taiwan rushed to household registration offices to change their names within two days. Some changed their names to "Chang-chien Salmon" (張簡鮭魚), while others adopted bizarre names spanning 36 characters. [^7]

This "chaos" actually reflects the uniqueness of Taiwan's _Name Act_: Taiwanese citizens have the right to change their names three times in their lives. This makes a name in Taiwan not just a mark given by parents, but an administrative tool that can be "corrected" or used to "exchange for benefits" at any time. As of 2025, statistics show that about 20% of the "Salmon Family" from that year have still not changed their names back. [^8]

---

## Why This Matters

A name is the collective memory of an island.

When you call out "Ya-ting," you are not just calling a woman, but a generation that experienced Taiwan's economic miracle, academic pressure, and the rise of the internet. When you see a child named "Cheng-en," you see a group of modern parents who want to give their children names with more "ethereal" (仙氣) qualities.

Wet market names are not something to be ashamed of; they are evidence of the lives we have lived together.
