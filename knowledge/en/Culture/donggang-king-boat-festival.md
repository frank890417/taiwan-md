# Donggang's Welcoming the King Peace Festival

In Donggang, Pingtung, the king ship is not a vessel meant to cross the sea, yet it is treated as a voyage that must be completed. Every three years, the Donglongong Temple in Donggang welcomes the king deities who patrol on heaven's behalf, and the local community hosts them with eight days and seven nights of rituals, processions, feasts, and the king ship dharma assembly. Finally, this ship—constructed from wood, painted, decorated with objects, and built through human labor—is sent into flames at the seaside. This ending, which appears to be "burning things away," actually sends off disease, misfortune, wishes, local commitments, and an entire system of social division of labor, while leaving behind the memory of the next triennial festival[^1][^2].

## 30-Second Overview

Donggang's Welcoming the King Peace Festival is a nationally designated important folk practice held every three years at Donglongong Temple in Donggang, Pingtung. The festival includes not only the final burning of the king ship, but also continuous rituals of inviting the king, processions, the king ship dharma assembly, ship relocation, and sending off the king. The king ship is therefore not an isolated craft object, but the central object around which the entire festival operates[^1][^3].

What makes this cultural practice most worth understanding is not its "mystique" or "spectacle," but rather how it allows a fishing port to transform faith into visible public cooperation: some build the ship, some carry the palanquins, some conduct the rituals, some prepare food, some return home to participate. Donggang residents even use "which triennial cycle" to mark marriages, births, and life milestones, demonstrating that the festival has become the local community's shared calendar[^4][^5].

## More Than a King Ship Festival: How Eight Days and Seven Nights Form a Place

The basic concept of "welcoming the king" is to invite the king deities to patrol on heaven's behalf, enter the locality to inspect and drive away misfortune, and then send the king back after the mission is complete. Donggang's festival unfolds according to ancient ritual, with the king invited at the seaside, after which the royal procession enters the settlement for circumambulation. The king ship dharma assembly and loading gradually equip the ship with the objects and order a proper vessel requires. The Ministry of Agriculture's introduction also summarizes the overall process as inviting the king, circumambulation, the king ship dharma assembly and honoring the king, ship relocation and circumambulation, and finally sending off the king[^1].

This sequence cannot be simplified into a tourist itinerary. Each stage draws different people in: the seaside invitation transforms the harbor back into a ritual space, the procession makes streets a shared pathway, the king ship dharma assembly transforms the ship into a "royal palace" carrying sacred duties, ship relocation temporarily makes land serve as sea surface, and finally the sending-off completes the departure. The Research Center for Traditional Arts at Taipei National University of the Arts points out that the festival simultaneously contains Daoist rituals, Confucian ceremony, king ship construction, intricate organizational division of labor, and maritime cultural context. This is precisely why it should not be reduced to the two words "temple fair"[^5].

## Ritual Is Not an Itinerary: Who Does What During Eight Days and Seven Nights

The reason Donggang's welcoming the king process deserves to be unpacked is that each named stage corresponds to different spaces and work. The National Cultural Heritage Network records thirteen main procedures of the festival, while official religious and cultural materials further clarify that the king ship begins preparation roughly a year before the festival; ship construction, dragon keel installation, inviting the king, circumambulation, the king dharma assembly, and sending off are not hastily assembled activities[^3][^10]. The table below cross-references the process from public records with "ritual actions" and "local work," helping readers understand how the festival operates.

| Stage                                     | Ritual Action                                                                                                                  | Local Work and Cultural Significance                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pre-construction                          | Dragon keel installation, ship launching participation in construction preparation                                             | Craft masters, temple, and local organizations confirm ship body, materials, and timeline, giving a symbolic vessel executable ritual function.[^10]                      |
| Inviting the King and Water               | Welcoming the king deities and water routes at the seaside                                                                     | Transform harbor and coastline into festival entrance, establishing spatial order for the king's entry into and patrol of the locality.[^1][^10]                          |
| Circumambulation and King Dharma Assembly | Royal procession, ritual performance, and feasting                                                                             | Jiaotou, palanquin bearers, performance groups, households, and temple divide labor; streets become pathways residents walk together.[^4][^5]                             |
| Loading and Ship Relocation               | Prepare food, clothing, shelter, and other objects according to the ship cabin ledger, giving the king ship royal palace order | Residents entrust life objects and wishes to the king ship; items have fixed positions and cannot be arbitrarily added or removed based on appearance alone.[^3][^10]     |
| Sending Off                               | Complete final rituals and cremate the king ship at the seaside before dawn                                                    | Flames symbolize sending off the mission and misfortune; rules like closing umbrellas, removing hat ribbons, and ceasing noise mark the sacred moment of sending off.[^3] |

This table also clarifies that welcoming the king cannot be understood only as "burning the ship at the end." If one only photographs the flames, one will miss the preparation beginning a year earlier, household loading, palanquin bearer transmission, and the rearrangement of coastal space. The festival's weight lies not only in the final few minutes, but in many people over a long time linking different tasks together, allowing the king ship to depart according to locally recognized rules.

## Why the King Ship Matters: One Object Connecting Deities and Residents

The king ship's function is not decoration. For believers, it is the transportation vehicle for the king deities' patrol of the locality and the ritual medium for carrying plague and misfortune away from the territory. For craft masters, it is a wooden vessel requiring handling of hull, cabin, painting, and decoration. For local organizations, it is a public project requiring time, funding, materials, and shared technical expertise[^2][^6].

Therefore, the king ship's most moving characteristic is its "temporality." It is carefully crafted, yet not meant for long-term preservation. The more complete it is, the better it can fulfill the sending-off mission in its final moment. This object philosophy differs from the modern museum's habit of collecting, preserving, and displaying, and reminds us: cultural heritage does not necessarily equal an object that remains unchanged forever; sometimes it more closely resembles a set of relationships that can only be re-enacted through participation.

_Sinorama_ magazine's reporting records that Donggang's wooden king ships use fishing vessel construction methods, with the ship's interior and exterior configured with crew quarters, living spaces, and symbolic objects. Official materials indicate that early king ships were made of paper, but after 1973 shifted to wooden construction, using traditional Chinese sailing vessels as reference and scaled down to roughly one-tenth the size of actual vessels as symbolic hulls[^6][^10]. This transformation is not merely a material substitution; it also shows that the festival adjusts its form as local techniques, resources, and aesthetic conditions change. Reports also note that in the past there were more local shipbuilders taking turns constructing the ship, but now technical transmission faces pressure from aging masters and reduced labor[^6]. The importance of this information is not to label the king ship as "ancient," but to show: the festival's future depends on whether shipbuilding techniques can continue to be learned and practiced under new living conditions.

## The Seven Jiaotou and Palanquin Bearers: The True Organizational Backbone of the Festival

Large festivals are often filmed as scenes of tens of thousands gathered, but crowds are not the festival's organization. Donggang's welcoming the king is structured by the jiaotou, palanquin bearers, and festival roles that have formed over the local community's long history. Research published in the Taiwan Historical Records, using Dingtou Jiao as an example, points out that the seven jiaotou palanquin bearers are important operational actors in the festival, with many rituals and roles preserved through local interviews, actual participation, and generational transmission[^4].

A tension worth preserving also exists here: scholars' explanations of king deities, plague, and sending-off may not entirely align with understandings transmitted across generations by Donggang residents. This does not mean one side is necessarily correct and the other superstition. Rather, it shows that folk practice is a place where multiple knowledge systems intersect. If one only uses external theory to explain residents' faith, it easily overlooks how local people remember, how they divide labor, and why they are willing to return every three years for the festival cycle.

Donggang's welcoming the king transmission is also not closed and unchanging. As population mobility and declining birth rates increase, roles originally undertaken by families or local youth gradually need participation from friends or those who have moved from elsewhere. Transmission therefore is not copying the past unchanged, but finding ways for new people to enter the locality while ritual rules remain respected.

## Burning the King Ship: Ending Is Not Destruction, but Sending Responsibility to the Boundary

Sending off is the most visually striking moment of the entire festival: the king ship loaded with offerings, final rituals held at the seaside, then ignition. Observers easily remember only the flames, but the ritual's meaning lies in "sending out" rather than "burning away." The king ship receives the mission after the king deities' patrol, and the flames push it beyond the locality's boundary. After sending off, people lower their flags and disperse, symbolizing not bringing back the misfortune that has been sent away[^2][^6].

Yet this tradition also faces new challenges in modern public space. Increased participants, accelerated image transmission, and the risk that a solemn plague-sending ritual might be transformed into carnival by viewing demands. Large crowds, fireworks, burning, and traffic management require coordination between organizers and public departments. Understanding the festival does not mean demanding it remain in an imagined "pure tradition," but rather asking: between safety, environment, and religious autonomy, how can the locality find sustainable approaches for the next cycle.

## From Faith to Cultural Heritage: Who Has the Right to Explain Its Meaning

The National Cultural Heritage Network lists Donggang's Welcoming the King Peace Festival as a folk practice cultural asset, emphasizing the festival's connection to Donggang's regional development history and the king ship's functions of blessing, disaster prevention, and calamity resolution[^3]. Registering as cultural heritage gives this local practice greater public visibility and brings preservation resources, educational promotion, and research opportunities. Taipei National University of the Arts' field courses bring students into Donggang's settlement, temples, processions, king ship dharma assemblies, and shipbuilding craft sites, making "cultural heritage" not just a line in a registry but an observable, questionable, and learnable living space[^5].

However, heritage designation must avoid turning living faith into a fixed specimen. The king ship will burn, palanquin bearers will change, rituals will respond to epidemics and population changes—these shifts do not automatically equal decline. What truly needs preservation may be the locality's collective decision-making, technical transmission, role division, and the right to explain ritual meaning, rather than only one year's king ship appearance.

## What Donggang's Welcoming the King Leaves Taiwan to Consider

Research indexed in Airiti Library, using merchants and residents as subjects, has discussed the impact of Donggang's king ship festival on local recreational development. This reminds us that the festival does not simply choose between sacred and secular, but actually enters local economy, tourism, and residents' lives[^7]. Research from the Institute of Ethnology at Academia Sinica approaches the ritual and ship relocation procedures, showing that sending off is not a suddenly appearing flame scene, but a complete action composed of multiple ritual steps[^8].

For outsiders, the best way to participate is not to seek only the most spectacular images, but first to understand which spaces can be observed and which rituals are not open to the public, respecting residents' daily life and religious taboos, and not treating believers' faith as exotic spectacle. For Donggang, what matters most is not having everyone visit once, but ensuring the next generation still has opportunities to learn shipbuilding, carry palanquins, conduct rituals, prepare food, and explain their own festival.

Donggang's Welcoming the King Peace Festival is therefore an ideal cultural question for Taiwan: why is a ship known to be burned worth years of preparation? The answer lies not only in the deities, but in people's mutual trust. Someone passes technique to the next person, someone brings family memory back to the streets, someone lets outside visitors know when to draw near and when to step back. The king ship eventually leaves Donggang, but what it leaves behind is proof that the local community can still complete something together.

## One Ship's Life History: From Timber to Loading

King ship construction usually does not begin with "making an exhibit," but with the roles and functions the festival requires. The hull must be able to bear the symbolic royal palace order, the ship must have space for the king deities and their retinue, and the ship's appearance must be marked with painting, dragon patterns, flags, and bow decoration to show it is not an ordinary fishing vessel. Each element has its proper place and cannot be arbitrarily added or removed simply to pursue beauty. Shipbuilding masters therefore face two simultaneous demands: on one hand, mastery of wood, joinery, and waterproofing techniques; on the other, knowledge of what objects belong where and what role they play in ritual.

_Sinorama_ magazine's reporting mentions that Donggang's king ship construction methods were cultivated by the local fishing vessel industry. This clue is important because it places the festival back in the harbor's labor history, rather than imagining the king ship as a mysterious object unrelated to local economy. Donggang's shipbuilding technique did not appear from nowhere; it is connected to fishing, the harbor, master-apprentice transmission, and a group of craftspeople able to shift between different kinds of work. As the fishing industry shrinks, king ship technique becomes not only an abstract "cultural preservation" question, but a real question of whether masters still have work and apprentices still have time to learn[^2].

Loading allows residents to enter the king ship in another way. The ship cabin ledger is not an ordinary shopping list, but a specification of order for the ship's navigation, insignia, documents, living quarters, and grooming objects. Some provide daily necessities, some prepare provisions, some entrust misfortune they wish sent away to ritual handling. These supplies are not merely items on a list; they bring residents' lives into the king ship. When the ship is burned, what is sent away is not an empty vessel, but the locality's provision for the king deities, wishes for peace, and shared memory of "having prepared together."

## Donggang People's Time: Why "Cycle" Becomes a Life Coordinate

The three-year cycle gives the festival periodicity. It is neither like daily incense offerings that occur constantly, nor like ceremonies appearing only on specific commemorative dates. The three years between cycles allow shipbuilding, palanquin bearer training, role assignment, family discussion, and local anticipation to accumulate gradually. When the next cycle arrives, children who have left Donggang for school or work may return home, young people may encounter the work for the first time, and elders pass on experience from the previous cycle to the next generation. This periodicity places different age groups on the same timeline.

Taiwan Historical Records' research particularly reminds us that scholars' academic explanations and residents' transmitted understanding may not be identical. This also explains why "cycle" is more than just a calendar term. For researchers, it can distinguish festival years and materials. For Donggang residents, it may be the life memory of which cycle a family member married, which cycle a child was born, or which cycle one began serving as a palanquin bearer. When cultural heritage preserves only procedures, it misses this way of measuring human life by the festival.

This sense of time also makes welcoming the king a return-home institution. It does not necessarily require everyone to stay permanently in their birthplace, yet allows those who leave to still have a place to return. Some return to help for a few days, some relearn family roles, some only meet with relatives before and after sending off. Local community continuity sometimes is not daily close shared living, but knowing how to reassemble at crucial moments.

## Seeing the Festival's Boundaries: Participants, Residents, and Visitors

The welcoming the king festival attracts visitors, but "seeing" does not equal "possessing." Donggang's streets, temple grounds, coastline, and ship-building workshops are simultaneously ritual spaces and places where residents live. When photographing processions, the camera may capture performance groups and crowds, yet cannot capture the work residents undertake for traffic, cleaning, rest, and safety. If the festival is treated only as freely available image material, the locality's contributions become hidden.

Taipei National University of the Arts' field courses provide a better reference through methods of visiting, observing, collecting, and sharing. Rather than first translating the festival into beautiful photographs, they let learners enter the settlement and temple spaces, understanding how performance groups, king ship dharma assemblies, visual arts, and faith meaning connect to each other[^5]. For general visitors, this means first reading activity guidelines, respecting rituals not open to the public, not asking believers to explain or pose during rituals, and not treating personal curiosity as the festival's sole meaning.

Local tourism and cultural preservation can cooperate, but cannot substitute for each other. Airiti's research discussing the king ship festival's recreational impact from merchants' and residents' perspectives shows that the festival brings real economic effects, but whether an activity is welcomed cannot be judged by visitor numbers alone[^7]. More complete questions include: Can residents participate in decision-making? Do revenues return to the locality? Is shipbuilding and ritual knowledge explained correctly? As activities increase, do public facilities and environmental management keep pace?

## Preserving Donggang After the Flames

Listing the king ship as a cultural heritage asset cannot only preserve a proportionally accurate display ship. A display ship can help those who miss the festival understand the hull, but it cannot replace the palanquin bearers' steps, the ritual master's recitations, the shipbuilding master's touch, nor recreate the quiet dispersal of people before dawn after sending off. Cultural museums, field courses, local archives, and video records are all important, but should be viewed as tools supporting transmission, not transmission itself[^3][^5].

Future preservation work has at least three directions. First, let records of shipbuilding methods not stop at finished product photographs, but document material selection, tool use, master-apprentice teaching, and failed attempts. Second, let local memories of palanquin bearers and festival roles be expressed by the people themselves, cross-referenced with external research, rather than defined by a single institution for everyone. Third, let environment, safety, fire prevention, traffic, and crowd management be incorporated into festival planning, because only by safely completing the sending-off can the next cycle's continuation be discussed.

All three directions point to the same core: preservation is not sealing the festival in the past, but enabling it to face new problems. Declining population, aging masters, youth outmigration, increasing visitors, instant image transmission—these are contemporary conditions. If welcoming the king can only be preserved within an unchangeable imagination, it may actually lose true vitality. Conversely, if the locality can still negotiate between rules, faith, and public responsibility, tradition remains the local people's life, not a specimen preserved by outsiders for the locality.

## Understanding Taiwan Culture Through One Festival

Donggang's welcoming the king also lets us reconsider the often-grandiose phrase "Taiwan culture." It is not a list placing all traditions side by side, nor something automatically worth preserving simply because it has ancient origins. Culture becomes culture because people in concrete places use it, debate it, teach the next person how to do it, and when environment changes, decide which core elements cannot be abandoned and which forms can be adjusted. Donggang's king ship perfectly shrinks this process into one visible object.

It simultaneously connects sea and land, sacred and secular, family and outsiders, technique and memory. When the king ship burns at the seaside, what truly persists is not the wood, but the social relationships that make people willing to prepare together, follow rules together, and send off together. These relationships may not be without conflict, nor does each generation necessarily understand the king deities the same way, but precisely because negotiation is required, it is not a passively replayed past.

If Taiwan is to introduce its culture to the world, Donggang's welcoming the king offers not a consumable spectacle, but a more honest answer: culture never consists only of stage performance, but also includes who maintains order before dawn, who remembers a role's details, who is willing to pass on technique, and whether outsiders can learn to maintain respect before unfamiliar faith. After the king ship departs, this invisible work remains in Donggang, and therefore deserves to be carefully recorded in Taiwan's own knowledge base.
