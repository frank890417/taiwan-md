> **30 Second Overview:** Taiwan's Ghost Festival is not merely a religious ritual; it is a centuries-old "social treaty of peace." The hardships of early colonization and the bloody conflicts between ethnic groups compelled the ancestors to establish competitive mechanisms, such as the "rivalry replacing head-breaking" seen in the Keelung Ghost Festival. This festival treats those who died away from home—the "outsiders"—as "good brothers," embodying a unique spirit of immigrant compassion and community cohesion in Taiwan.

In 1855, the smell of gunpowder had not yet dissipated along the banks of the Xuchuan River in Keelung. After a major conflict involving over 100 casualties, both sides realized that if they continued to fight, this port town would be left with no people. Thus, they signed a special agreement: the remains of the deceased were interred together at the "Old Man's Temple," and each lineage took turns leading the offering, replacing fatal violence with magnificent ceremonies.

This is the origin of the "Cage Ghost Festival" (Jilong Zhongyuan Jie), and it holds the most counter-intuitive truth about Taiwan's Ghost Festival: **Although the festival honors "ghosts," it is fundamentally a social peace treaty signed to allow the "living" to continue coexisting.**

## From "Foreigners" to "Good Brothers": The Collective Compassion of Migrant Society

In mainland China, when people offered rites to solitary souls, they were often referred to as _bai ren ke_ (foreigners). In Taiwan, we refer to them as _bai hao xiongdi_ (good brothers). This subtle difference is rooted in the history of "nine deaths and one life" since the 17th century.

Early immigrants faced venomous snakes, plagues, and frequent societal upheaval. Many arrived in Taiwan alone, with no descendants to perform ancestral rites, becoming a source of social anxiety for the early settlers. However, these solitary souls who died away from home were often comrades who had sweated alongside them during colonization. Driven by this religious sentiment of "sympathy for shared suffering," high-density "Wai Ying Gong" (offering deities) or "Wan Shan Ci" (all-goodness temples) emerged across Taiwan; just in the Muzuo District of Taipei, there were 26 such shrines[^1].

📝 **Curator's Note:** The term "good brothers" is the most tender invention of Taiwanese immigrant society. It transforms fear into kinship, allowing those lonely souls to regain community belonging during an annual feast.

## Cage Ghost Festival: Rivalry Replacing Head-Breaking

As Taiwan's first designated "Important Folk Culture," the Keelung Ghost Festival fully preserves the entire ritual from the "Lighting Night" on the 6th lunar month to the "Closing Shrine Gate" on the 1st of the 8th month[^2].

The crucial turning point in this festival is the "lineage-based rotation." In early days, conflicts over commerce, land, and belief were constant between the漳 (Zhang) and 泉 (Quan) groups. After 1855, both sides negotiated to replace the concept of ancestral origin with lineage names (such as Zhang, Liao, Jian), taking turns hosting the offering. This competitive mechanism transformed potential violence into a contest for position in front of the "main altar" and performance displays.

> "Rivalry replacing head-breaking"—this centuries-old saying encapsulates the wisdom of the Keelung people in dissolving ethnic hatred through cultural art[^3].

## Vertical Struggle: Ghost Seizing (Qianggu) in Toudeng and Hengchun

If the Keelung festival represents horizontal ethnic reconciliation, then the "Ghost Seizing" (Qianggu) in Toudeng and Hengchun is a vertical record of survival.

Qianggu originated during the Qing dynasty when wealthy people would donate offerings to the poor after their own rites. To prevent injury during the scramble for these donations, it evolved into an athletic competition: climbing the "solitary platform" (gongpeng). In Toudeng, contestants must climb a pillar coated in thick lard, sometimes over ten meters high, vault over the solitary platform, and then ascend to seize the "Wind Flag" at the top[^4].

This is not merely a test of physical strength; it is an homage to the arduous life of the ancestors. The lard symbolizes the difficulty and slipperiness of survival, while the high-placed Wind Flag was a sacred object believed by early seafarers to ensure safe passage. In Hengchun, among 36 pillars, 4 are left empty for the "good brothers" to climb, illustrating a worldview where humans and ghosts share existence without infringing upon one another[^5].

## Modern Transformation: From Fear to Cultural Carnival

As social structures change, the form of the Ghost Festival is also undergoing transformation.

1. **From Taboo to Tourism:** The "Ghost Month," once filled with mystery and fear, is now one of the "12 Major Festivals in Taiwan" recognized by the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, attracting countless domestic and international tourists[^6].
2. **Environmentalism and Technology:** To adapt to climate change, many temples are promoting "rice for gold" offerings to reduce the burning of paper money. The Ghost Festival offering in Huwei, Yunlin, is famous for its spectacular "kneaded face-mask displays," transforming worship into a showcase of folk craftsmanship[^7].
3. **Extension of Social Welfare:** Offerings after the festival are generously donated to vulnerable groups, continuing the spirit of early "relief and aid" (zhengji)[^8].

## Conclusion: Finding Light in the Darkness

The Ghost Festival reminds us that peace on this island is not self-evident; it was achieved through countless conflicts and negotiations. When we burn incense by the roadside, offering rites to these "good brothers," we are worshipping not just ghosts, but the bloody history that guided different ethnic groups from hostility toward coexistence.

📝 **Curator's Note:** Taiwan's Ghost Festival is a feast of "memory." It reminds us of forgotten names and teaches us how to coexist with those who hold differing views, even while acknowledging the divine and the spectral.
