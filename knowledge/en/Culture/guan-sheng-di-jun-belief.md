# Guan Sheng Di Jun: How a Defeated General Became Taiwan's All-Purpose God

Every morning at six, a queue has already formed at Xingtian Temple on Taipei's Minsheng East Road. Blue-robed volunteers murmur incantations and waft incense around anxious office workers and young mothers clutching baby clothes to "calm fright." This temple that draws over 6 million visits a year has one feature that baffles foreign tourists—since 2014, it has removed all incense burners and offering tables, forbidding worshippers from burning even a single stick of incense.

Taiwan's busiest temple is also the first in Taiwan to ban incense. And its presiding deity is a Three Kingdoms general who was defeated and beheaded 1,800 years ago.

That man was Guan Yu.

## The War God Who Crossed the Sea to Taiwan

In 1665, Zheng Jing built a Guan Di Hall in Chengtian Prefecture (around today's Chihkan Tower in Tainan), one of the earliest recorded Guan Di temples in Taiwan—now the "Sacrificial Martial Temple" listed as a national historic site and a three-star Michelin Green Guide attraction. In the fifth year of Yongzheng (1727), the Qing court elevated it to official sacrificial status, with the court presiding over the spring and autumn rites; to this day it remains the only martial temple in Taiwan bearing the "Sacrificial" title.

But Guan Gong truly took root in Taiwan not through official power, but through immigrants' fear.

"Taiwan was originally called 'burying grievances.' Early settlers crossing the sea had to endure storms and miasma, and after landing they had to fight with mountain compatriots—danger everywhere." Folklore scholar Ruan Chang-jui notes that Guan Gong was ennobled by both the Ming and Qing as the "Great Emperor Who Subdues Demons," perfectly matching the settlers' need to exorcise evil and avert misfortune. Ships carried Guan Di statues at their prows; villages built Guan Di temples upon landing. The red-faced, glaring war god became the immigrants' first psychological defense line.

Not just subduing demons. Zhangzhou and Quanzhou immigrants fought armed clashes; Hakka and Hoklo people battled over territory. For self-protection, settlers emulated Liu, Guan, and Zhang's "Peach Garden Three Sworn Brothers," burning incense and swearing oaths before Guan Di temples, exchanging sworn-brother documents. Many old Taiwanese families still preserve brotherhood papers inscribed with "A Peach Garden Pact, a Tale for the Ages."

📝 Curator's Note: Mazu protects you crossing the sea; Guan Gong protects you landing ashore—Taiwan's early settlers bought double insurance.

## The Making of an All-Purpose God

Guan Gong's "portfolio" in Taiwan kept expanding, to a near-absurd degree.

In the Tang dynasty he entered the Martial Temple as the **Martial Sage**. But his image—one hand stroking his beautiful beard, the other holding the _Spring and Autumn Annals_—was so deeply ingrained that Confucianism honored him as the "Sage Emperor of the Literary Balance," making him a **god of education and literature** as well. Every exam season, Confucius temples see modest traffic while Guan Di temples are packed with students dragged there by parents. "The Shandong Master wrote the _Spring and Autumn_; the Shanxi Master reads the _Spring and Autumn_"—that's how Guan Gong stole Confucius's business.

Even more interesting is how he became the **god of commerce**. According to _Romance of the Three Kingdoms_, while captive under Cao Cao, Guan Yu meticulously recorded the gold, silver, cloth, and silk Cao bestowed under four categories—"original, received, expended, stored"—and returned the ledgers intact upon departure. Traditional merchants' bookkeeping methods and the "simple daily-clear ledger" are legendarily attributed to Guan Gong's invention. A general famed for "righteousness" thus became the patron deity of merchants who prize "profit."

Then comes the most contradictory scene: **police worship him, and triads worship him too.**

Police and fire units enshrine Guan Gong, praying for righteous protection and successful investigations. In Hong Kong films, triad initiation ceremonies must also worship Guan Gong, invoking "brotherhood." The same deity, two utterly different "justices"—if Guan Gong himself knew, he'd probably feel quite awkward.

From nearly 200 Guan Di temples island-wide in the 1950s to over 400 today, Guan Di ranks fourth in Taiwanese folk belief after Mazu, Wang Ye, and the Earth God. And he is the only "foreign deity" not originating from Hokkien ancestral worship to bloom across Taiwan—a Shanxi man born in the distant Three Kingdoms era.

📝 Curator's Note: Guan Gong is probably Taiwan's busiest god. He has to simultaneously bless police to catch criminals, and bless criminals to keep their brotherhood.

## En Zhu Gong: Taiwan's Unique Faith Species

If you ask on a Taiwanese street "Who is En Zhu Gong?", nine out of ten will say "It's just Guan Gong." But En Zhu Gong belief is actually a religious phenomenon unique to Taiwan, with no counterpart in mainland China or Southeast Asian Guan Di worship.

The term "En Zhu" (Grace Lord) comes from _luan tang_—a religious form that conveys divine oracles through _fu luan_ (planchette writing, similar to Western séances). Believers hold that certain deities descend to save the masses, bestowing grace upon the people, hence "En Zhu," a concept akin to Christianity's "Savior."

Taiwan's En Zhu faith centers on the "Five En Zhu": Guan Sheng Di Jun, Fu You Di Jun (Lü Dongbin), Si Ming Zhen Jun (Stove God Zhang Dan), Huo Luo Ling Guan (Wang Shan), and Jing Zhong Wu Mu Wang (Yue Fei). The five are headed by Guan Sheng Di Jun, so the folk directly call Guan Gong "En Zhu Gong."

More radical _luan_ sects even proclaim: under the joint nomination of the five patriarchs of Confucianism, Buddhism, Daoism, Christianity, and Islam, Guan Gong has succeeded the retired Jade Emperor to become the supreme ruler of the celestial bureaucracy. A Three Kingdoms general promoted to Universe CEO.

This belief evolution is unique in the global Chinese world. Southeast Asian Chinese worship the "Great Emperor Who Subdues Demons" or "Martial God of Wealth"; mainland Guan Di temples enshrine "Lord Guan"; only Taiwan has elevated Guan Gong to savior divinity and developed an entire system of _luan tang_ rituals, virtue books, and social service networks.

## Xingtian Temple: A Rebellious Mega-Temple Built by a Coal Tycoon

The iconic architecture of En Zhu Gong faith is Taipei's Xingtian Temple. And Xingtian Temple's story begins with a coal magnate.

Huang Zhang, born 1911 in Shulin District, New Taipei City, ancestral home Anxi, Quanzhou, Fujian. At 20 he and his brother entered the coal business, becoming one of Taiwan's five mining tycoons. In 1943, at 32, Huang Zhang took refuge under Guan Sheng Di Jun to pray for his ailing father, receiving the Daoist name "Wu Dao," later changed to "Xuan Kong."

In 1945, a malaria epidemic broke out around Baiji, Sanxia, where mountain medical resources were scarce. Huang Zhang dispatched mine railcars for emergency patient transport, and simultaneously established "Xingxiu Hall" at the Haishan Erkeng mine office, welcoming a Guan Sheng Di Jun statue to pray for the plague's end. Local legend says: when the Guan Emperor arrived, the plague actually receded. From then on, the Guan Emperor's name shook Sanxia.

For the next 25 years, Huang Zhang poured nearly his entire personal fortune into temple building. He solely funded three temples—Sanxia Xingxiu Palace, Beitou Branch Palace, and Taipei Main Palace—collectively known as the "Three Xingtian Palaces." He donated funds to pave the industrial road from Baiji to Sanxia, facilitated TRA's addition of Beitou Zhongyi Station, and donated land for Baiji Minyi Elementary School. In 1970, Huang Zhang donated all temple assets to the "Taipei Xingtian Temple Foundation," and passed away the same year at age 59.

Huang Zhang left not just buildings, but a set of "palace rules" exceedingly rare in Taiwanese temples. He advocated "examine your heart and revere the gods," establishing Xingtian Temple's "Eight Nos":

No burning joss paper, no planchette mediumship, no theatrical performances for gods, no thank-you gold plaques, no sacrificial animals, no merit boxes, no profit-seeking, no external fundraising.

"Faith needn't cost a fortune." Huang Zhang said. "If a person's heart is kind, they naturally emit the fragrance of morality—En Zhu Gong likes that best."

On August 26, 2014, Xingtian Temple went further, removing all large incense burners and offering tables, encouraging worshippers to press palms together, replacing stick incense with "moral heart-incense." The news shook Taiwan's religious circles. Critics said the gods had left; supporters said the air had cleared. But the data speaks: after the incense ban, Xingtian Temple's visitor numbers didn't drop—they rose. Younger worshippers and tourists became even more willing to enter because of the tranquil environment.

📝 Curator's Note: A coal tycoon spent his life savings building a temple that doesn't charge you a cent, then took away the very incense in your hand. This is probably the most elegant rebellion in Taiwan's temple history.

## The Guan Di Gene Permeating Daily Life

Guan Sheng Di Jun's permeation of Taiwan goes far beyond temples.

Tainan has an administrative district called "Guanmiao District"—the only district in Taiwan named after a Guan Di temple. Tainan's Wusheng Night Market takes its name from the Guan Di temple on Wusheng Road. When you eat fried chicken at the night market, you may not realize you're standing in the Guan Emperor's sphere of influence.

Every Lantern Festival, Yanshui's beehive fireworks in Tainan are Taiwan's most violent folk festival. Tens of thousands of fireworks aren't celebrating the Lantern Festival—they're welcoming Guan Sheng Di Jun's inspection tour. Legend says 190-odd years ago, Guan Gong, aided by thunderous firecrackers, suppressed a plague that had ravaged Yanshui for over 20 years; the festival has been passed down ever since and is now a nationally important folk custom.

And in the "calm fright" queue at Taipei's Xingtian Temple, you see the truest cross-section of Taiwanese society: young parents bringing kids for fright-calming, middle-aged merchants praying for business turnarounds, mothers hoping daughters ace exams, grandmothers requesting talismans for grandsons about to enlist.

Fright-calming is free, divination is free, poem interpretation is free. 6 million visits, zero revenue.

This is Guan Sheng Di Jun's ultimate form in Taiwan—a belief machine that runs without incense, a Shanxi man who walked from Three Kingdoms battlefields to Taipei streets, using 1,800 years to become the most reliable friend in Taiwanese hearts.

## Challenges and Controversies

Guan Di belief in Taiwan is not without controversy. _Luan_ sects' claim that "Guan Gong becomes the Heavenly Duke" (succeeding the Jade Emperor) has drawn fierce backlash from traditional Daoist circles, seen as usurping divine rank. Xingtian Temple's 2014 incense ban devastated the livelihoods of surrounding offering shops, and some worshippers question whether it violates the foundations of Taiwan's traditional sacrificial culture. Moreover, the triads' use of Guan Di belief as spiritual backing for "brotherhood" remains a long-standing social concern—how does a deity symbolizing justice view injustice committed in his name?

These tensions precisely prove that Guan Di belief in Taiwan is not a museum specimen, but a living, breathing, still-evolving faith.
