> **30-second overview:** The 2025 _Guidelines for the Adoption of Traditional Names by Taiwan's Indigenous Peoples_ summarizes the traditional naming systems of the 16 recognized groups into five structures. The portion following a personal name may be a father's name, mother's name, clan name, house name, or a form of address that changes with the birth of children. The rare Saisiyat Chinese surnames "Dou" (豆), "Feng" (風), "Ri" (日), and "Si" (絲) retain the history of clan names entering Chinese-style household registration. When reading these names, do not rush to split them into "surname + given name"; more important is recognizing which segment of kinship, house, and community relations the name points to.[^1]

Before his eldest son was born, Syaman Rapongan was called Si Nozayin. After his son Rapongan was born, he changed to Syaman Rapongan, meaning "father of Rapongan." Here, a name is not a fixed label but the way a person is renamed by family as they move through life stages.[^2]

Now look at the Saisiyat surname "Feng" (風). It looks like a rare Chinese surname, yet behind it may lie the `baba:i` clan, kinship cooperation, ancestral spirit ritual groups, and marriage taboos. If one sees it only as a "rare character," an entire history of clan systems overlapping with household registration is missed.[^3] [^4]

## Don't Look for the Surname First — Look at Whom the Name Points To

Taiwan's Indigenous peoples do not share a single "surname first, given name last" rule across all groups. The five naming systems compiled in the current guidelines are a map for understanding structure, not a template to apply to every individual. Practices within the same group may also differ by region, family, and contemporary choice.[^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Chinese-style names tend to prompt "What is your surname?" Indigenous traditional naming systems often answer a different question first: Whose child are you? From which house do you come? Which clan are you connected to? What stage of life are you in now?

The Council of Indigenous Peoples (CIP) summarizes the 16 groups' traditional naming systems into the following five. The same two- or three-part arrangement may carry completely different social meanings when placed in different groups.[^2]

| Naming Structure                        | Common Arrangement                        | Officially Listed Groups or Communities        | What the Final Segment Typically Records                                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Parent-child linked name                | Personal name + parental name             | Atayal, Seediq, Truku, Amis, Kavalan, Sakizaya | Father's or mother's name or other parental name, situating the individual in a direct lineage context.[^2]                        |
| Parent-child linked name with clan name | Personal name + parental name + clan name | Some Amis communities, Saisiyat                | Beyond parental name, includes clan identification and social position.[^2] [^5]                                                   |
| House name                              | Personal name + house name                | Paiwan, Rukai, Puyuma                          | House affiliation, inheritance, and social relations.[^1] [^2]                                                                     |
| Clan name                               | Personal name + clan name                 | Bunun, Tsou, Thao, Kanakanavu, Saaroa          | Family history, marriage taboos, and group identification.[^2]                                                                     |
| Parent-named-after-child                | Form of address + eldest child's name     | Tao (Yami)                                     | Parents or grandparents change their form of address upon the birth of the eldest child; the name responds to life stage.[^1] [^2] |

## Miru, Kaliting, Pacidal: Every Segment of a Name Says Something Different

The name of Atayal artist Miru Hayung (米路・哈勇): Miru is the personal name, Hayung is the father's name. Official materials also note that Miru relates to painting and reading, while Hayung can refer to pine tree. This is an example of a personal name linked to a parental name.[^2]

Amis singer and host Ado' Kaliting Pacidal (阿洛・卡力亭・巴奇辣) has three coordinates: Ado' is the personal name, Kaliting is the mother's name, Pacidal is the clan name. CIP materials explain that Pacidal carries the meaning of "sun" and connects to the clan's guardian spirit.[^2]

Paiwan Ljaucu Zingrur (Ljaucu・Zingrur): Zingrur is the house name. A house name can identify the household a person belongs to and may also involve house inheritance and social hierarchy. In cases of leaving the natal house or inter-ethnic marriage, the house names of Paiwan, Rukai, and Puyuma may also be adjusted.[^1] [^2]

Bunun writer Tulbus Tamapima (拓跋斯・塔瑪匹瑪): Tamapima is the clan name. CIP records note that daily interaction typically uses only the personal name. In situations requiring verification of marriage taboos — such as first meetings or marriage negotiations — the clan name is spoken in full. The name thus simultaneously preserves the boundaries of who may associate and who may not marry.[^2]

## "Dou" and "Feng" Are Not Odd Characters: Clan Clues Inside Saisiyat Chinese Surnames

Saisiyat society is based on patrilineal clans. The CIP notes that the 18 clans each have names, drawing naming material from flora and fauna, natural phenomena, and bodily/physical phenomena. Clans further form mutual-aid groups, ritual congregations, and inter-clan relations, observing specific marriage taboos.[^3]

During the Qing era, the Saisiyat clan system encountered Chinese surname concepts. Through phonetic translation or selection of characters based on original meaning, clan names gradually formed the Chinese surnames Dou (豆), Zhao (趙), Zhu (朱), Feng (風), Gao (高), Pan (潘), Qian (錢), Gen (根), Zhang (樟), Xia (夏), Xie (蟹), Ri (日), Si (絲), Xiong (芎), Hu (狐), Chan (蟬), Shi (獅), Xue (血), Mo (膜). Later some characters were rewritten as Xie (解), Hu (胡), Zhan (詹). These characters are clues left by clan names entering Chinese-style registration; they should not be used directly as tools to determine an individual's clan affiliation.[^3]

The CIP's Saisiyat traditional name page notes that 14 clans correspond to 18 major Chinese surnames; in modern traditional name restoration, many people append the clan name after the personal and parental names. The page gives Obay Ataw Hayawan as an example: Obay is the personal name, Ataw is the father's name, Hayawan is the clan name; the `ha` sound in Hayawan is close to "Xia" (夏), illustrating one origin of the Xia surname.[^5]

## Why Does One Clan Leave Behind Several Chinese Surnames?

A 2019 study based on 2013 household registration data organized part of the Saisiyat "clan name — Chinese surname" correspondences into quantifiable data. The study calls the initially corresponding character the "original surname" (本姓) and subsequent variant forms "derived surnames" (衍姓). This is the study's analytical tool, suitable for examining registration history, but cannot replace community, family, or household administration determination of individual cases.[^7]

| Clan Name Spelling Used in 2013 Study | Corresponding Chinese Surnames and Population                       | Total | What This Data Shows                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `baba:i`                              | Feng (風) 905, Feng (楓) 87, Guan (鄷) 35, Bai (栢) 1, Dong (東) 23 | 1,051 | Beyond Feng, multiple character forms extend; number of Chinese surnames cannot directly equal number of clans.[^7]                  |
| `tatawazay`                           | Dou (豆) 157, Zhao (趙) 789                                         |   946 | Dou and Zhao belong to the same clan correspondence organized in this study.[^7]                                                     |
| `sawan (kas’ames)`                    | Pan (潘) 408, Qian (錢) 243, Gen (根) 233                           |   884 | Multiple Chinese surnames can become historical registration traces of the same clan or related kin group.[^7]                       |
| `minakes`                             | Zhang (樟) 34, Zhang (章) 341                                       |   375 | Similar or different character forms may all originate from clan name transliteration and administrative registration processes.[^7] |
| `tanohila:`                           | Ri (日) 205, Zhang (張) 103                                         |   308 | Semantics, phonetic translation, and subsequent registration choices may all operate simultaneously.[^7]                             |
| `titiyon`                             | Zhu (朱) 1,008                                                      | 1,008 | Some clans show only one major Chinese surname in this study's data.[^7]                                                             |

> ⚠️ **Data Reading Reminder:** The year in the table above is **2013**; the data source is a 2019 study, not current population statistics. The table's value lies in showing the structure of "one clan corresponding to multiple Chinese surnames," not in using numbers to conclude anyone's identity.[^7]

"Dou" and "Feng" are worth writing about not because they are rare, but because they pull a surname that appears to be just one character back into the long duration of clans, marriage norms, ancestral spirit ritual groups, and multiple household registration system changes.

## From Qing Era to Japanese Surnames to Standalone Indigenous Names: How the Naming System Reached Today

Indigenous names were not redesigned on a blank form. Behind today's ID document options lie layers of traces left by different regimes, different household registration systems, and Indigenous movements.[^7] [^8]

| Time                   | Naming System Event                                                                                                                                                          | Impact on Names                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Qing era               | Saisiyat clan names combined with Chinese surname concepts through phonetic translation or semantic character selection.[^3]                                                 | Characters such as Dou, Feng, Ri, Si became visible clan clues.                                                                                                        |
| 1940                   | Research records that Saisiyat adopted 97 Japanese-style surnames during the Japanese-era name change movement; the policy ended less than 5 years before regime change.[^7] | Japanese surnames brought new differentiation, but research considers their confusion with old Chinese surnames and original clans limited.                            |
| December 1945          | "Measures for Taiwan Province Residents to Restore Original Names" required "referencing Chinese names to self-determine names" within 3 months.[^7]                         | Rapid, comprehensive Chinese-style name registration became the background for many families' name turning points.                                                     |
| 1995—2006              | Ministry of the Interior and _Enforcement Rules of the Name Act_ underwent 3 amendments.[^7]                                                                                 | Indigenous name registration gradually no longer mandated Chinese names and allowed Romanized Indigenous names in parallel.                                            |
| May 2024               | _Name Act_ amendment passed.[^8]                                                                                                                                             | Indigenous peoples may use Indigenous scripts as standalone names; name changes due to cultural custom and not personal factors are not counted in name change limits. |
| January and April 2025 | Related operational guidelines amended; _Guidelines for the Adoption of Traditional Names by Taiwan's Indigenous Peoples_ published.[^1] [^9]                                | Administrative processes and cultural custom reference frameworks more complete, emphasizing diverse structures of traditional names.                                  |

## Law Handles Registration; Culture Handles Relations

The current _Name Act_ stipulates that Indigenous name registration follows cultural custom, may use Indigenous scripts, and may be listed in parallel with Chinese names or Han Chinese names. After the 2024 amendment, Indigenous scripts may stand alone as names. If a name change follows cultural custom and is not due to personal factors, it is not counted in the general name change quota.[^8] [^10]

But regulations do not compress clan knowledge into a checkable menu. The Ministry of the Interior's "Operational Guidelines for Indigenous Peoples' Restoration of Traditional Names and Correction of Names" provides that applicants may apply to restore Chinese or Indigenous script traditional names, list Indigenous scripts in parallel, and correct surnames and parental names. For inconsistencies in kinship surnames caused by initial household registration at Retrocession, applicants may submit Japanese-era household investigation registers, initial household registration transcripts, etc. If no Japanese-era household investigation register exists, proof from two or more relevant elder relatives is acceptable.[^9]

The importance of these procedures lies in: a rare Chinese surname or Indigenous language word cannot alone serve as proof of naming eligibility. Whether a traditional name conforms to cultural custom must still return to the context of ethnicity, family materials, community knowledge, and household administration verification. When in doubt, household administration practice may request assistance from ethnic group experts or elders for confirmation.[^1] [^9]

## The Interpunct Is Presentation; Relations Are the Rule

The "・" is often seen as a hallmark of Indigenous names, but it is merely a presentation method. Household administration practice in principle respects the individual's choice to use an interpunct or space for segmentation. More critical is that the name's arrangement should conform to the cultural custom of the individual's ethnic group, not cater to outsiders' imagination of "what an Indigenous name should look like."[^9] [^10]

Restoring traditional names is not rewinding history. The Japanese era, postwar household registration, inter-ethnic marriage, migration, and long-term use of Chinese names have made each family's path back to traditional naming systems different. Researchers compiled the _Indigenous Peoples' Name Registry_ precisely so families who lost clues would have traceable materials. How to name in the end must still respect community knowledge and individual choice.[^7] [^11]

The person behind the name may be a father, mother, eldest child, clan, or house. When readers see that "Dou" and "Feng" are not merely rare Chinese character surnames but a compressed map of relations, the arrangement rules of Indigenous names truly begin to emerge.
