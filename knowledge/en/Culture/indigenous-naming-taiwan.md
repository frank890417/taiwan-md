> **30-Second Overview:** The _Guidelines for the Use of Traditional Names by Taiwan’s Indigenous Peoples_, released in 2025, categorize the traditional naming systems of the 16 recognized tribes into five structures. The part of a name following the personal name may be a patronymic, matronymic, clan name, house name, or a title that changes upon the birth of a child. Rare Han surnames among the Saisiyat, such as "Dou," "Feng," "Ri," and "Si," preserve the history of clan names after they entered the Han-style household registration system. When reading these names, do not rush to split them into "surname + personal name"; more importantly, identify which kinship, house, and tribal relationships they are pointing to. [^1]

Before his son Lanboan was born, he was called Si Nozayin. After his son Lanboan was born, he changed his name to Syaman Rapongan, meaning "Father of Lanboan." Names here are not fixed labels, but a way a person is called anew by family as they move through different life stages. [^2]

Look again at the "Feng" surname among the Saisiyat. It may appear to be a rare Han surname, but behind it lies the `baba:i` clan, kinship cooperation, the ancestral spirit festival groups, and marriage taboos. If one views it merely as a "rare character," one misses an entire history where clan systems intersected with household registration. [^3] [^4]

## Look First at Who the Name Points To, Not for the Surname

Taiwan’s Indigenous peoples do not have a single rule shared by all tribes that places "surname first, personal name second." The five naming systems organized in the current guidelines serve as a map for understanding structure, not as a template to be applied to every individual. Practices within the same tribe may also vary by region, family, and contemporary choices. [^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator’s Note:** Han-style names tend to ask first, "What is your surname?" Indigenous traditional naming systems often answer a different question: Whose child are you? From which house do you come? Which clan are you connected to? What stage of life are you currently in?

The Council of Indigenous Peoples summarizes the traditional naming systems of the 16 tribes into the following five types. The same two- or three-part sequence may carry entirely different social meanings when applied to different tribes. [^2]

| Naming System Structure                   | Common Arrangement                     | Tribes/Communities Listed Officially           | What the Latter Part Usually Records                                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Patronymic/Matronymic Linkage             | Personal Name + Kin’s Name             | Atayal, Seediq, Truku, Amis, Kavalan, Sakizaya | Patronymics or matronymics place the individual within a direct kinship context. [^2]                                 |
| Patronymic/Matronymic + Clan Name Linkage | Personal Name + Kin’s Name + Clan Name | Some Amis communities, Saisiyat                | Beyond kin names, it includes clan identification and social position. [^2] [^5]                                      |
| House Name System                         | Personal Name + House Name             | Paiwan, Rukai, Puyuma                          | House affiliation, inheritance, and social relations. [^1] [^2]                                                       |
| Clan Name System                          | Personal Name + Clan Name              | Bunu, Tsou,邵 (邵), Kanakanavu, Laaluwa        | Family history, marriage taboos, and group identification. [^2]                                                       |
| Child-Dependent Name Type                 | Title + Eldest Son’s Name              | Yami (Orchid Island)                           | Parents or grandparents change their titles upon the birth of the eldest son; names respond to life stages. [^1] [^2] |

## Miru, Kaliting, Pacidal: Each Segment of the Name Says Something Different

In the name of Atayal artist Miru Hayung, Miru is the personal name and Hayung is the patronymic. Official introductions note that Miru relates to painting and studying, while Hayung can refer to the pine tree. This is an example of a personal name linked to a kin name. [^2]

Amis singer and host Ado’ Kaliting Pacidal has three coordinates: Ado’ is the personal name, Kaliting is the mother’s name, and Pacidal is the clan name. Data from the Council of Indigenous Peoples explains that Pacidal means "sun" and is connected to the clan’s guardian deity. [^2]

In the Paiwan name Ljaucu・Zingrur, Zingrur is the house name. House names can identify the household to which a person belongs and may involve house inheritance and social hierarchy. In cases such as leaving the main house or inter-tribal marriage, house names among the Paiwan, Rukai, and Puyuma may also be adjusted. [^1] [^2]

In the Bunu writer Tulbus Tamapima’s name, Tamapima is the clan name. Records from the Council of Indigenous Peoples note that in daily interactions, people are often addressed by their personal names. The clan name is only fully spoken in contexts such as initial meetings or marriage negotiations where marriage taboos need to be confirmed. Thus, the name simultaneously preserves the boundaries between whom one can interact with and whom one cannot marry. [^2]

## "Dou" and "Feng" Are Not Rare Characters: Clan Clues in Saisiyat Han Surnames

Saisiyat society is based on patrilineal clans. The Council of Indigenous Peoples points out that there are 18 clans, each with its own name, and naming materials can be drawn from flora, fauna, natural phenomena, and physical bodily phenomena. These clans form mutual aid groups, festival groups, and inter-clan relationships, observing specific marriage taboos. [^3]

During the Qing dynasty, the Saisiyat clan system encountered Han surname concepts. Clan names, through phonetic translation or selection of characters based on original meaning, gradually formed Han surnames such as Dou, Zhao, Zhu, Feng, Gao, Pan, Qian, Gen, Zhang, Xia, Xie, Ri, Si, Xiong, Hu, Chan, Shi, Xue, and Mo. Later, some characters were rewritten as Jie, Hu, and Zhan. These characters are clues left by clan names entering Han-style registration and should not be directly used as tools to determine individual clan affiliation. [^3]

The Council of Indigenous Peoples’ page on Saisiyat traditional names notes that 14 clans can correspond to 18 major Han surnames; in modern efforts to recover traditional names, many people link clan names afterward. The page uses Obay・Ataw・Hayawan as an example: Obay is the personal name, Ataw is the father’s name, and Hayawan is the clan name; the `ha` sound in Hayawan is similar to "Xia" (Summer), explaining one source of the Xia surname. [^5]

## Why Does One Clan Leave Several Han Surnames?

A 2019 study based on 2013 household registration data organized the correspondence between some Saisiyat "clan names—Han surnames" into quantifiable data. The study refers to the initially corresponding characters as "primary surnames" and subsequent different character forms as "derived surnames." This is an analytical tool for the study, suitable for looking at registration history, but cannot replace the recognition of tribes, families, or household administration for specific cases. [^7]

| Clan Name Spelling Used in 2013 Study | Corresponding Han Surnames and Population  | Total | What This Data Set Explains                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----: | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `baba:i`                              | Feng 905, Feng 87, Shao 35, Bai 1, Dong 23 | 1,051 | Besides the Feng surname, there are multiple character extensions; the number of Han surnames cannot directly equal the number of clans. [^7] |
| `tatawazay`                           | Dou 157, Zhao 789                          |   946 | Dou and Zhao belong to one clan correspondence organized in this study. [^7]                                                                  |
| `sawan (kas’ames)`                    | Pan 408, Qian 243, Gen 233                 |   884 | Multiple Han surnames can become historical registration traces of the same clan or related kin group. [^7]                                   |
| `minakes`                             | Zhang 34, Zhang 341                        |   375 | Similar or different character forms may both originate from the process of clan name transcription and administrative registration. [^7]     |
| `tanohila:`                           | Ri 205, Zhang 103                          |   308 | Semantic meaning, phonetic translation, and subsequent registration choices may act simultaneously. [^7]                                      |
| `titiyon`                             | Zhu 1,008                                  | 1,008 | Some clans in this study’s data only present one major Han surname. [^7]                                                                      |

> ⚠️ **Data Reading Reminder:** The year in the table above is **2013**, and the data source is a 2019 study, not current population statistics. The value of the table lies in presenting the structure that "one clan can correspond to multiple Han surnames," not in using numbers to draw conclusions about anyone’s identity. [^7]

"Dou" and "Feng" are worth writing about not because they are rare, but because they pull a seemingly single-character surname back into the long history of clans, marriage norms, ancestral spirit festival groups, and multiple changes in household registration systems.

## From Qing Dynasty to "He-xing" to Single-List Indigenous Names: How Did the Naming System Reach Today?

Indigenous names were not redesigned on a blank form. The options on today’s ID cards carry layered traces left by different regimes, different household registration systems, and the Indigenous movement. [^7] [^8]

| Time                   | Naming System Event                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Impact on Names                                                                                                                                                               |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Qing Dynasty           | Saisiyat clan names combined with Han surname concepts through phonetic translation or meaning-based character selection. [^3]                                                                                                                                | Characters like Dou, Feng, Ri, and Si became visible clan clues.                                                                                                              |
| 1940                   | Research records that the Saisiyat adopted 97 "Japanese-style surnames" (Wa-xing) after the name change movement in the Japanese colonial era; the comprehensive adoption of Japanese-style surnames ended less than 5 years later due to regime change. [^7] | Japanese-style surnames brought new differentiation, but research suggests their confusion with old Han surnames and original clans was relatively limited.                   |
| December 1945          | The "Measures for Taiwan Provincial People to Recover Original Names" required people to "determine names referring to Chinese names" within 3 months. [^7]                                                                                                   | Rapid, comprehensive Han-style name registration became the background for many family name turning points.                                                                   |
| 1995–2006              | The Ministry of the Interior and the "Detailed Rules for the Implementation of the Name Act" underwent 3 amendments. [^7]                                                                                                                                     | Indigenous name registration gradually no longer required Han names and allowed parallel Roman script Indigenous names.                                                       |
| May 2024               | The "Name Act" was amended and passed. ## Law Handles Registration; Culture Handles Relationships

The current _Name Act_ explicitly states that Indigenous name registration follows cultural customs, may use Indigenous script, and may be listed parallel to Chinese names or Han names. After the 2024 amendment, Indigenous script can be listed singly as a name. If a name is changed according to cultural customs and not due to personal factors, it is not counted in the general number of name changes. [^8] [^10]

However, regulations do not reduce clan knowledge to a checkable menu. The "Operational Guidelines for Taiwan Indigenous Peoples to Recover Traditional Names and Correct Names" from household administration stipulate that one may apply to recover Chinese or Indigenous script traditional names, list Indigenous script in parallel, and correct surnames and parents’ names. For situations such as kinship surname inconsistencies caused by initial household registration after the Liberation, applicants may provide data such as Japanese colonial era household census books or initial household registration copies. If no Japanese colonial era household census book exists, two or more related elder kin may provide proof. [^9]

The importance of these procedures lies in this: a rare Han surname or Indigenous word cannot alone serve as proof of naming qualification. Whether a traditional name conforms to cultural customs must still return to the context of ethnicity, family data, tribal knowledge, and household administration verification. When doubts arise, household administration practice may invite experts or elders of that tribe to assist in confirmation. [^1] [^9]

## The Dot Is Presentation; Relationships Are the Rule

The "・" is often seen as a hallmark of Indigenous names, but it is merely a presentation method. Household administration practice generally respects the individual’s choice to use dots or spaces for segmentation. More importantly, the arrangement of names should conform to the cultural customs of the tribe to which the individual belongs, rather than catering to observers’ imaginations of "what an Indigenous name should look like." [^9] [^10]

Recovering traditional names is not rewinding history. Japanese colonial era and post-war household registration, inter-tribal marriage, migration, and long-term use of Han names have all made each family’s path back to traditional naming systems different. Researchers compiled the _Indigenous Peoples’ Name Dictionary_ precisely to provide traceable data for families that have lost clues. Ultimately, how to name must still respect tribal knowledge and individual choice. [^7] [^11]

The person behind the name may be the father, mother, eldest son, clan, or house. When readers see that "Dou" and "Feng" are not just rare Han characters, but a compressed map of relationships, the rules for arranging Indigenous names truly begin to develop.
