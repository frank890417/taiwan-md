# Taiwan's 16 Indigenous Peoples: A Cultural Map

On October 27, 1930, in Wushe, Nantou, Sediq Chief Mona Rudao led his people to resist Japanese colonial rule. That action, known as the "Wushe Incident," left the most tragic historical memory in Taiwan's indigenous history. Seventy-eight years later, on April 23, 2008, Sediq descendants held a renaming oath ceremony in Puli, reading their pledge in Dgang, Toda, Truku, English, Japanese, and Mandarin in succession. They had waited half a century to reclaim their own tribal name. Before that, official documents classified them as "a branch of the Atayal."

"Waiting to be recognized" is the shared modern history of Taiwan's indigenous peoples. The number 16 tribes appears fixed, yet it has never stopped shifting.

## The Spectrum of 620,000: From 210,000 to 441

Taiwan's currently registered indigenous population exceeds 620,000 (2025 census), comprising approximately 2.6% of Taiwan's total population. Yet this round number masks a startling disparity among the 16 tribes. The Amis lead with approximately 210,000 people, dominating the coastal plains and longitudinal valleys from Hualien to Taitung—the largest indigenous population in Taiwan. The Paiwan number around 100,000, the Atayal around 90,000, and these three peoples together account for nearly 60% of the total indigenous population.

At the other end of the spectrum lies a different set of numbers:

> **📊 Smallest tribal populations in 2024**
> Thao: 890 people (Sun Moon Lake, Nantou)
> Tao: 4,938 people (Lanyu)
> Saaroa: 477 people (Taoyuan District, Kaohsiung)
> **Kanakanavu: 441 people** (Namasia District, Kaohsiung)

441 people. That is approximately the number of households in a large apartment building.

But the Kanakanavu are not a "newborn people." Dutch colonial records from the 1600s already documented their existence as "Kanakannavo." Their history on this island predates any Han Chinese migration by thousands of years. Small population does not mean thin culture; a small tribe does not mean a short story.

📝 The Thao people best illustrate all of this. Only 890 tribal members remain around Sun Moon Lake, yet they maintain a unique ancestor-veneration belief system through the gluteal-striking stone and mortar-pounding rhythms (women creating polyrhythmic patterns by pounding mortars with wooden pestles). In 2001, the Thao were formally recognized as Taiwan's indigenous peoples. Before that, they had been classified as Plains Austronesians, cooked savages, or a branch of the Tsou—each name given by others.

## "16" is the Result of Political Negotiation

"Why 16 tribes instead of 17 or 26?" The answer to this question lies not in biology or linguistics, but in government gazettes.

Taiwan's indigenous tribal recognition has always been a political process. During Japanese rule, anthropologists classified peoples by language and custom. After the war, the government redefined boundaries, with different categorization methods in different eras. The naming-rights movement accumulated momentum from the 1980s onward, with tribes completing the application, review, and announcement procedures one after another:

- Truku (2004 official recognition, 12th tribe): Originally classified as the "Truku group" of Sediq
- Sediq (2008 official recognition, 14th tribe): Originally classified as Atayal
- Kanakanavu (2014 official recognition, 15th tribe): Originally classified as Tsou
- Saaroa (2014 official recognition, 16th tribe): Originally classified as Tsou

Each naming struggle meant that a people spent decades trying to convince others: "We are not who you say we are."

📝 Yet "16" has not ended. Descendants of the Siraya in Kaohsiung and the Hoanya people of Yunlin, estimated at tens of thousands, are conducting tribal identity reconstruction movements and have not yet achieved official recognition. The Siraya obtained local-level recognition from Tainan City Government in 2005, but federal-level recognition remains unresolved. "16" is not a final point.

## Lanyu's Flying Fish Calendar

To find the best example of indigenous peoples' deep dialogue with nature, the Tao are the answer. The Tao are Taiwan's only major indigenous people inhabiting an offshore island, distributed across Lanyu, 82 kilometers southeast of Taiwan's main island. Six villages line the island's shore: Langdao, Dongqing, Yeyin, Hongtou, Yuren, and Yeyou, with a population of approximately 4,938 people (2024).

Their entire social structure revolves around one thing: flying fish.

From March to June each year is the Tao flying fish season. During this time, fishermen move into communal fish houses, catching only flying fish and no other species, with all catches shared with the village. The Tao's maritime beliefs hold that flying fish are food bestowed by the sky god; if all the season's catch is distributed and consumed before the flying fish season ends, the following year will bring endless abundance. This ritual encompasses taboos, prayers, shared meals, and gender divisions of labor—Lanyu's social relationships operate within the flying fish season's rhythm, a time-system that has sustained island survival for over a thousand years.

The Tao language belongs to the Austronesian language family's Batanic group, with deep kinship to the people of Batanes Island in the Philippines. They represent a crucial node in Austronesian peoples' Pacific expansion history. Between globalization's pressures and nuclear waste controversies (beginning in 1982, Taiwan's government selected Lanyu as a nuclear waste repository, triggering decades of Tao protest), the flying fish season's rhythm still supports nearly 5,000 people on this small island.

## Mountains and Coasts: Geography as Identity

The distribution of 16 tribes is nearly a complete mirror of Taiwan's topography. From the Central Mountain Range's high ridges to the Pacific coast, different geographic environments have shaped distinct survival wisdom and social systems.

The Atayal of northern mountains have guarded high-altitude watersheds in Hsinchu and Taoyuan for thousands of years. The Saisiyat, wedged between the Atayal and Han migrants, preserve the Pasi-taai ceremony (矮靈祭) on the Hsinchu-Miaoli border—held every two years to mourn and reconcile the historical harm inflicted on the "small people," making it one of Taiwan's most reflective ceremonies.

The Bunun's traditional lands extend from Nantou to Hualien and Taitung. Their eight-part harmony (Pasibutbut), a polyphonic chorus mimicking bee flight hums, was recorded by Japanese ethnomusicologist in 1943 and brought to Europe, allowing Western academia to first recognize that Taiwan's indigenous peoples possessed an independently developed polyphonic musical tradition. That recording was the starting point for Austronesian music entering global academic discourse.

The Paiwan and Rukai territories span Pingtung and Taitung mountains. The Paiwan's chieftain hereditary system, stratified society, and ceramics and hundred-pace-viper designs form a rare aristocratic social structure among Austronesian peoples. The Five-Year Festival (Maljeveq), held every five years, features young warriors climbing poles and piercing balls—Laiyi Township's Five-Year Festival in Pingtung has been designated a National Important Folk Custom.

The Amis inhabit the longitudinal valley and coastal plains, renowned for their age-grade system. Young people from age 13 enter the "youth meeting house" to receive training, learning tribal responsibilities through group divisions. The Harvest Festival (Ilisin), held from July to September each year, is Taiwan's largest indigenous ceremony. The Tafalong Harvest Festival in Guangfu Township, Hualien, has an uninterrupted celebration record spanning centuries.

## From Villages to Venice

Paiwan artist Sakuliu Pavavaljung (born 1960), when interviewed for his biography, said: "Chiang Bin gave me the most important influence—to faithfully record." In 1977, a National Taiwan University anthropology graduate student walked into his Sandimen village in Pingtung, making him realize the importance of systematically documenting his own culture. He subsequently initiated the "The Village has Classrooms; Elites Return" movement (1993), encouraging educated tribal members to return home and organize traditional knowledge. In 2018, Sakuliu received Taiwan's 20th National Award for Arts.

Atayal weaver Yuma Taru established the "Wild Tung Workshop," rediscovering plant dyes and traditional pattern knowledge. She continuously emphasizes that Atayal traditional weaving is not merely handicraft; each diamond geometric pattern carries identity, hierarchy, and village memory—a history worn on the body. The young weavers trained by the workshop now spread throughout tribal areas of Taoyuan, Hsinchu, and Miaoli.

Amis artist Rahic Talif represented Taiwan at the 2019 Venice Biennale, becoming the first indigenous artist to represent Taiwan. Rukai curator Eleng Luluan has curated the "Art Festival on a Slope," transforming Pingtung's mountains into an important platform for indigenous contemporary art. This generation of artists has made fissures a creative material, bringing colonial history, naming-rights movements, and land disputes onto canvas and stage—no longer merely guardians of tradition, but active interpreters of contemporary culture.

## Language is Alive; Only the Living Matter

Linguists have reached an indigestible conclusion: from Madagascar to Hawaii, from New Zealand to the Philippines, over 300 million people speak Austronesian languages, and one dispersal origin point traces back to Taiwan. Taiwan's indigenous languages preserve the Austronesian language family's oldest phonological systems. Linguist Robert Blust's research estimates that the Austronesian language family dispersed outward from Taiwan approximately 5,000 years ago, making Taiwan's indigenous peoples' ancestors the "ancestral mothers of the Austronesian peoples."

Between this fact and current reality lies a cruel gap. Currently, 95.5% of Taiwan's indigenous peoples use Mandarin as their primary daily language (2010 census). According to UNESCO assessments, most of the 16 tribal languages are severely endangered or critically endangered. Indigenous population registered in urban areas has reached 46%, and children growing up in cities find tribal languages have become a foreign language requiring special "learning."

The Indigenous Languages Development Act passed in 2017 designated 16 tribal languages as national languages, implementing language certification systems, with over 30,000 people passing certification. Tawei Primary School's Paiwan immersion teaching in Pingtung County (taught entirely in Paiwan from first grade) and Boyu Primary School's Atayal experimental education in Hualien County represent tribal efforts to catch language within the school system. Policy can preserve language's outer shell. But what truly keeps language alive are those moments when it is used to tell jokes, argue, sing, and pass along secrets—and those moments require people, communities, and land.

## The Map is Not an Ending

The Kanakanavu completed the naming process, becoming the 15th tribe with their population of 441. Siraya descendants, estimated at tens of thousands, still await federal-level recognition. This is not about which tribe is particularly weak, but because the door of political recognition has its own rhythm.

We are accustomed to writing culture as a stable list: 16 tribes, 16 languages, 16 ceremonial systems. But the real situation is that every number is still breathing. Someone is teaching their grandchild tribal language in a city apartment; someone is building flying fish racks in the village waiting for the season; someone is organizing documents for a naming application. This cultural map is a snapshot from 2026, not a final edition.
