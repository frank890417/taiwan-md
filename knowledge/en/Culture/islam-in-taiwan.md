> **30-second overview:** Islam in Taiwan is not a foreign import but an ancient memory buried deep in the island's lifeblood. From the descendants of Quanzhou "Hui ding" who arrived with Koxinga in the late Ming and early Qing, to the 260,000 Indonesian Muslim migrant workers who today sustain Taiwan's long-term care and fisheries, this faith has undergone a fantastical journey from assimilation and disappearance to diverse flourishing.

In 1994, a scholar from Quanzhou, Fujian, Zhuang Jing-hui, set foot on the land of Taixi, Yunlin. To his astonishment, he discovered that this remote fishing village, where "Ding" is the dominant surname, shared a blood connection severed for two hundred years with the Muslim Ding clan of Chendai, Quanzhou.[^1] This was no isolated case. In Lukang's Beitou Guo compound in Changhua, residents worship Guo Ziyi, yet observe the peculiar taboo of offering no pork during ancestral rites.[^2] These "Hui" descendants sealed by history, together with the Indonesian migrant workers praying in Taipei Main Station's hall today, piece together the truest portrait of Islam in Taiwan.

### The Vanished "Hui ding": The Islamic Code Hidden in Ancestral Worship

The first wave of Islam in Taiwan arrived as early as the 17th century. Among the soldiers and civilians who came with Koxinga were large numbers of Hui descendants from Quanzhou, most famously the "Baiqi Guo clan" and the "Chendai Ding clan."[^3]

These families' ancestors were largely Arab or Persian merchants who came to Quanzhou during the Yuan dynasty. Forced by the "Yisibaxi" turmoil at the Yuan's end and the Ming's early exclusionary policies to conceal their names and settle in the countryside,[^4] they saw their faith gradually "dry up" after reaching Taiwan, under the overwhelming encirclement of Hoklo Han culture.

📝 Curator's note: Faith may vanish, but taboos become genetic memories, left on dining tables and altars.

Today, the Lukang Guo clan and the Taixi Ding clan largely no longer profess Islam; they burn incense and worship ancestors like Han Chinese. Yet history's traces remain stubborn:

- **Lukang Guo compound**: Some clan members offer no pork during ancestral rites; some families even have a precept of "not eating pork," though they no longer understand the religious significance behind it.[^5]
- **Taixi Ding clan**: Known as the "Taixi Aladdin," their ancestor Ding Su migrated from Chendai, Quanzhou during the Qianlong era; the clan genealogy explicitly records their Western Regions lineage.[^6]

"PE class, 15 people total — I'm in the back group, Chi Lin is in the front group."[^7] This search for and identification of identity exists in another form among Taiwan's Hui descendants as well — in their root-seeking journey, they rediscover their connection to the distant Western Regions.

### The Second and Third Waves: From Generals to Caregivers

In 1949, the Nationalist government's retreat to Taiwan brought the second wave of Muslim immigrants. This group of roughly 20,000 "Chinese-speaking Muslims" were mostly military, civil service, and education personnel; the most famous among them was Minister of National Defense Bai Chongxi.[^3] They established Taiwan's first mosque on Lishui Street in Taipei, transforming Islam from "hidden family precepts" into an "institutionalized religion."

Yet what truly made Islam "visible" on Taiwan's streets was the third wave of immigrants surging in after the 1990s: Southeast Asian migrant workers.

### Taipei Main Station's Black-and-White Checks: A Spiritual Oasis for Strangers

Every Eid al-Fitr, the black-and-white checkered floor of Taipei Main Station's concourse fills with Indonesian Muslims in vibrant attire. This once sparked controversy over "occupying the station," but for workers like Desin, this is their only "home" in Taiwan.[^10]

"To be migrant-worker-friendly, Taipei Main Station added a Muslim prayer room and signage on the first basement level, so Muslims no longer have to hide in corners to pray."[^10] This contestation and empowerment of space reflects the shift in Taiwanese society's attitude toward Islam.

📝 Curator's note: A city's progress lies not in how many skyscrapers it has, but in whether it can make room for a single prayer rug.

Yet challenges persist. In 2010, a scandal erupted when an employer forced an Indonesian Muslim to eat pork, drawing international attention.[^11] Even in 2025, mosques across Taiwan remain few; many live-in caregivers, due to the nature of their work, struggle to attend Friday (Jumu'ah) prayers at a mosque.[^12]

### Conclusion: The Crescent on the Island

From Quanzhou to Taixi, from Jakarta to Taipei, the history of Islam in Taiwan is an epic of "adaptation" and "persistence." Some Taiwanese ancestors once hid their faith to survive; today's migrant workers strive to let their faith shine in a foreign land.

When we see a caregiver wearing a _hijab_ pushing an elderly person on a stroll, that is not merely an input of labor — it is the latest piece in this island's 400-year Islamic mosaic. Taiwan's Muslims are no longer just "Hui descendants" or "foreign workers"; they are the gentlest and most resilient crescent in this land's multicultural tapestry.
