> **30-Second Overview:** Lunar July, the "Ghost Month" in Taiwanese vernacular, is not merely a fear of spirits; it is a centuries-old "practice of coexistence." From the 'Tiger Gate' opened late at night at Xinzhu City God Temple to the "Ghost Scramble" (搶孤) competition in Toucheng before the gates close—Taiwan transforms this belief into deeply localized culture. This article will take you through the firelight of Wanghai Lane, the sweat on the lonely scaffold, and the dietary and dress taboos etched into our DNA. We will uncover what might be a "political marketing" campaign originating from the Ming Dynasty royalty, and understand how this island achieves social reconciliation across time through the gentle wisdom of "hospitality with intention, yet without expectation."

In 1853 (the third year of Xianfeng), the air in Keelung was thick not with incense smoke, but with the metallic scent of bloodshed. The Fujian Zhangzhou and Quanzhou migrants who settled here engaged in brutal "Zhang-Quanzhou械鬥" (inter-clan conflict) over commercial interests and land boundaries, resulting in heavy casualties near Yangong-Chai (now Shihquling). Just as the conflict threatened to spiral into endless revenge, elders from both sides intervened, deciding to inter the remains of the deceased in a "Grand Ancestral Tomb," with various clans taking turns hosting the rites. This is the genesis of one of Taiwan's most vital cultural assets—the "Jilong Mid-Autumn Rite" [^1].

### 📝 Curator's Note:

> The Ghost Month in Taiwan is essentially a "large-scale social reconciliation experiment." We do not merely worship our ancestors; we honor the strangers who fell during the process of settlement.

### I. Local Rituals: Moments of Opening and Closing

#### 1. Xinzhu City God Temple: The 'Tiger Gate' Opened in the Dead of Night

On the eve of the first day of the seventh lunar month (the night of the 29th of the sixth month), the Xinzhu City God Temple holds the solemn "Opening of the Tiger Gate" ceremony. This gate, usually tightly shut, is considered a spiritual passage between the realms of Yin and Yang. Only upon the command of the City God deity, opened by a divine attendant holding a tiger plaque, does it signify that good brothers are formally allowed to enter the mortal realm to partake in offerings. The subsequent "Yoke Breaking" (夯枷) ritual involves thousands of devotees wearing paper shackles while parading with the divine palanquin, symbolizing the cleansing of sins from the past year [^2].

#### 2. Chiayi Minxiong Fire Cremation Shrine Rite: Compassion and Ascension by Fire

In Minxiong, Chiayi, during the latter part of Lunar July, the renowned "Great Sage Rite" (大士爺祭) is held across Taiwan. The statue of the Great Sage (an incarnation of Guanyin, depicted with a fearsome visage to subdue wandering souls) is not usually displayed but is constructed from red cloth only for the festival. At the end of the rites, this massive statue is carried to the cremation grounds to "ascend." The resulting blaze symbolizes carrying away earthly calamities and good brothers back into tranquility [^3].

#### 3. The Firelight of Wanghai Lane and the Sweat on the Lonely Scaffold

The "Water Lantern Release" (放水燈) in Keelung's Wanghai Lane is meant to guide lost souls from the sea ashore; while the "Ghost Scramble" (搶孤) in Toucheng and Hengchun is intended to comfort the spirits who died during the settlement period. In Toucheng, participants must "flip the scaffold," a moment that merges physical extremity with religious devotion [^4].

### II. The Deep Logic of Taboos: More Than Superstition, It Is Deference

The taboos of Lunar July are manifold, from clothing to diet; each one harbors an awe for the "intangible guests" and a deference to space.

#### 1. Clothing and Household Taboos: Avoiding Negative Connections

| Category       | Prohibited Act                               | Underlying Logic and Folk Beliefs                                                                                                        |
| :------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Clothing**   | Not wearing all black, all white, or all red | All black and white are easily associated with funerals; all red is believed to attract powerful spirits or anger the good brothers [^5] |
| **Household**  | No wind chimes, no walking against a wall    | The frequency of the chime is thought to be similar to a soul-summoning bell; walls are seen as a cool passage for good brothers [^6]    |
| **Daily Life** | Not drying clothes at midnight               | The silhouette of wet clothing in the night is believed to invite spirits to "borrow wear" [^7]                                          |
| **Sleeping**   | Shoes should not point toward the bed        | Legend holds that the direction of the shoe points the good brothers to "sleep with humans" [^8]                                         |

#### 2. Dietary and Social Taboos: Do Not 'Mislead' the Good Brothers

- **Not eating duck meat**: In some areas, duck is considered an offering for the rites; consuming it normally might be mistaken by good brothers as them being "stolen."
- **Do not kick spirit paper**: Spirit paper is a form of "alms" for the good brothers; kicking or trampling it is seen as extreme disrespect.
- **No whistling, no patting shoulders**: The sound of a whistle is thought to attract attention; patting the shoulder can extinguish the "three fires" (head and both shoulders) on a person, lowering their vital energy [^9].
- **Do not randomly pick up roadside red envelopes**: This is not just a risk associated with ghost marriages; in July, it is more likely an item left for "luck transference" or "passing fortune" [^10].

---

### III. The Counterintuitive Core: A Centuries-Long "Political Propaganda"?

**Not all taboos stem from ancient fear; some may originate from calculated political manipulation.**

Historical research suggests that the Ming Dynasty Emperor Taizu, Zhu Yuanzhang, deliberately spread rumors that July was a month of great misfortune for ghosts in order to reserve this period for imperial funerary rites. He even dispatched Taoist priests to disseminate omens among the populace.

This "political marketing" arrived in Taiwan during the Ming-Qing migration waves, merging with the existential anxiety caused by high mortality rates during the settlement period. For migrants of that era, facing unknown miasma, clan warfare, and maritime disasters, "fear" became a tool for social cohesion. This political propaganda eventually solidified into the folk system we hold as unquestionable today [^11].

### IV. The Art of the Rite: Hospitality with Intention, Without Expectation

The most captivating element of Taiwan's Zhongyuan Festival is the bowl of "empty vegetable soup." This soup is usually unseasoned, merely hot water boiled through vegetables.

"Hospitality with intention, without expectation." This phrase encapsulates the social wisdom of the Taiwanese people. The empty vegetable symbolizes "no pretense," meaning that although I have sincerely prepared a feast for you (the good brothers), I hold no unresolved grievances with you—eat well and please depart swiftly.

This is not just a dialogue with spirits; it is the most dignified posture an immigrant island can adopt when facing unknown forces. We do not strive for "exorcism"; we pursue "coexistence" and "deference."

### 📝 Curator's Note:

> Lunar July in Taiwan is, in fact, a thirty-day "island psychotherapy session." Through taboos, we regulate fear; through rites, we release grief. Finally, amidst the firelight and incense smoke, we achieve reconciliation with past bloodshed.

### V. Conclusion: Seeing Gentleness Within Taboo

When we see small offering tables set up outside convenience stores during the midsummer of 2026, or witness online Zhongyuan rites in the digital age, perhaps we can attempt to step out of the framework of "superstition." Lunar July reminds us that this island's history is woven from countless named and unnamed lives. When we light a stack of spirit money on the roadside, it is not merely an awe before supernatural forces; it is a collective homage to the impermanence of life.
