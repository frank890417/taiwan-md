# Siù-siù-á: A Sweet Lunar New Year Tray, and Blessings That Don't All Have to Taste Good

> **30-second overview:** Sheng-ren tang uses "sheng-ren / sheng-ren" (peanut / stranger, homophone for "new family member") to read peanuts as a clan's wish; cun-zao borrows a single "zao" (jujube) character to leave the imagination of "early birth of a noble son"; ma-zao puts "eat until old-old-old" into maltose and sesame[^3]. The Ministry of Education dictionary defines "siù-siù-á" as snacks, nibbles[^2]. But the sugar on the New Year table speaks not only of auspiciousness, but also of taste. Ma-zao and cun-zao run about 500–600 kcal per 100 g; food spot checks put peanut brittle into aflatoxin testing[^6] [^7]. What this article wants to see is how a single tray of candy simultaneously becomes blessing, habit, and daily life that needs to be chosen anew.

## Sheng-ren Tang Puts "People" Into Words First

Sheng-ren tang often sits between New Year candy and offerings: it is both a sweet to share with guests and something that may be placed on the offering table. This position is not like a supermarket shelf classification; it is more like someone opening the candy box on the living room table, pointing at each compartment, and asking: "Have you eaten this one before?"[^1]

The common form of sheng-ren tang is peanut kernels wrapped in layer upon layer of red-and-white sugar frosting. Jiu Zhen Nan's cultural article calls it "sheng-ren tang" and links "sheng-ren" (peanut) with "sheng-ren" (new family member / stranger) via homophone, connecting it to flourishing descendants. The article also records it has another name, "Tian-gong dou" (Jade Emperor's bean), linking it to the folk practice of worshipping the Jade Emperor on the ninth day of the first lunar month[^3]. These sayings belong to folklore and brand culture compilation; not every household uses the same set of explanations. But they illustrate one thing: New Year candy was preserved never just because of its sweetness.

![Taiwanese sweets](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/73/Taiwanese_sweets_%281088069273%29.jpg)

_Taiwanese sweets. Photo: pelican, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwanese_sweets_(1088069273).\_[^8]

This photo places sheng-ren, candy, and other sweets in the same gaze. New Year nibbles rarely serve only one meaning; they accompany each other in the same box, letting the offering table's blessing, the living room's hospitality, and the child's picky hand appear simultaneously[^8].

The literal "sheng-ren" can be re-read once more by blessing. Peanut is food; new family member is wish. A sugar-coated peanut thus stands between dining table and offering table at once. Some people even forget the taste of certain candies — this uncertainty instead makes the New Year credible: tradition is not something that must be memorized by rote every year, but something someone is still willing to ask the name of again.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Circulating New Year lists love to translate every nibble into a line of auspicious talk, but this translation hides the choices of disliking, picking, and buying just for hospitality. What the candy box leaves behind is not proof that "everyone loves it," but how the family negotiates between blessing and taste.

## Cun-zao Has No Jujube; Ma-zao Lets You Eat Until "Old"

Cun-zao is often placed into this New Year vocabulary set. The Jiu Zhen Nan article calls it "zao-zhi" (jujube branch) or "cun-zao tang" (inch-jujube candy), and records a saying: in early days red jujubes were rare, so glutinous rice was pinched into jujube shapes and coated with white sugar to replace real jujubes. Hence the name has "zao" but the ingredients may not have jujube[^3]. This story is best understood as "legend" — it has explanatory power but should not be written as the sole definitive origin.

The interesting part of cun-zao is that the name completes the blessing before the ingredients do. When a food is called "zao," people can put the wish for "early birth of a noble son" into the New Year context. Even if what enters the mouth is actually fried glutinous rice. Rather than saying this mystifies food, it is better to say it compresses family expectation into a sentence understandable at the table.

The Jiu Zhen Nan article describes ma-zao as a puffed snack made from taro and glutinous rice, fried then coated in maltose and dipped in toppings; it also records the blessing "eating ma-zao lets you eat until old-old-old," and its place in the Jade Emperor's birthday, engagement betrothal gifts, and the bride's return visit[^3]. The "old" here is not aging, but putting longevity, growing old together, and living safely a little longer into a texture that is crisp yet sticks to the teeth.

The habit of buying whole bags of ma-zao before the New Year brings it from abstract meaning back to life: it is not an offering only viewable through museum glass, but food that can be bought by the bag, brought home, and opened when relatives visit. The Taoyuan City Government Tourism Bureau, when introducing local food vendors, also lists ma-zao, sheng-ren, cun-zao, winter melon strips, and nougat as seasonal New Year goods sold just before the holiday[^4].

The 2010 Dihua Street photo puts ma-zao, sheng-ren, and other sweets back into the shopping scene. People do not reach out only after reading the meaning; they decide whether to take a pack home after seeing colors, prices, and familiar items. New Year culture therefore also contains a very practical action: walking into the market before the holiday and buying the blessing home[^9].

## "Siù-siù-á" Is Not Four Kinds, But a Whole Snack Vocabulary

The Ministry of Education's Dictionary of Frequently-Used Taiwan Minnan records "siù-siù-á" pronounced `sì-siù-á`, glossed as "snacks, nibbles," with the example sentence "You go buy some siù-siù-á to snack on"[^2]. The importance of this word lies not in drawing strict boundaries for snacks, but in preserving how Taiwanese families speak of food: not as nutrition-science dim sum, not as items in a brand catalog, but in the everyday tone of "buy some first to try eating."

Twisted fried dough, pistachios, melon seeds, gold-coin chocolate, nougat, snowflake crisp, southern jujube walnut cake, and candied beans can be placed in the same New Year story, yet need not pretend they share a single origin. Gold-coin chocolate carries the imagination of imported goods; melon seeds preserve the family habit of cracking seeds; candied beans pull focus to sugar-preserved legumes. These differences together display the fact itself that "the New Year table has everything"[^1].

This mixture is very Taiwan: traditional offerings, local nibbles, imported chocolate, and wedding candy can appear simultaneously in one box. TaiwanPlus's English report also describes Taiwan's Lunar New Year candy as symbols of joy and good fortune, and points the lens at Taipei's Dihua Street New Year market. Candy here is not an isolated food, but a festival object composed jointly of shopping, gifting, hosting, and blessing[^5].

![Dihua Street Lunar New Year market, 2007](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b3/Dihua_Street_market%2C_Chinese_New_Year_2007.jpg)

_2007 Dihua Street Lunar New Year market. Photo: Allentchang, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dihua_Street_market,_Chinese_New_Year_2007.jpg)._[^10]

The 2007 market photo pulls the gaze back. Dihua Street is not a shop selling only one kind of candy, but a festival streetscape where offerings, dried goods, nibbles, and decorations are lined up side by side. The candy box can mix in chocolate, peanuts, and preserved fruit precisely because the New Year market allows sweet flavors from different eras to be brought home at the same time[^10].

## New Year's Politeness Makes Room for a "Not Tasty"

Gold-coin chocolate, Hershey's bars, and three-color gummy candy do not quite match the festive tone of traditional New Year. Their appearance at New Year does not directly equal being worth liking. A certain candy may have an auspicious saying behind it, but that does not mean everyone must accept its taste. This contrast is important: candy is responsible for providing blessing; people still retain the freedom to refuse the blessing's vehicle.

This freedom has always been in New Year food. The family prepares a tray of siù-siù-á so guests have something to take, so conversation has something to accompany. As for who only eats peanuts, who picks out the chocolate, who leaves the disliked candy to the end — those are often the details that truly remain in family memory. If tradition can only be praised, it quickly becomes a slogan. If tradition can be complained about, it may continue into the next generation's mouths.

![Dihua Street Lunar New Year vendor, 2007](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4b/2007-02-13_vendor_at_the_Chinese_New_Year_Fair_of_Dihua_Street.jpg)

_2007 Dihua Street Lunar New Year vendor. Photo: Michael Rehfeldt, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2007-02-13_vendor_at_the_Chinese_New_Year_Fair_of_Dihua_Street.jpg)._[^11]

The vendor's figure gives "New Year nibbles" a role often overlooked: the seller. Family memory only remembers which candy was sweetest; market memory also contains who called out to customers among lanterns, red paper, and tray after tray of goods. Siù-siù-á is therefore not just household food, but also festival work that repeatedly appears on the street[^11].

However, honest taste must also connect with the modern body. The Matsu Daily in 2013, when compiling New Year snacks, pointed out that ma-zao, melon seeds, and cun-zao have about 500–600 kcal per 100 g, and sheng-ren tang approaches 500 kcal. The article also relayed the Health Promotion Administration's suggestion that discretionary snacks and dim sum intake should not exceed 10% of daily caloric needs, while also watching sodium content[^6]. This is not to turn the New Year table into a nutrition class, but to remind us: a tray of candy's cultural weight cannot cover up the fact that it is indeed eaten into the body.

Food safety sits at the same table. The Keelung City Health Bureau's 2023 (ROC year 112) Lunar New Year and Lantern Festival seasonal food spot-check roster lists peanut brittle sampled in November 2022, test item aflatoxin, result "compliant with regulations"[^7]. New Year nibbles thus gain another modern path: they are both the language of blessing and commodities that need labeling, spot-checking, and responsible production.

## A Tray of Candy — Whose Memory Remains at the End

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Food spot-checks and calorie labels seem to pull candy away from folk custom; in fact it is exactly the opposite. When New Year nibbles become commodities that can be bought, labeled, and spot-checked, blessing gains the chance to keep circulating in today's families.

New Year candy does not need a tidy conclusion. Putting name after name, legend after legend, and tongue sensation together lets one see a not-so-tidy culture: sheng-ren tang can bless flourishing descendants; cun-zao can borrow a "zao" character to pray for early noble sons; ma-zao can bless eating until old. At the same time, certain candies may be complained about as tasting like plastic, even forgotten by the whole tray.

This is precisely where "siù-siù-á" has more warmth than "traditional New Year food." The former is not a completed checklist, but a verb: bring out, distribute to people, eat while chatting, ask names, speak homophones, finally admit one doesn't like it. Next New Year when seeing that candy box again, perhaps no need to rush asking what auspicious phrase each compartment represents. First ask the person sitting next to you which compartment they really want to eat.

The value of siù-siù-á lies not in awarding prizes to Taiwan's New Year candy, but in turning a tray of seemingly familiar food back into a question: what we keep — is it the food, the sayings, or that afternoon complaining together about a certain candy?
