> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's independent bookstore culture is not a fixed store type, but a business model that binds curation judgment, spatial scale, and public relations. It may be located in the basement of a university town, or hidden in the old streets of Guanshan, beside a highway in Changbin, or in the alleys of Penghu. Bookstores sell books, but more often, they arrange encounters for unfamiliar readers and preserve a language for a place that can continue to be spoken. From the book streets of Chongqing South Road and the humanistic bookstores of Wenluotin to the micro-spaces of small towns and offshore islands, they face different challenges regarding discounts, logistics, rent, and population, yet all answer the same question: how does a place ensure that hard-to-see books meet their readers? This value cannot replace revenue concerns, but it explains why people are still willing to bet on store rent, time, and life on a row of bookshelves after the rise of online discounts and digital reading.

## Don't Photograph the Bookstore as a Postcard

When Taiwanese talk about independent bookstores, they often start with a beautiful image: wooden bookshelves in an old house, a table by the window, and the owner recommending a hard-to-find book on social media. These images are not wrong, but they quickly turn the bookstore into a tourist backdrop. The truly difficult question is how such a space pays rent every day, acquires books, arranges manpower, and maintains a trust in reading on afternoons when no one enters.

In 2019, _The Reporter_ introduced ten independent bookstores, calling them "guides." The article recorded not a single type of decoration, but how each bookstore led readers into issues of employment for people with disabilities, migrant worker reading, women's issues, wrongful conviction rescue, gender, agriculture, and local culture. _Siam Press_ brought Southeast Asian languages and the reading needs of migrant workers into the bookstore; _Fish & Co._ placed books, food, and social support in the same space; and _Hweicui Salon_ maintained a living room in Yunlin where local and public issues could be discussed. [^1]

The common thread among these cases is not that every store makes good money, nor that every store meets a single standard of "independence." They are more like answers proposed by owners to their locations: What reading is missing here? Which people are not seen by existing bookstores? What problems require a room where people can sit down and talk?

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> **If we only understand independent bookstores as small and beautiful storefronts, we miss their true work. The curation the owner does every day is actually deciding for a place which problems are worth bringing to the forefront and which unfamiliar experiences can be taken home.**

## First Ask What "Independent" Means, Rather Than Counting How Many There Are

The definition of an independent bookstore is not simple. In a 2017 _Openbook_ interview with Chen Long-hao and Liao Ying-liang of the Taiwan Independent Bookstore Culture Association, the association was preparing for a nationwide bookstore survey. Interviewees noted that government-held bookstore counts varied because some store names did not indicate they sold books, some bookshops primarily sold stationery, and some stores lacked complete registration but still served readers in practice. The association therefore divided the entire island into ten regions, hoping to visit stores with stores to understand the types, scales, and operational difficulties of bookstores. [^2]

"Independent" does not simply mean without government subsidies. A commentary published by _The Reporter_ pointed out that bookstores have relationships with publishers, distributors, government agencies, NGOs, media, authors, and readers. The real question is whether they can maintain their own curation and operational judgment in interactions. A store that has not received subsidies is not necessarily more independent. Conversely, accepting public resources does not mean the owner has abandoned autonomy. [^3]

This distinction is important because "independent bookstore" is sometimes treated as a moral title, as if receiving subsidies, selling coffee, or undertaking local government projects makes one impure. But getting a book to a small town often requires cooperative logistics, support from public policy, and even income from coffee, stationery, courses, or freelance work to subsidize book sales. Purity does not automatically bring survival, and survival does not equal betrayal of culture.

## This Story Is Actually Much Older Than the "Hipster Trend"

Taiwan's modern bookstore history has a long pre-history. _Openbook_'s historical review notes that the Ruicheng Bookstore was established in Taichung in 1912, Chiang Wei-shui founded the Wenhua Bookstore in 1926, the Central Bookstore appeared in 1927, and Xie Xue-hong opened the International Bookstore in 1929. These bookstores did not just sell paper; they placed religion, knowledge, social movements, and political trends into accessible publications. Bookstores in the 1920s already had the functions of enlightenment, gathering, and transmitting new knowledge. [^4]

After 1949, Chongqing South Road gradually formed a new book street. During the Martial Law period, publishing was restricted, but bookstores remained places for readers to find textbooks, classical novels, foreign masterpieces, academic collections, and limited new knowledge. Used book stalls, newsstands, and specialized bookstores provided different levels of entry. These spaces cannot be simplified into a free market or into political resistance, but they did allow certain texts to continue circulating in an environment of censorship and resource scarcity. [^4]

The Tangshan Bookstore, opened in 1982, is another important clue. Long before the term "independent bookstore" was common in Taiwan, it focused on humanities and social sciences, leftist thought, and niche publishing. After Eslite Bookstore was established in 1989, large bookstores redefined reading spaces through events, lectures, curation, and spatial design. The two are not the same, but together they show that bookstores are not just about arranging books waiting for checkout; they are fields that can intervene in thought and life. [^4] [^5]

Wenluotin connects bookstores with the university town, social movements, and dense alleyways. Tangshan, Donghai Bookstore, and other shops formed a knowledge network accessible on foot. The importance of this network lies not in the number of stores, but in how different owners' judgments on book lists, issues, and readers are adjacent to each other, allowing a reader to pass through several different scales of thought in a single afternoon. [^2]

## After 2000, Ideals Had to Learn to Do the Math

The digital age made books faster to acquire, but did not make bookstore costs disappear. _Openbook_'s 2023 industry analysis, using bookstore gross margins, purchase discounts, and monthly revenue estimates, pointed out that if small bookstores sell books close to the list price, the gross margin may still be insufficient to pay for rent, labor, and logistics. The article noted that when large chains can purchase at lower discounts, independent bookstores find it hard to sustain operations relying solely on new book sales. This is an industry analysis, not to be rewritten as a government census, but it clearly explains how price wars become the daily math inside the store. [^6]

Therefore, many stores began adding work related to books but not solely dependent on book sales. They hold lectures, do publishing, undertake local collection, run cafes, sell stationery, or turn bookstores into guesthouses, exhibition spaces, and community classrooms. These actions do not necessarily make the store richer, but they transform the bookstore from a single retail point into an intersection of multiple income streams and relationships.

_Openbook_ recorded "Small Book & Vegetable," which placed second-hand books and friendly agricultural products together. _Pasela'an Huanhuan Shuwu_ (Slowly Book House) is closer to an Indigenous humanities and history studio, serving the local area through books, mother tongue, folk songs, and tribal fieldwork. These cases remind us that composite operation is not about turning the bookstore into a mall that sells everything, but about the owner finding the relationship where books and the local context can truly support each other. [^6]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> **Dismissing coffee, courses, or local government projects as unprofessional is to jump to conclusions too quickly. What is more worth asking is whether this income allows curation, reading events, and employee labor to continue, and who bears the increased workload of composite operation.**

## From City to Small Town, What Changes Is the Entry Point

With the rise of the language of "local revitalization," bookstores are often described as tools to "light up places." This statement is infectious but still needs to be grounded in details. A 2023 report by _Smile Taiwan_ recorded the Guanshan Lilon Information Office x Guanshan Organic Bookstore, the Haiyu Small Village in Taimali, the Liuyu Bookstore in Pinglin, the _Love_ Bookstore in Xincheng, Hualien, and the _Nian Nian You Zhen_ (Yearly Have Snapper) in Penghu. The commonality among these spaces is not that they share the same business model, but that they first answer what the locality lacks: an entry point for exchanging books, a place for children to read, a room bringing marine education into daily life, or a place for residents to talk about their living ground again. [^7]

In Changbin, Taitung, _Shuzhou_ (Book Porridge) can be seen from the Commons archive page marked as an independent bookstore in a remote village. In this image, the bookshelves do not completely separate the road from the store interior; readers can still see the trees and street outside before approaching the reading table. It is not a miniature model of a "remote village city bookstore," but a concrete example of how a bookstore connects with daily routines. [^10]

Here, we cannot write "locality" as a naturally harmonious community. When bookstores enter small towns, they may also encounter contradictions of aging population, insufficient reading population, rent, transportation, and commercialization of tourism. Whether a bookstore survives depends on the owner's professionalism, local relationships, public support, and cash flow, not just having a beautiful old house. True locality is often the owner's willingness to listen to local people over the long term and slowly adjust curation and events based on these relationships. Over time, the event posters in the store, reader comments, local publications, and oral stories become a small archive, letting later people know what this place once cared about, and letting the bookstore not only preserve books published by others but gradually preserve memories formed together with the locality, leaving clues for the next generation to read.

## Bookshelves Are Also a Form of Editing

The hardest part of independent bookstores for e-commerce to replicate is not just that someone picks up the book for you, but that someone makes a judgment for you first. When online platforms push books to the forefront through categorization, ratings, and recommendation algorithms, independent bookstores use a slower form of editing. The owner might place an obscure poetry collection at the entrance, placing it next to local history, gender studies, or a newly published novel. A reader comes to buy the book they originally wanted but encounters questions on the shelf they have not yet searched for.

_Openbook_'s 2026 guided tour of bookstores in the Zhongshan business district of Taipei provided several detailed examples. _Poetry Life_ selects about 95% literature; _Shinge French Bookstore_ focuses on French-language books, French literature, and Taiwan-France exchanges. One store lets clerks and readers pass sticky notes on books relay-style; _Punding_ places independent publishing, design, exhibitions, and selected goods together; _Kuifuju_ re-translates the local memory of Chengfeng Street using Taiwan humanities/history, old maps, and antique furniture. These bookstores do not need to be judged on who is more "truly independent"; they show how curation forms different public entrances. [^8]

_Taiwan Panorama_'s English report also brings the lens outside the city, introducing how independent bookstores become nodes of reading and exchange in local society. The value of this English source is not in putting an international label on Taiwan's bookstores, but in reminding us that the cultural function of bookstores can be translated for external readers, but translation should not erase its relationship with transportation, population, local work, and community organization. [^9]

The image of _Zhiyin Bookstore_ (Soulmate Bookstore) in Magong, Penghu, presents another scale. The Commons archive page records the store was established in 2019, located on Shude Road in Guangfu Village. The image shows bookshelves and a narrow indoor space, indicating that independent bookstores do not necessarily need large areas or complete city-style displays. They can start with a recognizable storefront for a community and gradually accumulate reader relationships. [^11]

## Policy Can Stop the Bleeding, But Cannot Replace Bookstore Judgment

After the Ministry of Culture was established in 2012, independent bookstores were more clearly placed into cultural policy discussions. _Openbook_'s interview records that the Ministry of Culture once used independent bookstores as a theme for the National Policy Conference on Culture, and subsequently allocated resources to support bookstores. The 2022 industry review recorded that in 2013, the Ministry of Culture launched the Independent Bookstore Dream Project, and in 2014, the Friendly Book Industry Cooperative was established, attempting to address the problems of small bookstores having few books, high logistics costs, and unstable supply. [^2] [^5]

The role of policy is to spread out costs that were previously borne solely by the owner, providing space for new stores to trial and error, and allowing bookstores to be seen as community micro-cultural centers rather than just retail businesses on a business registration. However, policy cannot choose books for the owner, cannot schedule shifts for employees, and cannot guarantee that local residents will buy books. If subsidies only pursue the number of events, small stores may become short-term project executors without leaving stable readers and labor conditions.

In 2024, the Ministry of Culture issued an announcement regarding the controversy over the visiting committee members recommended by the Independent Bookstore Culture Association, stating that the association undertook the physical bookstore visit business and that the relevant committee members' visit and review work were immediately suspended. This announcement is not the entirety of the independent bookstore culture, but it reminds us that when bookstores enter public policy, issues of representation, selection methods, and who has the right to determine cultural value arise. Writing the subsidy system as a one-way cultural goodwill ignores the need for transparency, prudence, and verifiable procedures. [^12]

## The Future of Bookstores Is Not Returning to the Past

There have been several obvious turning points in Taiwan's bookstore history. Early bookstores connected publishing, education, and social movements; post-war book streets provided textbooks, used books, and new knowledge; the 1980s chain bookstores brought bright and large-scale displays; the 1990s Eslite incorporated events and space into bookstores; after 2000, online channels rewrote price and logistics; and in the 2010s, more micro-bookstores entered localities. Each change was not the complete disappearance of the previous type of bookstore, but a redistribution of its original functions. [^4] [^5]

Therefore, the future of independent bookstores does not have to be replicating the golden revenue of the 1990s, nor pretending to return to before the internet appeared. What is more worth preserving is the bookstore's ability as a public intermediary. It can have a book selected, explained, and placed in the context of a place's life, allowing readers and strangers to see a certain issue simultaneously. When it cooperates with schools, libraries, or community groups, bookstores can also take curation judgment out of the store, becoming the starting point for reading courses, local walks, and public discussion. This work cannot be fully measured by sales numbers, but it cannot therefore escape the calculation of salaries, rent, logistics, and working hours.

For readers, supporting independent bookstores is not just about taking photos in the store, nor does it require buying expensive hardcovers every time. It can be buying a book the owner spent time selecting, participating in an event that does not rely on traffic but is well-prepared, respecting store rules, or asking why a place lost a reading entrance after the bookstore closes. For the government and the publishing industry, support is not just subsidies or promotion, but establishing a fairer supply chain so that small stores are not forever forced to work under worse conditions due to low purchase volumes. For schools and libraries, bookstores can also be partners in cooperative procurement, thematic curation, and reading education, allowing students to see how books are selected, introduced, and placed into life, rather than only appearing in exam or borrowing statistics.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> **What is most worth preserving in independent bookstores is not a certain fixed wooden bookshelf, but the ability for a locality to still choose, interpret, and exchange knowledge itself. When storefronts change and books circulate digitally, this question of public intermediary remains.**

Taiwan's independent bookstore culture is therefore not a simple story of "many bookstores mean happiness," nor a tragic story of "e-commerce arrived so bookstores must disappear." It is a history of continuous negotiation: owners negotiate between ideals and cash flow, readers negotiate between convenience and relationships, the government negotiates between assistance and governance, and localities negotiate between tourism, human connections, and long-term life.

When a book is placed on the shelf, the owner has actually made a choice for the reader. After the reader walks in, they may change the bookstore with their own questions. This back-and-forth makes the bookstore a living room for a place, not because it is always quiet or always romantic, but because it is still willing to leave a chair for strangers.
