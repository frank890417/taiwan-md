> **30-second overview:** Iron window grilles appeared in Taiwanese architecture during the Japanese colonial period; wartime metal recycling interrupted their use; from the late 1960s through the 1970s they proliferated alongside housing and steel supply. They combined anti-theft, daylighting, and decorative functions, while also inscribing homeowners' occupations, beliefs, and family memories onto the street. This article traces a path from the 1958 Taiwan Provincial Assembly's 48 grille panels, through Lao-wu-yan Studio's field records, to historic preservation in Lukang and Anping, explaining how an unsigned iron component became a history of ordinary people's lives.

![Residential window grilles in a Taichung Beitun alley, showing how iron windows became everyday components of residential facades](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a6/Houses_with_window_grills_in_Beitun.jpg)

Image used for streetscape context, not as evidence of a specific era or homeowner story._

In 1958, the windows of the Taiwan Provincial Assembly building were fitted with 48 iron window grille panels. Each panel measured approximately 136 cm tall by 86 cm wide, and no two patterns were exactly alike. These were not the standardized security mesh common today, but a set of iron pieces that placed geometric lines, hand welding, and the gravitas of political architecture on the same wall.[^1]

Looking further back, iron window grilles had already appeared in Taiwanese architecture during the Japanese colonial period. After the war expanded in 1937, the Japanese government issued metal recycling orders, and the iron windows and railings on civilian homes were dismantled to become wartime materiel. Iron window grilles were not always on the streets; they once disappeared from daily life because of war.[^1]

Therefore, the iron window grilles familiar to Taiwanese cannot be summarized merely as "old house decoration." They are the result of postwar housing, industrial supply, family security, and artisanal skill growing together. The most counterintuitive aspect is that they were originally meant to keep people out, yet ultimately displayed the homeowner's stories to passersby.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Taiwan's iron window grilles are not the final line that closes off the home, but the first way the home speaks itself to the street.

## From Wartime Scarcity to Shophouse Daily Life

Materials from the Legislative Yuan's Democracy and Politics Park make the timeline clear. In the early postwar period, steel was mainly allocated to reconstruction and national defense, with civilian use restricted. By the late 1960s, industry shifted from agriculture to manufacturing, and after China Steel Corporation's establishment in 1971, steel supply stabilized, allowing iron window grilles to gradually enter ordinary residences.[^1]

This timeline explains why iron window grilles are often seen as a visual marker of "postwar Taiwan." The 1950s to 1970s were precisely when housing rapidly increased and shophouses and apartments appeared en masse. _Taiwan Panorama_ reported that this was also a period when houses went up quickly but patterns remained free.[^2] The "proliferation" here cannot be understood as every household using the same pattern; rather, material supply, housing demand, and ironworking technique gradually made iron windows an affordable, customizable building component.

At the time, iron windows were not chosen from a catalog by model number. The Kaohsiung City Urban Development Bureau's old house renovation manual notes that early iron windows used primarily black iron, shaped by forging and bending, with no fixed standards; geometric arrangements, flora, and figurative totems could all become the artisan's compositional material.[^3]

This approach made windows on the same street look like relatives, yet not like the same mass-produced product. Some wanted flowers, some wanted landscapes, some embedded their occupation, faith, or family preferences. When the Taipei Public Library compiled _Lao-wu-yan and Iron Window Grilles_, it specifically noted that landscapes and flora, music and dance, animals and insects, and religious feng shui could all appear in iron window grilles.[^4]

## One Grille Panel, One Segment of a Homeowner's Life

![Lukang historic house facade, providing visual context for understanding the relationship between local shophouses and windows and doors](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/02/Old_taiwanese_house_in_Lukang%2C_Taiwan.JPG)

This image illustrates old house facade and living scale; it does not claim every window shown is the iron window grille discussed in this article._

In 2020, _Readmoo Frontline_ interviewed Hsin Yung-sheng and Yang Chao-ching of Lao-wu-yan Studio. The two originally just rode scooters through Tainan alleys photographing old houses; as they shot, they discovered the patterns on windows revealed residents' personalities more than the houses' exterior walls. In half a year, they photographed one to two thousand iron window grilles.[^5]

Their working method resembled fieldwork. They didn't just shoot facades; they found homeowners to chat with, asking how patterns originated, how houses came to be built. In Toucheng, Yilan, there is a dragon-and-phoenix iron window; in Lukang, a homeowner turned memories of a European trip with their daughter into an Arc de Triomphe pattern. The iron window was originally an inorganic iron piece; the story emerged only after the homeowner opened the door and served drinks.[^5]

Hsin Yung-sheng said they "wanted to find the taste in memory." This phrase originally referred to breakfast and old shops, but applies equally to iron window grilles. Iron window grilles are not art detached from life; together with terrazzo floors, spirit altars, door frame heights, and potted plants, they constitute the space a family uses every day.[^5]

This also changes how we look at old houses. Real estate agents call houses "properties"; homeowners treat them as vessels a family has lived in. The value of window grilles lies not only in form, but in how they let a stranger know what this household once believed, who they remembered, what they wanted to leave before the eyes of those outside.[^4] [^5]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The most precious part of an iron window grille is often not how beautifully it is welded, but that it makes the homeowner willing to spend five more minutes talking about their home.

## The Legislative Yuan's 48 Panels: Why Is Every Panel Different?

The Taiwan Provincial Assembly building offers a public architecture counterpart. Completed in 1958, this building used large-area iron window grilles that simultaneously bore anti-theft and decorative functions. The front had 30 panels, the left and right sides 8 each, totaling 48, and every panel had a different design.[^1]

The reference page notes that shapes resembling Western swords can be seen in the grilles. A local interpretation holds that provincial legislators served as the people's voice in the chamber, engaging in verbal duels with officials or other legislators, so the sword-fighting imagery was placed in the window grilles. The original design intent of this account cannot be verified, so it is better treated as the building's local memory rather than a definitive design history conclusion.[^1]

This example matters not because it proves every flower has a code. It reminds us that iron window grilles in Taiwan were never limited to the scale of private residences. They can be a small grille outside a kitchen, or an entire row of architectural vocabulary on a public institution's facade. Function and symbol have been stuck together from the start.

## Who Is Recording These Unsigned Works

The creators of iron window grilles mostly left no names. Artisans might work only from a homeowner's "make it a bit floral" or a hand-drawn sketch; once the house was complete, the work became part of the architecture. Unlike sculpture with exhibition titles or paintings with signatures, usually only the people living inside know why it looks that way.

Lao-wu-yan Studio, founded in 2013, brought this unsigned streetscape into large-scale organization and sharing for the first time. The Taipei Public Library records that Hsin Yung-sheng and Yang Chao-ching began by sharing photos online, gradually building a community, publishing books, and turning iron window grilles from "something very ordinary on old houses" into a Taiwanese element worth preserving.[^4]

The National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts' special topic page documents artist Wu Chien-yi's use of iron window grilles as a creative and research subject since 2015. When iron window grilles enter artistic creation, they cease to be mere old house appendages and become a medium for re-viewing collective memory.[^6]

In 2026, Tainan's Yuejin Art Museum hosted a lecture on Yang Chao-ching's "Taiwan Iron Window Grille Records" as part of its salon series, showing this topic has moved from personal photography to public discussion at local cultural institutions.[^7]

This shift carries a contradiction. Iron window grilles move us precisely because they were not originally made to be seen. As they are organized, published, and exhibited, Taiwan begins to see its own daily life, but must also take care not to over-package homeowners' life stories into consumable retro aesthetics.

## Preservation Does Not Mean Turning Every Window into an Exhibit

The difficulty of old house preservation usually stems from buildings simultaneously needing safety, daylighting, ventilation, and new uses. Removing old windows for aluminum ones is fast and easily meets modern demands. Retaining iron windows requires understanding their structure, rust condition, fixing methods, and original use context.

The Hualien County Cultural Affairs Bureau's historic building reference page lists "iron window grilles and white walls" as one of the recognizable features of Forestry Bureau dormitory architecture. When the Pingtung County Government introduced the Victory New Village preservation plan, it noted that 120 historic households retained old-style iron window grilles, cement flower bricks, and colored glass windows. These cases illustrate that preservation's focus is not moving decoration alone into museums, but keeping it within its original architectural relationships as much as possible.[^8] [^9]

Kaohsiung's old house renovation manual offers another direction. Old houses can be reused, and iron window grilles can become materials in new spaces, but the premise is understanding the original techniques and proportions, not merely copying a pattern that "looks retro."[^3]

A report from Shih Hsin University's Journalism Department writes that iron window grilles are being transformed into cultural creative crafts. This path can give old techniques new income sources, but may also flatten the originally complex life contexts into commodity patterns. What is truly worth preserving includes not only flowers, butterflies, or geometric grids, but also how artisans made them, why homeowners chose them, and what kind of life these patterns once clung to.[^10]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The minimum standard for preserving iron window grilles: after polishing, they must not lose the house they originally protected, lit, and told stories for.

## What We See Today May Be the Last Layer of Iron Paint

Taiwan's urban renewal and old house renovation will continue. Not every iron window deserves to be kept intact, nor must every new building imitate postwar flower windows. A more honest approach is to record first, then judge: photograph patterns, interview homeowners, identify techniques, confirm the house's age, and only then decide on restoration, adaptive reuse, or demolition.

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs NGO Bilingual Website's reprint of an iron window grille article mentions a Lukang homeowner who placed the local landmarks Shiyi Lou and Banbian Jing into window grilles. Such examples show that window grilles are not mere decorative frames, but residents' way of shrinking the local map to their doorstep.[^11]

If we only see iron window grilles as anti-theft equipment, we miss their second function. If we only see them as retro aesthetics, we forget they once responded to real security anxieties, material shortages, and family needs. They are simultaneously protection and display. They hold the outside world at bay a little, and reveal the lives inside a little.

## Three Things to Distinguish When Viewing Images

First, images can help readers recognize the scale of iron windows, windows and doors, and old house facades, but cannot alone prove a pattern's era. Second, the author and license information on the Commons file page, and the historical sources of Taiwan's iron window grilles, are two different evidentiary chains. The former answers "can this photo be used"; the latter answers "how was this window formed." Third, the appearance of window grilles in old house photos does not mean homeowner stories can be freely invented by viewers. What can be confirmed relies on the file page and article sources. What cannot be confirmed remains as visual impression; speculation is not written as historical fact.

This distinction may seem conservative, but it is the most basic respect when preserving vernacular architecture. Iron window grilles inherently lack signatures; once photos detach from original captions, they are easily relabeled with wrong locations, eras, or homeowner identities. Images are therefore not article decoration, but historical source portals that must be verified together.

Next time you walk through a Taiwanese alley, don't rush to find a pretty old house. Look at the windows first. See if there's an asymmetrical corner, a pattern like a travel souvenir, a flower obscured by a potted plant, or a stretch of black paint already faded. There may be no author's name, but someone once gestured before an artisan, someone once installed it for safety, and someone once placed what they most wanted to leave behind in this life outside the window.
