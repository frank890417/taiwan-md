> **30-second overview:** Lacquerware is not simply "coating wood with a layer of shiny lacquer." It was once Taiwan families' tea trays, lunch boxes, cake stands, and wedding tea ceremony utensils, as well as an industry exporting to Japan in the 1970s. Later, plastic products replaced lacquerware, and lacquer tree planting, lacquer extraction, mass production, and master-apprentice traditions all declined. Today, when we talk about Taiwan lacquer art, we are actually discussing an industrial chain that goes from the mountains and forests to daily life, and then back from daily life into memory.

## Beneath the Shining Surface, There Is a Tree

The easiest misunderstanding about lacquerware is that people only see its surface. True natural lacquer comes from lacquer tree sap. After raw lacquer is collected, it must be refined into mature lacquer, and mineral pigments can be added for use. Lacquer is waterproof, corrosion-resistant, heat-resistant, acid and alkali resistant, and also has antibacterial and insulating properties. It is both a paint and an adhesive that binds materials together.[^2] [^4]

Therefore, the first material of lacquerware is not wood, but time. The body can be made of wood, bamboo, rattan, paper, or ceramic, followed by sealing the body, applying primer, and lacquering layer by layer, turning the surface into something usable, touchable, and preservable. Plain lacquering, inlaying gold, carving lacquer, inlaying, and covering mirrors are not just different names for "coloring." Each requires technical judgment. [^2]

Natural lacquer is not a nostalgic version of modern chemical paint. It comes from the mountains and forests and must rely on tree species, climate, collection, and refining. The final luster of the object is connected to a whole set of understanding of the material's temperament. This is why a seemingly quiet black lacquer box may better explain how Taiwan transformed nature into daily life than a flamboyantly shaped artwork.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The "slowness" of lacquerware is not a romantic decoration. Every layer of lacquer needs to wait for drying and stabilization. If this waiting is removed, what remains may still be shiny, but it is no longer the same object.

## Puli Polished Lacquer: How to Paint Taiwan onto Objects

Taiwan had early lacquer usage habits introduced by Han Chinese settlers, but lacquer craft truly matured during the Japanese colonial period. The National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Institute notes that in 1916, Shang Zhonggong established the Shang Zhong Craft Workshop in Taichung, incorporating indigenous totems, plants, and local specialties into decorative patterns, gradually developing "Puli Polished Lacquer." The government later set up the Taichung Craft Training Institute to cultivate lacquer artisans.[^3]

The significance of "Puli Polished Lacquer" is not just about whether it looks good. It places Taiwan's local imagery onto objects influenced by both Chinese and Japanese techniques, making lacquerware no longer just reproductions of imported styles. The Kaohsiung Museum of History also points out through its collection exhibition that the style of Taiwan lacquerware was formed under the influence of different cultures, not developed in a straight line from a single source.[^1]

After introducing the Annamese lacquer tree in 1921, the trial planting in Nantou Fish Lake was successful, and Miaoli Cuifeng and Tainan Yujing also planted large quantities and set up refining factories. This detail is crucial: the story of Taiwan lacquerware did not only happen in workshops, but also in forest land, agricultural experiments, and raw material processing plants. The cultural history of objects has always included a history of plants and industry.[^3]

## Lacquerware Was Once Part of Daily Life at Home

The Kaohsiung Museum of History describes lacquerware from the Japanese colonial period as an indispensable part of common life. After the founding of the Republic of China, usage gradually declined, but lacquerware remained in wedding banquets and gift-giving scenes. The museum's 2018 exhibition "Lacquer Travel Memoirs" started from common life, selecting over 100 pieces of collection to try to let visitors see the usage patterns of lacquerware during its heyday.[^1]

These objects do not need to be placed in a museum before they have cultural value. Tea trays have held tea for others, cake stands have received festival foods, document boxes have held papers for a family or business. The history of lacquerware is often not written by famous works, but by the repeated touch of objects in daily life. When the National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Center introduced the Meiyun Lacquer Studio, it also recorded the connection between wedding tea trays used in Taiwan weddings and early mass-produced exports.[^5]

| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Wedding Tea Ceremony             | Holding tea cups, completing the ritual | Family customs and object dignity         |
| Festival Cakes                   | Packaging, distributing food            | Food and family relationships             |
| Document Boxes and Storage Boxes | Saving documents and small items        | Daily memories of families and businesses |
| Gift Giving                      | Expressing relationships and identity   | Objects as social language                |

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> "Common people" is not a downgraded version of craft history. When a tea tray is brought out, lacquerware is simultaneously furniture, customs, industry, and touch. Its value lies not only in who made it most finely, but also in how many people have treated it as part of their lives.

## From Peak Export to Being Replaced by Plastic

After World War II, Taiwan's lacquer factories continued the human resources and institutional foundations left over from the Japanese colonial period. The National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Institute notes that the 1970s was the peak period of Taiwan lacquerware exporting to Japan. The centers of lacquer tree planting also shifted to Nantou Puli and National County. In addition to domestic use, raw lacquer was once exported to Japan in large quantities.[^3]

This golden age is easy to write off as "the golden age of traditional crafts," but a more accurate description is: it was a craft industry that connected manual skills, factory management, and the international market. Wang Xianmin recalls that in the 1960s, his family's factory often had a shortage of workers, and mass production and export of lacquerware were once considered a forward-looking business.[^5]

The turning point became clear after the 1980s. The Ministry of Culture points out that plastic products gradually replaced lacquerware, along with changes in economic structure and rising wages, the lacquer industry nearly shrank in the 1990s, and domestic lacquer materials also almost relied on imports.[^4] This is not simply because "everyone suddenly stopped loving tradition," but because price, speed, labor, and material supply simultaneously rewrote daily life.

| Period                   | Main Position of Lacquerware in Taiwan                | Key Changes                                                            |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Japanese Colonial Period | Daily objects and gradual maturation of craft systems | Shang Zhong Craft Workshop, Puli Polished Lacquer, and training system |
| 1960s–1970s              | Mass production, export, and industrial growth        | Factoryization and Japanese market                                     |
| 1980s–1990s              | Decline in daily use                                  | Plastic replacement, rising wages, and industrial structure changes    |
| 2000s and beyond         | Cultural heritage, creation, and restoration          | Preservation, education, material self-sufficiency, and redesign       |

## The Hardest Thing to Preserve Is the Entire Material Chain

In 2022, the lacquer art community designated July 7th each year as "Taiwan Lacquer Art Day." The wordplay between "lacquer" and "seven" is a good memory entrance, but what the Ministry of Culture's press release really points out is that Taiwan lacquer art lacks material self-sufficiency, has few lacquer refiners, and the workforce is aging. Preservation work cannot stop at displaying finished products.[^4]

This is also why today's revival includes lacquer tree restoration, local lacquer refining, talent training, and value chain reconstruction. If only artists are still creating, but no one knows how to take care of lacquer trees, collect raw lacquer, refine materials, handle the body, and repair old objects, then what is left is only the appearance of the work, and the ecology of the craft has already been broken.

The National Cultural Heritage Database has listed "Taiwan Natural Lacquer Industry Technology" as a cultural heritage case. This registration reminds us that what should be preserved is not just a lacquer box, but the knowledge and objects needed to make, repair, and practice this technology.[^6]

## Wang Qingshuang and the Relay of a Lacquer Road

The National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Center introduces that Wang Qingshuang once studied lacquerware in Japan and brought the technology back to Taiwan. In 2010, he was designated as "Important Traditional Artist - Lacquer Craftsman" under the Cultural Heritage Preservation Law. The same page also records his son Wang Xianmin taking over the workshop, moving from mass production and export to creation and teaching.[^5]

This father-son lacquer road is not a simple story of "master passing skills to the next generation." It passes through the peak of the industry and the shrinkage of the market. What the next generation receives is not just skills, but also how to answer a modern question: when a lacquer object is no longer the cheapest and fastest container, why is it still worth making?

The detachable lacquer objects provide a concrete answer. The introduction by the Craft Research and Development Center explains that during production, lacquer and hemp cloth are repeatedly attached to the molded body, and after the thickness is formed, the clay mold is removed, leaving a hollow and lightweight cloth lacquer body. Its value is not in imitating antiquity, but in the result formed by material performance, body feeling, and process.[^5]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Inheritance is not to seal the past into a specimen. For lacquer art, true inheritance must face whether there are lacquer trees in the forest, people in the workshop, and people in life who are still willing to use it.

## Local Workshops Are Not Just Local Stories

The locality of lacquerware is not only about what kind of flowers and birds are painted on the object, but also about how a place reconnects materials, workshops, and exhibitions. The Taoyuan Municipal Daxu Wood Industry Ecological Museum's exhibition "Daxu Takes Lacquer" sorts out the contemporary image of lacquerware, making lacquer art not only stay in the memory of old factories, but also meet wood industry settlements, local museums, and new-generation creators.[^7]

The natural lacquer exhibition in Puli, Nantou, provides another entrance. The Long Nan Museum of Natural Lacquer Wares introduced by the Sun Moon Lake Tourism Network puts natural lacquer, lacquer industry, and Puli local tourism in the same narrative framework. This kind of display still needs continuous verification and updating, but at least it reminds us that the preservation of lacquerware is not to move an object into a showcase, but to let people see where the raw materials come from, how the workshop operates, and why the local area is still willing to discuss it.[^8]

Taiwan lacquerware thus has two easily overlooked scales. One is the scale that can be touched by hand, such as the thickness of the edge of a lacquer tray and the surface luster. The other is the scale on the map, such as the production, training, and exhibition network of Puli, National County, Cuifeng, Yujing, and Taichung. Looking only at the former will make lacquerware become an artwork. Looking only at the latter will make lacquerware become an industrial timeline. Putting both together is the only way to see how it leaves weight in local life.

## One Object, Three Different Times

Lacquerware lives in three times at the same time. The first is the time of the tree: lacquer trees need to grow, raw lacquer needs to be collected and matured, and raw materials are not inventory that can be increased by turning on the factory power. The second is the time of the craftsman: the body, primer, lacquering, and trimming must be carried out according to the state of the materials. What the skilled person accumulates is not only fixed steps, but also hand feeling and judgment. The third is the time of the user: the object must be taken out, cleaned, put back, and used again in the family, so that it can be transformed from a commodity into a life memory.

Industrialization once compressed the first two times into the factory, allowing lacquerware to be mass-produced and exported. Plasticization then changed the third time to "buy and use, break and replace." Therefore, the decline of lacquerware does not mean that an ancient aesthetic was eliminated alone, but that the three times no longer meet in the same object. Today's restoration and creation, if it is to be meaningful, cannot only make lacquerware look like the past, but also redesign the reason it is used in contemporary life.

Repairing old lacquerware is therefore not to completely eliminate cracks. Restorers must judge whether the body is deformed, whether the lacquer layer is lifted, and whether the added lacquer can coexist with the original surface. Over-pursuing a brand-new appearance may instead erase the usage history of the object. This judgment requires material knowledge, as well as an understanding of the original function of the object. When the National Cultural Heritage Database takes natural lacquer industry technology as the preservation object, what is preserved is exactly these decisions that cannot be learned only by looking at the exhibition cabinet.

## Why Do We Need to Re-understand Lacquerware Today

Taiwan lacquerware is important, not because it is "very traditional," but because it stacks several Taiwan issues that are often discussed separately on the same object: the flow of technology during the colonial period, the generation of local images, the post-war export industry, family customs, the plastic consumption transformation, and how cultural heritage faces the material chain breakage. The Ministry of Culture's promotion of Taiwan Lacquer Art Day and lacquer tree restoration is exactly a response to this bigger problem than a single work.[^4]

Putting lacquerware back into daily life can also avoid speaking for it with adjectives such as "exquisite, warm, and elegant." It was once used to hold food, serve tea, save documents, and give gifts. It once supported a family with a factory, and was later squeezed out by cheaper materials. Today it re-enters exhibitions, education, and design, which does not mean history is retreating to a golden age, but that Taiwan is trying to decide which slow crafts are worth re-arranging into life.

The luster of lacquerware is not painted on at one time. It comes from the sap of the lacquer tree, the hands of the craftsman, the time waiting for drying, and the user's repeated taking it out. When we look at an old tea tray again, what really needs to be identified may not be how bright it is, but how much of that light still contains Taiwan's ability to produce, understand, and pass it on by itself.
