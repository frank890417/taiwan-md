# Half a Century of Telling Taiwan to the World: From _Taiwan Panorama_ Magazine to the Online Taiwan Panorama

> **30-Second Overview:** _Taiwan Panorama Magazine_ is a monthly publication published by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs for international readers. It was founded in 1976 as _Panorama Pictorial_, renamed _Panorama_ in 1978, and officially renamed _Taiwan Panorama Magazine_ in 2006. Over half a century, it has centered on bilingual print editions (Chinese/English), gradually expanding to digital versions in Chinese/Japanese, Indonesian, Thai, and Vietnamese, as well as a searchable digital database. What it preserves is not just the scenery and figures of Taiwan, but also a long record of how a society changes and chooses to present itself externally.[^1] [^2]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> This article does not treat _Taiwan Panorama_ merely as a magazine introducing Taiwan, but reads it through three overlapping roles: an official publication of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, a cross-linguistic medium for Taiwanese narratives, and an archival material that can be retrieved by future generations. This arrangement is because official documents best confirm the publishing system and distribution methods, while academic research reminds us to investigate how an overseas propaganda periodical selects, organizes, and presents "Taiwan's cultural image."[^6]

## A Name, Three Transformations

When founded in 1976, _Taiwan Panorama_ was called _Panorama Pictorial_. This name itself carried a clear mission: to introduce Taiwan’s national conditions and daily life to overseas readers through images and text. In the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' 50th-anniversary review in 2026, this history was organized into three nodes: founding in 1976, renaming to _Panorama_ in 1978, and official renaming to _Taiwan Panorama Magazine_ in 2006. The change of name is superficially a brand update, but fundamentally reflects the reordering of relationships between readers, Taiwan, and the world.[^1]

As a publication from an authoritarian period, it initially served the function of conveying messages externally; however, as Taiwan underwent industrial changes, social liberalization, and democratization, mere "introduction" was insufficient to encompass the complexity of the island. The official summary of 50 years did not portray Taiwan as a single upward success curve, but rather noted that it witnessed Taiwan's transition from an agricultural society to a high-tech island, while also documenting its shift from authoritarianism to democracy.[^1] Academic research views _Panorama_ from 1976 to 2005 as texts of overseas propaganda, studying the cultural image of Taiwan it constructed; this offers another reading perspective: the magazine not only reflects the era but also participates in shaping how Taiwan is seen through its selection of topics, photographs, and translations.[^6]

## "Soft Issues" Are Not Soft at All

The official website of _Taiwan Panorama_ categorizes its content as "soft issues" such as tourism, culture, and local customs, while also listing major topics like environmental protection, vulnerable group care, technological development, and government policies. This classification is easily misunderstood as "only writing about lighthearted Taiwan," but in reality, life, culture, and local experiences are often where the traces of social change are most evident.[^2]

How a market is redeveloped, how a traditional craft is passed down, how a local festival faces commercialization—these topics may not appear in policy white papers, yet they allow readers to see how systems manifest in daily life. On the other hand, environment, technology, and public policy are not just "hard news"; they also require people, settings, and time for readers to grasp the weight of abstract decisions on lived experience.

Therefore, the editorial positioning of _Taiwan Panorama_ is closer to a long-exposure photograph. While news tracks events, it often zooms out to observe how an issue enters a local context, how a policy changes people's choices, and how a society adjusts itself amidst controversy. This is why the official website describes the magazine as "witnessing Taiwanese history," rather than just positioning it as a travel or culture magazine.[^2]

## Bilingualism Is Not Decoration; It Is an Editorial Method

_Taiwan Panorama_ currently distributes bilingual print editions (Chinese/English) and Chinese/Japanese online versions to over one hundred countries and regions. The official English page states that readers include academic institutions, cultural workers, business professionals, overseas Taiwanese, and international readers interested in Taiwan.[^2] This publishing structure means that from the outset, articles must address two questions: what do Taiwanese readers already know, and what needs to be explained to overseas readers?

Translation is therefore not an add-on process after the article is finished. It influences how the original text frames the background, how it handles proper nouns, and how it balances local vernacular with cross-cultural understanding. For Taiwanese readers, an article can proceed directly from shared experience; for overseas readers, the same place name, festival, or system may require historical context. The side-by-side bilingual presentation forces editors to re-examine what they "assume everyone knows."

The Indonesian, Thai, and Vietnamese editions launched in December 2015 took this cross-linguistic publishing a step further. The official explanation is that these three versions are published bi-monthly and hosted digitally on the website; their background relates to Taiwan's promotion of Southward policies, increasing Southeast Asian countries' understanding of Taiwan, and presenting the aspects of life in Taiwan for people from Southeast Asia.[^2] The significance here is not just adding three languages, but because the readers' imagination began to change: Southeast Asia was no longer just "a place that needs to understand Taiwan," but also a part of Taiwan's internal social story.

## From Magazine to Database

The weakness of a magazine is its temporality: one issue is easily overshadowed by the next; but the strength of a magazine is its accumulation: decades later, when readers look back, the photos, terminology, and interview subjects from that time become historical material. _Taiwan Panorama_ early on attempted to liberate individual reports from the journal's lifecycle. The official website records that in 1984, the first series, _Facing Contemporary Figures_, was launched, followed by over thirty other thematic series covering international Sinology, indigenous cultures, Taiwanese flora and fauna, customs, and cuisine.[^2]

The significance of the serials is re-organization. Journals are organized by month; series are organized by theme; the former allows readers to advance with the times, while the latter allows readers to find a connection years later. When a series of human interest stories is compiled into a book, they are no longer just content from one month but can become references for studying Taiwanese society, publishing history, and cultural change.

Digitalization changed this mode of accumulation. The official English page states that _Taiwan Panorama_ organized three hundred issues and tens of millions of characters into a digital database called "Treasures of Taiwan," offering various search functions; the official Chinese version notes that institutions such as the National Library, Academia Sinica, local cultural bureaus, and American universities are users.[^2] The value of this project is not just turning print into screen-readable files, but enabling articles scattered across different eras to be searched, compared, and reassembled.

> **When a database can search for the same place name, industry, or cultural theme from different eras, the magazine ceases to be merely a product delivered monthly; it becomes an infrastructure of Taiwanese memory that can be repeatedly studied.**

This also brings an editorial responsibility: the more searchable the data, the more important the source and temporal context. A report from the 1980s cannot be presented as today's reality without explanation. The terminology used at the time must also be placed within its original contextual understanding. Digital preservation does not erase historical distance; rather, it gives editors and readers a greater opportunity to see that distance.

## From Print Cover to Online Portal

Today’s _Taiwan Panorama_ website is no longer just an index of print magazines. The homepage and navigation bar divide content into categories such as "Global Perspective," "Artistic Context," "Industry Innovation," "Island Travel," "Diverse Groups," and "Visual Dialogue," with dedicated sections for cover stories, journals, events, and LGBTQ+ issues.[^3] This classification retains the curatorial feel of a magazine while utilizing the immediacy of the web, allowing readers to read by theme rather than flipping sequentially from one issue.

_Building of the National Library. Photo by Solomon203, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0; embedded from original file URL in this article. Image page: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_rigit_side_of_National_Central_Library.jpg)._

The website also allows "journals" and "features" to coexist. Journals maintain the integrity of a publishing cycle and cover editor. Features allow the same theme to extend across issues, forming an continuously updated reading entry point. The 2026 "Panorama 50 Old Photos Review" is a clear example: the official website reviews artistic reports from different eras through a visual dialogue format, featuring performances by Youren Shengu, Yunmen Dance Troupe, Contemporary Legend Theater, Timor Ancient Song Dance Ensemble, and Ruanj Theatre, while tagging photographers, original issue years, and article titles.[^4]

This review is not merely turning the past into a nostalgic exhibition. It allows today's readers to examine "how it was photographed, how it was written, and who wrote it" at that time. The dancers, theaters, and artists in the photos are both cultural scenes from that era and evidence of the gradual formation of Taiwan's artistic system. When the official website places original issue years alongside images, readers can see that culture did not appear suddenly, but accumulated through performances, individual teams, and long-term reporting.

## The Distance Between Official Publications and Real Taiwan

The publisher of _Taiwan Panorama_ is the Ministry of Foreign Affairs; this cannot be sidestepped. The footer of the official website clearly states that copyright belongs to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and _Panorama Pictorial_ Magazine Company. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and its overseas missions position it as an important window for the international community to understand Taiwan, stating that the bilingual print edition is distributed to over one hundred countries and regions.[^1] [^2] [^7] This identity gives it an international communication network that other magazines may not possess, but it also requires readers to know: it is not an independent media entity outside of a national institution.

Acknowledging its publishing background does not mean pre-judging every article. The more important question is how the magazine operates between its role as an official external publication and the complexity of Taiwanese society itself. Based on the scope listed by the official body, it presents not only tourism and culture but also addresses issues like environment, vulnerable groups, technology, and government policies.[^2] Based on the current website sections, it also places global observation, arts and culture, industry, local areas, diverse groups, and visuals side-by-side, ensuring that Taiwan is not presented through a single lens of policy or tourist attraction.[^3]

The website structure retains both the order of journal publishing and the entry points of online reading. Readers can return to the complete context of an issue via "Journal," or read thematically along sections like "Global Perspective" or "Island Travel"; "Visual Dialogue" places photography and historical memory on the same page. For a long-running publication, this design allows new articles to maintain immediacy while giving old articles a chance to be rediscovered.[^3]

The greatest danger for a publication introducing Taiwan to the world is to organize Taiwan into an image without cracks: food, scenery, technology, democracy—all are real, but if only showcaseable achievements remain, readers cannot see how those achievements were debated, corrected, and paid for. The value of a long-running magazine lies in its opportunity to place highlights back in time, put people back into systems, and situate local stories within larger social changes.

Therefore, the best way to read _Taiwan Panorama_ is not just to ask "What did it say about Taiwan?" but also to ask "Where did it choose to start talking from?" A cover, a human interest story, or a set of old photos all reveal what a certain era deemed worthy of being seen. Fifty years later, these choices themselves have become part of Taiwanese history.

## The Next Half-Century: From Preservation to Re-narration

In 2026, coinciding with its 50th anniversary, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs held the "Witnessing Transformation, Sharing the Light of Panorama" review event at the Taipei International Book Fair, turning past covers, photos, and reports into a navigable timeline of Taiwan. The official press release also pointed future directions toward digital transformation, AI technology, new media formats, and deeper international reach.[^1] This direction has two layers of meaning. The first layer is technical: how to make existing text, images, video, and databases easier to search, understand, and use. The second layer is narrative: as readers shift from flipping print to searching, recommending, short videos, and generative AI, how can Taiwan ensure that its story is not just seen, but also correctly contextualized?

AI can organize tens of millions of characters faster, but it cannot unilaterally decide which differences must be preserved. It can help find reports on the same person across different eras, but it cannot substitute for an editor's judgment regarding whether a title carried power relations at the time, nor can it automatically distinguish if a photo is news documentation, promotional composition, or later recollection. The true sign of mature digitization is not when data is moved online, but when the source, time, context, and limitations of that data are also preserved.

For _Taiwan Panorama_, what is most anticipated in the next stage may not be abandoning print entirely, but rather having the depth of print enhance the searchability of the website. A magazine can offer an editorial perspective of an era; a database allows readers to travel through different eras; and a multilingual website allows Taiwan's story to enter more people's lives. Only together do they form a complete public cultural role.

Half a century ago, _Panorama Pictorial_ tried to send Taiwan to the world. Today’s _Taiwan Panorama_ is still doing the same thing, but "the world" is no longer just several overseas distribution points; it is search engines, databases, social platforms, academic institutions, and every person searching for Taiwan. What it truly preserves is not just how Taiwan was introduced, but how Taiwan decided what image to be understood as through each renaming, edition change, translation, and digital transformation.
