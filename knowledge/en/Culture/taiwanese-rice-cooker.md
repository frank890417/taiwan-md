> **30-Second Overview**: The Taiwanese rice cooker started from Japanese technology but evolved based on Taiwan's local rice varieties, power supply, processing conditions, and steaming customs. Its classic nature is not just in the name Datong, but in how an outer pot allowed a family to entrust heat control, time, and a meal to a single switch.

In 1960, Datong Company introduced the first batch of TAC-6 rice cookers into Taiwanese kitchens. This was not a moment where an "invention" suddenly descended: two years earlier, Yongda Electric Industrial Company in Taipei had already brought the Crown automatic rice cooker to market. In the same era, Taiwan had over thirty manufacturers making rice cookers.[^1]

Looking back today, the white body, aluminum inner pot, and black knob in the center of the lid seem timeless. However, what it initially faced was not a sterile kitchen awaiting modernization, but rather coal stoves, kerosene burners, large hearths, and a city with an unstable power supply.[^2]

Therefore, the most significant contrast in the story of the Taiwanese rice cooker is this: **it looked like a replica of Japanese technology, but what truly remained was Taiwan's reordering of rice, electricity, time, and a meal.**

## Waiting for Electricity While the Kitchen Was Still Burning Coal

In 1950s Taiwanese households, cooking rice, boiling water, and preparing vegetables were often completed using the same solid fuel system. For the rice cooker to enter the home, it first had to prove that it wouldn't cause other household light bulbs to go out. At the time, electricity supply was insufficient, and peak load shedding and power conservation were daily occurrences. The typical household meter capacity was around 5 to 10 amperes; an 800-watt rice cooker operating simultaneously with an iron and lighting could trip the circuit.[^1]

This is why low-power models of the Taiwanese rice cooker appeared early on. Datong's ten-serving TAC-8 used 800 watts, while the TAC-8s was reduced to 600 watts. The capacity was the same, but the power consumption differed. That difference was hidden in the fine print of the product catalog, revealing the engineers' question: "When this family cooks rice, can they still turn on the lights?"[^1]

By the mid-1970s, the rice cooker had become a primary cooking system in urban areas. Subsequently, imported Japanese electric rice cookers and domestically produced models after 1983 appeared, but the rice cooker did not immediately disappear. Both types were still used by different households into the late 1980s.[^1]

## More Than Thirty Brands, Only One Name Remained

Equating "Datong" and "rice cooker" risks erasing other manufacturers from history. Industrial cultural heritage data from the National Museum of Science and Technology indicates that there were over thirty rice cooker manufacturers in Taiwan during the 1960s. They were mainly concentrated in Taipei, Taichung, and Tainan, many of which originally made kerosene stoves, electric fans, motors, transformers, or metal machinery.[^1] [^3]

Thus, the story of the Taiwanese rice cooker is not just the corporate history of Datong Company. It is also a history of processing industries transitioning into home appliances: lathes, stamping machines, aluminum molding, and heating elements were reassembled into an item that appeared on dowry lists and in new marital kitchens.[^1]

Datong's advantage lay in timing and production capacity. The company accumulated manufacturing foundations by producing electric fans after the war. During the first phase of construction projects, it also received US aid to establish meter factories and collaborated with Toshiba in Japan, acquiring equipment, hardware processing, and mass production methods.[^1]

This made "the first one" an imprecise question. Museum data records Datong's 1960 rice cooker as Taiwan's first, but technology history research reminds us that the Crown brand had already been on the market in 1958. A more meaningful question is: which product most successfully connected technology, price, distribution channels, and household customs?[^2] [^3]

## The Pot from Toshiba Required a Different Cooking Method for Taiwan

The ancestors of the Taiwanese rice cooker can be traced back to the ER-4 automatic electric cooker manufactured by Toshiba in Japan in 1955. Japanese models featured detachable inner pots, allowing one to cook rice while simultaneously making miso soup or heating curry. This was an answer suited for nuclear families and communal living.[^1] Hong Kong introduced a direct-heating automatic warming rice cooker from Panasonic in 1959, which later became another Asian rice cooking trajectory.[^1]

What Taiwanese engineers received was a different problem: local households were already accustomed to separating rice cooking from steaming. Therefore, companies like Datong developed upper steamers or upper inner pots, allowing steamed dishes to be stacked above the rice, and enabling single use when no steaming was required. This small accessory changed the logic of use: the rice cooker was not just a machine for cooking one pot of rice; it also took on the work of steaming, stewing, and warming food over the stove.[^1] [^4]

The white Datong rice cooker collected by the National Taiwan Museum of History consists of four parts: the outer pot, lid, inner pot, and power cord. The collection description notes that it can cook rice, make porridge, and handle steaming, stewing, braising, and marinating. This seemingly ordinary list explains why it was more like a Taiwanese kitchen than a single-function cooker: one outer pot accommodating different meals over time.[^2]

## 230 Degrees Was Not Showmanship, But for That Pot of Rice

Taiwan and Japan both eat rice, but the rice is not entirely the same. In the 1960s, Taiwan had new rice, old rice, _lanai mi_ (a type of local rice), and _penglai mi_ (another type of local rice) coexisting; different varieties absorbed water differently. Households also cared about the aroma produced by coal stoves and large hearths. Engineers therefore set the maximum temperature of the rice cooker to about 230 degrees Celsius, higher than the approximately 180 degrees Celsius of Japanese models.[^1]

The temperature setting was also related to the manufacturing site. The thickness and processing precision of early aluminum pots were not easy to standardize; thicker pots absorbed more heat and cooled down slower. Increasing the temperature was both making the rice cook properly and compensating for differences in materials and processing power. This meant that "localization" was no longer just a cultural slogan, but an adjustment made by an engineer facing an uneven aluminum pot when adjusting the knob and heating element.[^1] [^5]

The lid was not quiet from the start either. During technological transfer, the difference between Japanese 100 volts and Taiwanese 110 volts might have caused the steam to vibrate the lid. Later, as the lid became thinner, users still reported shaking and noise during cooking. Engineers added notches next to the lid to provide an outlet for saturated steam. Grooves at the bottom of the inner pot were also added based on usage.[^1]

These modifications do not appear in photos of the "Japanese prototype," but are embedded in the sounds Taiwanese people heard every day: the switch clicking, the steam venting, and the rice remaining in the pot.

## Dowries, Dormitories, and Overseas Suitcases

The Datong rice cooker later came in red and green. Records from the Taiwan Industrial Cultural Heritage website show that the red color is connected to the "dowry appliance" series, while green was released alongside a green refrigerator. The colors placed a cold home appliance into life events: marriage, starting a family, moving house, or taking a pot away from one's parents' home.[^3]

The National Taiwan Museum of History classifies this white rice cooker as "Artifact > Tableware > Cooking," manufactured in Taiwan, with its history dating from 1965 to the present. It is collected not because every rice cooker is rare, but because a specific pot was needed to validate everyday life.[^2]

This artifact also traveled away from Taiwan with Taiwanese people. An article from the National Taiwan University Institute of Chinese Studies compiled the significance of the Datong rice cooker in the lives of overseas Taiwanese and foreign residents in Taiwan: it allowed them to continue cooking familiar rice after moving, and became a small container representing Taiwan.[^6]

The company stated that it had been producing rice cookers and steamers since 1960, selling over 15 million units in sixty years. This is the cumulative figure provided by the company and cannot be directly equated with household penetration rates in Taiwan. But it at least shows how a product transformed from a market option into an item carried across generations and nations.[^7]

## The Datong Baby: The Child Who Saves Change Next to the Rice Cooker

If the rice cooker was the body that entered the home, the Datong Baby was the face it grew in the living room. Official Datong records state that in 1969 (ROC Year 58), customers who purchased NT$10,000 worth of Datong appliances could receive a corporate doll wearing a red helmet and holding an橄欖球 (rugby ball). At the time, resources were scarce, so this doll was often placed on the television or refrigerator, transitioning from a promotional giveaway to a household decoration.[^12]

This era is important. The rice cooker, television, refrigerator, and electric fan were collectively ushering households into an appliance-based life; companies not only sold products but also wanted the brand to be visible in places people saw every day when they returned home. Thus, the Datong Baby shared the same living scene as the rice cooker: one responsible for cooking, the other standing nearby watching the family grow.[^14] [^15]

Datong officially described the first batch of dolls as "meditating" in appearance, with a square logo on the chest, later changing to an open-eyed, rounder design. The coin slot on top, the founding year on the chest, the oversized head, and the large feet were not random decorations. The company explained these as corporate meanings representing frugality, contemplation, practice, and down-to-earthness.[^12]

The "Ceramic No. 71 Datong Baby" collected by the Ministry of Culture's National Cultural Memory Bank preserves this visual language as a specific cultural artifact. It wears a red and white helmet, holds a rugby ball in its left hand, rests its right arm on its hip, and has "71" printed in gold on its sportswear. This record also notes that Datong produced the first Datong Baby in 1969 to commemorate the company's 51st anniversary, with subsequent dolls marked by a chest number indicating the founding year.[^13]

The rugby ball was not the most intuitive choice for a Taiwanese corporate mascot today. The official explanation from Datong is that in 1969, rugby was popular in Taiwan, and the image of players cooperating to send the ball toward a target corresponded to the company's emphasis on integrity, thrift, and service spirit. This was the company's own interpretation of the design, not a complete sports history, but it turned the ball in the doll's hand from a prop into a historical clue.[^12]

The interesting aspect of the Datong Baby is that it was simultaneously a commodity, a piggy bank, a giveaway, and a family memory. An article in _The Economist_ described it as a ceramic doll common in Taiwanese homes, pointing out that the act of children putting in change made thrift not just a slogan but a repeatable daily ritual. This observation mirrors the keywords "saving," "piggy bank," and "toy" found in the Ministry of Culture's collection.[^13] [^15]

However, the Datong Baby cannot only be written as warm nostalgia. A compilation by StoryStudio shows that early versions like No. 51 gradually entered the collector's market due to differences in quantity, material, and preservation status. When an item originally given with an appliance began to be valued separately, it transformed from a marketing item into historical evidence of Taiwanese consumer society.[^14]

From this perspective, the Datong Baby adds another layer to the rice cooker story: the rice cooker brought heat into the kitchen, while the baby placed the brand image in the living room. When they appeared together, Datong was selling not just appliances, but an imagination of "reliable domestic goods and a better life" built within post-war families. This imagination would later be collected, redesigned, or carried in memory by overseas Taiwanese.[^6] [^15]

## "Smart" Arrived, But Why Is the Old Pot Still Used?

Electric cookers can precisely control temperature; pressure cookers shorten time; smart appliances can connect to phones. In contrast, a traditional rice cooker only has one main switch: water is added to the outer pot, and it automatically switches off after the moisture evaporates. The National Taiwan Museum of History even listed "simple structure, low price, durability" as reasons for its continued use.[^2]

But "only one switch" is not the whole answer. The rice cooker delegated complexity to the user: how many times to wash the rice, how much water to add to the outer pot, whether to place a steamer rack, and when to put in sweet potatoes. Its flexibility also came from family experience, not menu displays. Everyone could use the same pot to cook slightly different meals.

Post-war food studies at Political Science University interpret the family dining table from 1950 to 1970 within the context of modern life imagination. The rice cooker is located precisely at this turning point: it made cooking easier by breaking it down into several parallel steps, and it allowed family members not to constantly stand by the fire.[^8]

When the Taiwan Rice Culture Association compiled the history of rice, they also placed the Datong rice cooker in its long-selling position after the appearance of electric cookers. This reminds us that an artifact's lifespan is not determined solely by new technology. As long as it can hold a memory of food, old methods have a reason to remain.[^9]

## A Pot's Parts Correspond to an Entire System of Domestic Division of Labor

The museum's parts list breaks down the Datong rice cooker into the outer pot, lid, inner pot, and power cord. This breakdown is interesting because users often remember not a complete machine, but the feel of a specific part: the inner pot being taken to the sink for washing rice, the lid becoming hot in steam, and the power cord being stored behind the cabinet; the outer pot always remaining on the counter.[^2]

The shape of the outer pot was not neutral. Collection records describe it as a barrel-shaped vessel—wider at the top and narrower at the bottom—with three wooden feet at the base, and a lever switch near the front edge and a power cord socket on the side. These details made the rice cooker a household item that could be moved, wiped down, disassembled, and reassembled, rather than a closed device only to be sent back to the factory.[^10]

The inner pot was another scale entirely. Early collected inner pots were made of aluminum metal, featuring vertical walls, round rims, and two side handles for easy lifting when serving rice. Modern inner pots often use stainless steel, reflecting changes in material selection and health considerations. In other words, the rice cooker did not continue every material, but the daily action of "the inner pot being removable."[^11]

This structure—being detachable, washable, and replaceable—also explains why old rice cookers were often kept. As long as the outer pot could heat, the inner pot, lid, or power cord might find separate replacements. The artifact therefore did not need to be scrapped as a whole; it could continue functioning in the family as parts.

## Family Time Reorganized

The change brought by the rice cooker was not just replacing fire with electricity. It broke down cooking into several staggered time slots: first washing the rice, adding water, pressing the switch, and then preparing side dishes or doing housework. Only when the moisture evaporated and the switch clicked did the sound remind the user that cooking was complete. This ability to "leave the hearth" meant the kitchen no longer required one person to stand continuously by the stove.[^1] [^8]

But this convenience should not be written as a simple story of liberation. After the rice cooker entered the home, tasks like washing rice, cutting vegetables, plating, and cleaning the inner pot still needed to be done, often by women. A more accurate description is that the rice cooker reallocated the rhythm of work but did not automatically eliminate internal family division of labor. This is a social layer that cannot be ignored when reading about "kitchen electrification."[^8]

From a food perspective, the rice cooker placed rice at the core while expanding the scope of steaming, stewing, braising, and marinating. Steamed eggs, sweet potatoes, pork bone soup, braised pork, and leftovers could all be completed using the water and heat from the outer pot. These uses may not have been the most important functions in the original product manual, but they accumulated into a trust of "everything can go in" through user experience.[^2] [^4]

Therefore, the cultural value of the rice cooker is not just that it cooks rice. It concentrates typical Taiwanese cuisine, steaming, and family sharing onto one predictable heat source. The Taiwan Rice Culture Association's placement of Datong rice cookers as long-selling items after the appearance of electric cookers illustrates one thing: when an artifact becomes tied to staple food, family rhythm, and memory, replacement is not just about performance comparison.[^9]

## From "Datong" Back to Taiwan

If we only say "the Datong rice cooker accompanied Taiwanese people for half a century," the story would be too smooth. It was once a reference point for Japanese technology and competed with over thirty Taiwanese manufacturers. The reason it remained is not that Taiwanese people rejected more complex machines, but because this pot early on incorporated limited electricity, different rice varieties, steaming customs, and processing conditions into one outer pot.[^1] [^5]

Therefore, reducing the Taiwanese rice cooker to "one brand representing one ethnicity" is still too simple. It is more like a collaborative work completed by engineers, housewives, user feedback, aluminum pot factories, the power system, and rice itself. The name Datong became resonant in the end, but it cannot erase the hands that helped make the rice cooker Taiwanese.

The next time you press that black switch, notice what it doesn't tell you to cook. It only heats, waits, and then clicks when the moisture disappears. From 1960 to today, perhaps not a specific model has been preserved, but the habit of Taiwanese families entrusting a meal to time and believing there will be heat in the pot upon return.

To place this pot within a larger Taiwanese context, one can read the everyday life collections at the National Taiwan Museum of History or the industrial history materials preserved by the National Museum of Science and Technology.
