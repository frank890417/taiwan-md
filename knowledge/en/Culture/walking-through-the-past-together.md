# Walking Through the Past Together: To Move Forward, We Must First Know Where We Came From

> **30-Second Overview:** 《Walking Through the Past Together》 is a documentary series broadcast by TTV over four consecutive days from July 4 to 7, 1988. The four episodes span from the end of World War II to the passing of Chiang Ching-kuo, with cinematography and editing by photographer Chang Chao-tang. Since its founding in 1981, _Common Wealth Magazine_ has contemplated how Taiwan arrived at its current state and where it is headed; founder Yin Yun-peng chose to release this film on the eve of the opening of the Kuomintang's 13th National Congress. The narration uses the official language of that year, and Chang Chao-tang brought in over sixty people who personally lived through those forty years: Ji Cheng, who was once an adopted daughter; tenant farmers who faced annual rent hikes; and _rongmin_ (veterans) blasting roads in the mountains. Representatives from both the Kuomintang and the Democratic Progressive Party also speak in the same film. A year and a half later, a Chinese pro-democracy activist said he had seen this videotape in Beijing and remembered it as "memories with flesh and blood" [^1].

## On the Eve of July 22, Yin Yun-peng Chose This Moment

In the July 1, 1988 issue of _Common Wealth Magazine_ (Issue 86), founder Yin Yun-peng wrote a short article inviting readers to turn on their televisions at the beginning of the month. The opening sentence was: "On the eve of July 22, the Kuomintang's 13th National Congress is imminent." Chiang Ching-kuo had passed away six months prior. She described many policies at the time as "still murky and unclear," with power struggles erupting and a sense of unrest permeating society. _Common Wealth_ "specifically chose this moment" to release a television special[^2].

The special was titled 《一同走過從前》 (_Walking Through the Past Together_), covering the period from the end of World War II to early 1988. From July 4 to July 7, TTV aired four consecutive days, one episode per day. The final day happened to coincide with the opening of the 13th National Congress[^3]. At that time, there were only three television channels. The cinematography and editing were handled by photographer Chang Chao-tang, while the planning and writing were done by _Common Wealth_'s Sun Man-pin and Li Se[^2]. Across the four episodes, more than sixty named individuals spoke to the camera. Among them were Kuomintang Deputy Secretary-General Ma Ying-jeou, Democratic Progressive Party Chairperson Yao Chia-wen, tenant farmers from Taoyuan, and veterans from Dayuling[^4]. _Common Wealth_ stated it had "condensed forty years into four hours." Today, the four episodes on the official channel run approximately 47 to 50 minutes each, totaling just over three hours[^5].

In another section of the same magazine issue, Yin Yun-peng listed one by one the unanswered questions of that summer: Would there be a cabinet reshuffle? Would the ruling party undergo reform? Where was the policy toward mainland China headed? The final question was: "After achieving prosperity, where is the nation's goal?"[^2] To answer where we are going, we must first understand how we arrived at today. This film constitutes the first half of that journey. In her invitation, she stated that the special hoped to "find qualities and spirits worth cherishing" from our shared memory[^6]. Since its founding year, _Common Wealth_ has been asking this very same question.

## Driven Out of Military and Diplomacy, the World's Fate Rested on the Path of Economy

_Common Wealth_ was founded on June 1, 1981, nearly ten years after Taiwan's withdrawal from the United Nations and more than two years after the severance of diplomatic relations between the ROC and the United States[^7]. In October 2026, Yin Yun-peng met with Wu Che-yu, the founder of Taiwan.md, and spoke about why she started the magazine back then: "Military and diplomacy had already been driven out by everyone; we were like orphans. We could only rely on ourselves, and the only remaining path was the economy."[^8] She linked this issue to dignity, saying, "Everyone hopes to live with dignity." At that time, the traditional social hierarchy of scholars, farmers, artisans, and merchants still prevailed, with merchants ranked last. She said _Common Wealth_ therefore "used the term 'entrepreneur' to describe merchants," and gradually, everyone began to act like entrepreneurs[^8].

On January 13, 1988, Chiang Ching-kuo passed away. For its February issue (Issue 81), _Common Wealth_ produced a special retrospective series titled 〈Walking Through the Past, Returning to the Future〉. In the preface, Yin Yun-peng wrote that at the moment the guards carried the coffin out of the main hall of the Martyrs' Shrine, "in an instant, the entire nation fell silent." She followed this with a sentence that was later borrowed for a film: "Short-sightedness, pursuit of immediate gains, roughness and instability, lack of appreciation, lack of respect, and an inability to be grateful—all because we are too timid to look back, neglect our memories, and have no history."[^9]

The special series was handled by three young editors who "interviewed over a hundred people from various walks of life," flipping through books and visiting newspaper offices and school archives. Li Se, who wrote about the first twenty years and was born in 1956, reflected on that work: "History is actually written so vividly upon every person and in every hand." Lin Chao-wu, born in Taitung in 1959, who covered the third stage, traveled to Taipei for exams the year he graduated from junior high school; he took a highway bureau bus to Kaohsiung and then transferred to a train, a journey that took nearly fourteen hours. He said his understanding of Taiwan's economic development was "gradually pieced together and built up through these fragmented yet extremely intimate sensations"[^9].

Regarding the idea of bringing this written special series to the silver screen, Yin Yun-peng said it "only became possible after meeting Chang Chao-tang." Once they started working, Sun Man-pin and Li Se realized that television was far more complex than imagined: "A moving story that can be easily glossed over in two paragraphs in text requires a seamless coordination of vivid images, footage, and sound when put on screen"[^2].

## Chang Chao-tang Goes Looking for the Living

When filming the guide videos for _Walking Through the Past_ in 2016, Chang Chao-tang recalled that before Yin Yun-peng made this film, she had consulted many people, and Lin Hwai-min suggested looking for him. He agreed to try, knowing that "the difficulty lies in the fact that this is a massive undertaking with a huge scope." When he took on the project in 1987, he said he was forty-five or forty-six years old, and perhaps it was precisely that age that allowed him to understand how his previous experiences had come to be[^10]. He first had the written transcripts from _Common Wealth_, which he called "the skeleton of printed text"; forty years of news footage and visual archives were the muscle; what was ultimately missing was the blood: "It is dead data; how do you find living people?" He wanted people who lived through that era to speak about their feelings and experiences at the time[^10].

Finding old footage was another challenge. Yin Yun-peng wrote about historical scenes and camera angles, noting that "in Taiwan, where there is a general lack of historical sense and little emphasis on data preservation, finding them is particularly difficult"[^2]. After reading the transcripts, Chang Chao-tang felt that most of the content discussed construction, education, and the economy, with "less mention of culture," so he told Lin Hwai-min, "We need to add more cultural elements"[^11]. Editing became the part of the work he later found most rewarding; he compared filming to going to the market to buy groceries, while editing is like cooking in the kitchen. Relying on his past experience, he blended good ingredients into a delicious dish: "Editing can make a dead horse run like a living one"[^10].

When the first episode was finished and screened, a friend asked Yin Yun-peng: "Why are there so many sounds of military marches and songs?" She responded publicly in the magazine, stating that one reason was the difficulty of finding grassroots footage, and another was that during the first decade of Taiwan's post-war era, "survival came first, and military priority was paramount." At that time, military parades and songs "originally represented security and excitement"[^2]. The film's opening also acknowledges that it stands in an uncertain era, with the narrator asking: "Where exactly did we transition from, and where are we transitioning to?"[^12] In the following four episodes, most of those who answer this question are the people Chang Chao-tang sought out.

## Ten Years Post-War: From Having Nothing to Owning a Piece of One's Own Land

To describe the poverty of the first few years after the war, the film uses the diary of writer Chung Li-ho: when a pig died in the countryside of Pingtung, the grieving looks on people's faces were more sorrowful than if they had lost their own parents[^13]. Some families, struggling with poverty and many children, gave their daughters away to be raised by others; the young Chi Cheng was one of them. She remembers that when her adoptive mother went out to buy groceries, she feared Chi would sneak back home, so she would tie her hands and feet to a chair. Having learned to sing _kua-á-hí_ (Taiwanese opera) as a child, being tied up made her feel her life story was pitiful; she would cry while singing the songs of the "bitter female" roles[^14]. Chen Ting-nan from Yilan remembers that during 1950 and 1951, every meal at his home was mixed with sweet potato shreds, and only his grandfather, who was in poor health, had a bowl of white rice. The two brothers grew tired of eating sweet potato rice and would scramble for their grandfather's bowl at every meal; eventually, the grandfather simply left plenty of leftovers at each meal so they could share them[^15].

_Common Wealth Magazine "Walking Through the Past" Episode 1, interview footage and title card for Chi Cheng (09:01). © Common Wealth Magazine._

At the end of 1949, the Nationalist Government retreated to Taiwan. The following year, Chang Shao-kang's mother traveled to Taiwan alone by boat, while his father remained on the mainland. When the boat leaked at sea, everyone on board threw all their belongings overboard, leaving only a single thermos; everyone took turns using it to scoop water out. At that time, he was still in his mother's womb[^16]. During this same period, tenant farmers in the countryside were waiting for a piece of land of their own. In 1949, the July 375 Rent Reduction Act was implemented; in 1951, public land was released for distribution; and in 1953, the "Land to the Tiller" program began[^17]. Zhuang Lung-ying, a farmer from Taoyuan, said that previously, most fields belonged to the landlords, and lease agreements had to be renegotiated every year. Even if a landlord agreed to renew, they would increase the demand—if it was 4,000 catties this year, it would be 4,500 next year. After the land reforms, each household finally had its own field[^18].

Those who gained land were the local tenant farmers; the veterans from the mainland, meanwhile, headed into the mountains. Construction on the Central Cross-Island Highway began in 1956; the film notes that from that year onward, three to four thousand veterans worked on construction deep in the mountains[^19]. Chen Chi-cheng, a veteran from Dayuling, spoke of life at the construction site: they would eat breakfast and start work, then there would be blasting at noon—once it exploded, you couldn't be sure if people were still alive or not[^20]. An Tang-hsing, a veteran from Lishan, had followed his father into the fields since childhood, but upon reaching the mountains, he still grew vegetables and flowers. Speaking of his own plot of land, he said: "This piece of land can be passed down; it can pass down my roots"[^21].

The film focuses its conclusion on one of the veterans who settled in the mountains and on farms. Liu Pi-chia, from a farm in Hualien, did not marry until he was fifty-two; after being discharged from the military, he brought his large family to settle on the farm. More than twenty years ago, Chen Yao-chi, who was studying film at the University of California, chose him as the protagonist for a documentary as his graduation project. That film attracted considerable attention in Taipei's cultural circles, but according to the narration, Liu Pi-chia himself has never seen it to this day. The final shot lingers on him at seventy-two, riding a bicycle along a small path between fields[^22].

## During the Years When the Artillery Fire Hadn't Ceased, Taiwan Began Making Its Own Things

With fields and roads established, the war across the Strait had not yet ended. Facing shortages of supplies, a "hardship-overcoming movement" was launched within the military; writer Sima Zhong-yuan said this spirit continued in the military for decades—while other countries had long since sent their old C-119 aircraft to museums, Taiwan's were still flying[^23]. When Chao Shao-kang was in his third year of junior high school, he often saw reports in newspapers about batches of anti-communist soldiers heading to mainland China, only to be annihilated upon landing. He and several classmates wrote letters saying they didn't want to study anymore and wanted to join the military; a battalion commander replied, advising them that they were still young and should focus on their studies first[^24]. Air combat hero Ouyang Yi-fen said that when they went on missions, they often didn't have time to eat, grabbing a steamed bun before boarding the plane; during the 1956 air battles in the Taiwan Strait, he shot down two MiG fighters[^25].

On the evening of August 23, 1958, PLA artillery bombarded Kinmen heavily. Chang Kuang-chi, a Chinese-American photographer for NBC, visited Kinmen for interviews more than twenty times. He remembered photographing between bunkers and artillery positions when a row of shells landed on top of a bunker, making his heart nearly leap out of his mouth. During the time the reporter followed supply ships to engage in beach landings, an amphibious vehicle caught fire and sank halfway through; several Chinese and foreign reporters lost their lives. One Chinese reporter drifted at sea for thirty hours before being rescued; from then on, he refused to eat fish, saying that since the fish hadn't eaten him, he wouldn't eat them[^26].

During that same period, near Tainan Airport, an elementary school student named Chu Hsin would often run to his classroom window to watch planes returning. His teacher told him that whether they were ours or American, "they are actually all planes made by Americans, not made by our own people." From then on, he vowed to study aviation; many years later, he led a group of young engineers to design the Foetus car[^27]. He wasn't the only one who wanted to make things themselves. The government allocated US-aid cotton to textile mills like Tai Yuen; Tai Yuen's sales team later supported Yulon, founded by Yen Ching-ling in 1953. Wu Shun-wen said her husband believed that only the automotive industry could integrate all mechanical industries; on National Day in 1956, Yulon produced its first jeep[^28].

After the August 23 Artillery Battle, the Taiwan Strait gradually calmed. The government enacted the Statute for Investment to attract domestic and foreign industrialists to set up factories through tax exemptions and simplified procedures[^29]. As life became slightly more comfortable, Yang Chuan-kuang won a silver medal in the decathlon at the Rome Olympics in 1960. The film mentions that when Ling Bo came to Taiwan for 《Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai》, she was surrounded by fans at Songshan Airport, and it took forty bodyguards forty minutes to push her through the crowd[^30]. In 1962, TTV began broadcasting, marking Taiwan's entry into the television era. The program _Stars Gathering_ sang for fourteen and a half years from its debut until it stopped airing in 1977; the narration states plainly that the cancellation was to coordinate with the government's promotion of "purified songs." The emergence of television also gave a boost to the home appliance industry, laying the foundation for these manufacturers to later produce computer terminals and cathode-ray tubes[^31].

## US Aid Ends, UN Withdrawal Occurs: A Generation Looks Back at Their Land

In 1965, US aid ceased, and Taiwan had to find its own ways to earn foreign exchange. In 1966, the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone was established. In his early forties, Chang Yung-fa started from a single old ship and founded Evergreen Marine[^32]. Tens of thousands of children from rural villages left their fields for factories, earning money to send home[^33]. With the implementation of nine years of compulsory education in 1968, Lin Hui-huang, an elderly farmer from Changhua, saw two of his children become PhDs. His eldest son, Lin Shu-chun, was then an associate professor at National Tsing Hua University; he noted that the family could afford it thanks to low tuition fees, as for such a small farming household, tuition of NT$4,000 or NT$5,000 was a significant burden[^34].

In 1968, the Hongye Little League team from Taitung defeated Japan's Chofu Star team. In the summer of 1972, Taipei City's little league and Meihe Little League both won world championships within a single week; people stayed up late at night watching overseas broadcasts through firecrackers—it was rare good news during those years of successive diplomatic setbacks[^35]. The previous year, the Republic of China had withdrawn from the United Nations. Ou Chin-te, who was studying in the United States, saw Chou Shu-kai announce the withdrawal on television, followed immediately by the PRC representative taking the stage; he felt that they could no longer rely on the support of other countries "unless we devote ourselves to the construction of our own nation"[^36]. The film mentions that over 2,500 people returned to the country within a five-year period; Ou Chin-te was one of them, and upon returning after completing his studies, he joined the expressway engineering projects, traveling daily to survey terrain[^36].

Successive diplomatic setbacks also prompted university students to look back at themselves. Ma Ying-jeou said that after the movement to protect Taiwan's sovereignty, intellectuals—who previously spoke only of Saudi Arabia and closed their mouths regarding Camus—began to reflect on their own local culture; subsequently, literature, music, and dance all began to grow indigenous elements[^37]. When Wu Mei-yun told someone abroad that she was from Taiwan, the other person replied they had just seen _The King and I_; she had no choice but to explain she was not from Thailand. After returning home, she and her friends launched the English version of _Han-sheng_ (Sound) magazine[^38]. Lin Hwai-min said that for his generation of children growing up in Taiwan, rather than staying in the United States to criticize domestic affairs, it was better to return to their own place to do work[^39]. Seeing university students rushing abroad immediately upon graduation, Huang Chun-ming went on to film television series to introduce this land and its people[^40].

The cultural elements that Chang Chao-tang felt were missing during his script reading are filled in here. In the folk song segment, following Yang Hsien, there is a photo of Li Shuang-ze; the lyric/composition cards state that Chen Hsiu-hsi and Liang Ching-feng wrote the lyrics, and Li Shuang-ze composed the music. This is followed by about fifty seconds of lyric subtitles accompanied by images of paddy fields, rice transplanting, and cattle plowing; although the song title is not displayed in the film, this song is 〈Beautiful Island〉[^41]. Chang Chao-tang later said that at the time it was a banned song, but because it sang of Taiwan's beauty and its people, "it shouldn't have caused any real trouble, so I just used it without worrying at the time." He included it intentionally; folk songs are not just campus folk songs—this kind of song is what truly "relates to the entire land" [^11]. Both Chang Chao-tang and the introductions from _Common Wealth_ state that this was the first time the song was broadcast on television[^42].

## From Chiayi on the Night of the Severance of Diplomatic Ties to the Streets after Martial Law Ends

Following the death of Chiang Kai-shek in 1975[^43], the next decade saw a new generation of entrepreneurs shift from footwear and apparel toward electronics. Shih Chen-rong, who grew up in Lukang and helped his mother sell duck eggs as a child, founded Acer with friends; he stated that entrepreneurship required research and development to market one's own brand internationally. Meanwhile, Minister of Economic Affairs Sun Yun-suan decided the government would fund the Industrial Technology Research Institute (ITRI) to introduce integrated circuit technology, which was later transferred to the private sector to establish UMC[^44]. On December 16, 1978, the United States announced the severance of diplomatic ties with the Republic of China. That same night, Cloud Gate Dance Theatre was holding the premiere of _薪傳_ (_Legacy_) in Chiayi. Lin Hwai-min remembers there were six thousand spectators that night: "On stage, sweat and tears flew together; in the audience, eyes were red from crying and hands were red from clapping."[^45] Later, when a U.S. delegation arrived in Taiwan, crowds outside Songshan Airport rushed toward the vehicles at night; the film displays only a single line of text over this footage without any narration[^46].

_Episode 4 of "Walking Through the Past Together": A protest banner outside Songshan Airport the night the U.S. delegation arrived in Taiwan, December 1978 (16:50). © Common Wealth Magazine._

One year after the severance of ties, opposition figures held a march in Kaohsiung that escalated into the Formosa Incident, leading to the arrest and imprisonment of figures such as Huang Hsin-chieh, Yao Chia-wen, and Lu Hsiu-lien[^47]. In 1984, Sun Yun-suan stepped down from his position as Premier due to illness; subsequently, the Shintien financial scandal led to the downfall of two consecutive Finance Ministers[^48]. In 1987, the government lifted martial law and ended the ban on political parties. On December 25 of that year, Chiang Ching-kuo attended the 40th Anniversary of Constitutional Reform in a wheelchair; the narration notes he listened silently to the protests from the Democratic Progressive Party. Eighteen days later, he passed away, and Lee Teng-hui succeeded him as President in accordance with the Constitution[^49]. Forty years pass here, reaching 1988, the year the film begins.

![An elderly Chiang Ching-kuo wearing glasses sits in a wheelchair being pushed into a venue; an officer in formal attire stands to his right; the subtitle below reads "He ignored the doctors' advice"](/article-images/culture/yitong-ep4-chiang-ching-kuo-wheelchair-1988.jpg)
_Episode 4 of "Walking Through the Past Together": Chiang Ching-kuo attending the 40th Anniversary of Constitutional Reform in a wheelchair on December 25, 1987 (27:36). © Common Wealth Magazine._

At this point, the film turns to discuss the price of success[^50]. The Zhuang family from Taoyuan, who had waited for their own farmland years ago, reappear on screen; this time, they speak of how young people are all flocking to factories, earning less than NT$10,000 a month from farming, and hope the government does not focus solely on industry while leaving agriculture so far behind[^50]. Huang Chun-ming compares academic advancement to a factory production line, where upon graduation, students are "labeled" with brands like NTU, NCCU, or Fu Jen[^51]. Chen Ting-nan, who used to scramble for white rice from his grandfather as a child, is now the Magistrate of Yilan County; he says the people on the streets are being somewhat excessive and that the government's response was also slightly hypersensitive, noting that for society to progress, one must endure this growing pain[^52].

Yin Yun-peng previously wrote a list of these "side effects of success" in the July issue, beginning with: "Many of today's problems are, in fact, the side effects of past successes." She wrote all the way from the shrinking size of cultivated land and the pollution of mountains and rivers to the idea that while the people have become wealthy, the government has become poor; her final point was, "Freedom and democracy, but lacking a clear national goal."[^2] The question she asked in that same issue returns here.

## On the Day of the Thirteenth Central Election, TTV Finished Broadcasting Episode 4

When this episode aired on Monday, July 4, 1988, martial law had been lifted for nearly a year, and the press ban had been removed at the beginning of the year. The only three wireless television stations still belonged to the party, the government, and the military[^53]. The four consecutive days of broadcasting coincided exactly with the eve of the first Kuomintang (KMT) National Congress following the death of Chiang Ching-kuo; on the day episode four aired, the Thirteenth Central Election was opening[^3]. The narration used the terminology of that year; regarding the February 28 Incident, there was only one sentence: "Due to the crackdown on smuggled cigarettes, the unfortunate February 28 Incident was triggered, and the military and police suppressed the riots."[^54] When discussing the _Meili Dao_ (Beautiful Island) Incident, the narrator stated that opposition crowds clashed with the military and police during a parade in Kaohsiung, then proceeded to read out the names of those arrested[^47].

![A close-up of a young girl wearing a hat and straight bangs, raising her hand in a salute with a focused expression](/article-images/culture/yitong-ep4-salute-1988.jpg)
_The end of episode four of "Walking Through the Past." After the narrator concludes and the National Flag Song begins to play, a shot shows a young girl saluting (46:44). © Common Wealth Magazine._

The same film gave these named individuals a platform to speak. Yao Chia-wen, whom the narrator had just mentioned, was by then the chairperson of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP); in the film, he discusses how he worked his way through school at the Directorate General of Telecommunications during his youth, observing the step-by-step commercialization of rural areas through telegraph content[^55]. Lu Hsiu-lien speaks about her advocacy for new feminism starting from the second half of 1971[^56]. Huang Hsin-chieh appears during the segment on the 1969 supplemental elections; Kang Ning-hsiang discusses his 1972 election as a legislator under an opposition banner, entering a Congress dominated by veteran members where youth and courage were his primary assets[^57]. On the KMT side, the film introduces Judicial Yuan President Lin Yang-kang, noting that when he first graduated from National Taiwan University, he often walked several hours along rural paths to hold village meetings; former labor union director Lee Huan and KMT Deputy Secretary-General Ma Ying-jeou also appear in the film[^58].

In her short essay in the July issue, Yin Yun-peng wrote that this special feature was _Common Wealth_'s way of giving back to society for its seventh anniversary, saying, "We welcome your viewing, criticism, and guidance"[^59]. Criticism arrived quickly. A commentator from _Mi Zuo_ published an article in the supplement of the _Liberty Times_ that same month, arguing that because the film looked back over forty years centered on the KMT regime's arrival in Taiwan, the _Meili Dao_ Incident was downplayed and lacked political judgment; furthermore, since the broadcast coincided with the eve of the Thirteenth Central Election, it caused many to look askance and make associations. The same article noted that prominent members of the DPP were given opportunities to appear and speak on camera[^60].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The narrator's few sentences and the sixty-plus faces in the film all came from the same editorial office. _Common Wealth_ and Chang Chao-tang used the official language of 1988 to draw conclusions about forty years, yet they also handed the camera to tenant farmers, _rongmin_ (veterans), and the opposition figures whose names the narrator had just read. Both were their choices; if one only praises one aspect and blames the other on "the times," then one has only understood half of this film.

## The Videotape Wan Chun-nan Watched in Beijing

A year and a half after the film finished airing, in February 1990, _Common Wealth Magazine_ held a symposium titled 〈Eastern Europe is Changing, Will Mainland China Change?〉. Hosted by Yin Yun-peng, the event was attended by Kang Ning-hsiang and Ma Ying-jeou. Wan Chun-nan, who had founded the Sitong Company in Beijing and was then serving as the Secretary-General of the Democracy in China Federation (an overseas pro-democracy organization), was present. When discussing whether Taiwan's experience would be useful to mainland China, he first mentioned this videotape, adding, "I watched it in Beijing," and stating, "This is the best Taiwan experience" [^1].

He continued candidly: "To be honest, many aspects of the Taiwan experience left no impression on me." What stayed with him was the "flesh-and-blood memories" found in this _Walking Through the Past Together_ tape; he noted that the impact of such memories "is far greater than the utility of abstract things" [^1]. Kang Ning-hsiang chimed in on the spot, saying that because this film had influenced Mr. Wan, it served as a concrete manifestation of the Taiwan experience [^1].

The Taiwan experience has also encountered resistance across the Strait: in early January 1989, the Chinese University of Hong Kong held a seminar on cross-strait economic development experiences. _Vision_ magazine recorded that while Taiwanese scholar Yu Tsung-hsien repeatedly emphasized the "fighting spirit" of small and medium-sized enterprises, mainland scholars could only respond: "We understand, but the situation in mainland China is different..." [^61]. Through that same videotape, what Wan Chun-nan remembered were the living people Chang Chao-tang had sought out back then. The questions _Common Wealth_ asked on behalf of Taiwan—how it reached its current state—ended up in the hands of someone currently asking if mainland China would change.

_Common Wealth_ has later stated multiple times that this tape reached Deng Xiaoping, though the phrasing has varied each time. A 2010 profile of Yin Yun-peng wrote that Deng Xiaoping "once called upon all important officials to watch it together" [^62]; a 2011 introduction for a new edition of the book stated that "Deng Xiaoping, having obtained the documentary tape through various channels, watched it alongside high-ranking Zhongnanhai officials" [^63]; and the website marking the 40th anniversary of _Common Wealth_ in 2021 wrote that the film "garnered attention from high-ranking officials on the other side, and even from the top leader, Deng Xiaoping" [^64].

Yin Yun-peng's own recollection is even more extensive. During a meeting in October 2026, she mentioned that after the film aired on television, it was "taken by the Ministry of National Defense for a period" to be used in recruit training [^8]. She also recalled that the four episodes of the videotape were handed over to someone in Singapore, then passed to Wang Tao-han. Wang summoned key Shanghai officials to watch it together, wanting to understand how the Kuomintang, after losing the war and retreating to a small island, could still rise again. Subsequently, another copy was sent to Deng Xiaoping, who watched it with officials from the Taiwan Affairs Office [^8].

## Those Final Five Minutes, with Nearly Twenty People Speaking in Turn

In April 2016, _Common Wealth_ uploaded the four episodes to its official YouTube channel and invited Chang Chao-tang to record three introductory guides. By October 2026, the first episode had reached over 180,000 views, while the fourth exceeded 370,000[^65]. In his third guide, Chang told young people who wanted to make films that equipment is not the priority; what matters is the subject matter—having a concern for one's own land is what leads one back to study one's own history[^66]. He passed away on April 2, 2024, at the age of 81[^67]; those people he brought together are still in the video, speaking one by one.

As the film nears its end, the narrator borrows a sentence from Yin Yun-peng's preface in the February issue, attributing society's ailments to a refusal to look back and a lack of history; then asks the audience what is worth cherishing when looking back at the past, and what requires more effort when looking toward the future[^68]. For the next five minutes, there is no narration. Instead, nearly twenty interviewees answer one after another—ranging from former directors of the Food Administration and chairpersons of the Democratic Progressive Party to air war heroes and general managers of foreign companies[^69].

Lee Lien-chun, a former director of the Food Administration in his eighties, says we must be united; Sun Yun-suan says equality and equitable distribution of wealth are the two most successful policy achievements; Kang Ning-hsiang says we must cherish this land of Taiwan because twenty million people have invested forty years of effort into it. Wei Yung, former chairman of the Research, Development, and Evaluation Commission, repeats three times that there must be no suppression—no suppression of any person, any group, or any opinion; Yao Chia-wen, chairperson of the Democratic Progressive Party, says everyone should open their eyes to society to jointly design a new blueprint. Wang Chi-mei remembers that back then, there was neither money nor technology, yet they still had to forge a path; Ouyang Yi-fen says today's prosperity came at a price and did not simply fall from the sky; Xu Zhuoyun says we can no longer hope for a "patriarch" to come and tell everyone how to maintain order[^69].

On July 7, 1988, the Shih San Quan (Thirteen All) complex opened; on that day, these words finished broadcasting on TTV. That summer, Yin Yun-peng asked: after becoming wealthy, what is the nation's goal? In those final five minutes, Wu Mei-yun, the founder of _Han Sheng_, spoke about how to find direction by first looking back: "As we move forward, we must know how we came to be"[^70].
