In 2024, the national average per-person Western medicine outpatient care continuity (COCI) was 0.405; the data scope excludes dentistry and Chinese medicine.[^1]

This figure is close to the 0.400 recorded in 2019, i.e., the pre-pandemic level.[^1]

## Zero Point Four Zero Five

Care continuity is an index ranging from 0 to 1: 1 means all of a patient's visits are at the same institution; the closer to 0, the more dispersed.[^1]

0.405 means that, on average, patients' outpatient visits are spread across multiple institutions.

## Five-Year Curve

Laying out the five years makes the shape clear.

2020: 0.422; 2021: 0.436; 2022: 0.424; 2023: 0.403; 2024: 0.405.[^1]

```tw-line
Average per-person outpatient care continuity (COCI)
2020 | 0.422 | Early pandemic
2021 | 0.436 | Community spread begins, five-year high
2022 | 0.424 | —
2023 | 0.403 | Alert level lowered
2024 | 0.405 | Close to 2019's 0.400
Source: NHIA "2024 National Health Insurance Monitoring Indicator Results Report" Indicator 2.3
```

The peak occurred in 2021, the year the pandemic entered the community-spread phase in mid-May; the trough was 2023, the year the alert level was lowered.[^1]

## How It Is Calculated

This indicator adopts the definition developed by Bice and Boxerman in 1977: first calculate the distribution of each patient's visit counts across institutions, then convert to a 0-to-1 value, and finally take the national average.[^1]

Before calculation, two categories of extreme values are excluded: patients with three or fewer attributed visits per year, and those with 100 or more, to prevent outliers from skewing the index.[^1]

The design focuses on the "patient" rather than the "institution": it asks not how many patients a hospital saw, but how many institutions each patient spread their visits across.

## Why the Pandemic Pushed It Up

The rise to 0.436 in 2021, the NHIA explained, reflected changes in public care-seeking behavior driven by pandemic policy; after community spread began in mid-May 2021, the Central Epidemic Command Center required hospitals to reduce load.[^1]

The NHIA's analysis further speculated that the public may have voluntarily deferred care or chosen to stick with a single institution to lower infection risk. This is a speculative interpretation, not an experimental finding.[^1]

In other words, the 2021 peak, the report speculates, reflects patients' own choice to concentrate on a single institution, not necessarily an improvement in care continuity.

## Decline After Downgrade

After the alert level was lowered on May 1, 2023, statistics show the 2024 full-year data has returned to near the pre-pandemic (2019) level.[^1]

The report's assessment of the five-year trend is "no obvious change"—merely a slight rise during the pandemic, then a return to near pre-pandemic levels after normalization.

## Continuity and Outcomes

Why care about this index? The report cites domestic and international studies indicating that high-quality, continuous care leads to better outcomes, higher patient satisfaction, and lower medical costs; domestic research further shows that patients with higher continuity have significantly fewer emergency visits and hospitalizations, and a lower probability of "avoidable hospitalizations."[^1]

These are correlational studies, not causal experiments. But they point in a direction: when a patient is consistently cared for by the same physician or team, medical history and medications are easier to track, and duplicate tests and drug interactions are more readily detected.

## How Policy Uses It

The NHIA's improvement strategy places continuity within the push for tiered healthcare and the family physician system: continue promoting tiered healthcare, enhance primary care capacity, expand the Family Physician Integrated Care Program, implement two-way referrals and chronic disease co-management between community medical groups and partner hospitals, and encourage the public to seek a family physician first, with referral by the family physician when necessary. The NHIA's analysis further recommends continued monitoring of this indicator and deeper investigation into the disease patterns and care-seeking behaviors of patients with low continuity.[^1]

In other words, the policy significance of the 0.405 figure is not to forbid patients from switching hospitals, but to make "having a regular doctor who knows your history" an easy option.

System integration is moving in this direction. The same report, in the diabetes indicators (2.2, 3.2), notes that the NHIA plans from 2026 onward to integrate the Western medicine primary care pay-for-performance scheme's diabetes, early chronic kidney disease, and diabetes with chronic kidney disease modules with the family physician program, so that duplicate enrollees are uniformly managed by the family physician program—preventing the same patient from being tracked by two separate systems.[^1]

Two reminders when reading this number. First, it is a national average that obscures variation among patients: some see a single physician consistently, others visit five institutions in a year, yet both average to 0.4. Second, it covers only Western medicine outpatient cases, so it cannot directly infer "overall care continuity."

From the five-year curve, the difficulty is also clear: continuity can be pushed up by external forces (the pandemic), and automatically falls back after normalization. Making it rise sustainably depends not on a one-off campaign, but on enabling primary care to truly absorb patients.
