> **30-Second Overview:** The average insured wage for Labor Insurance is NT$36,095, while the average total wage from the Directorate General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS) is NT$63,386—both are official figures. The gap arises from four factors: non-recurring payments like year-end bonuses are not included in insured wages; the NT$45,800 cap has not been adjusted since May 2016; the Labor Insurance roster includes 1.79 million occupational union members; and illegal high-salary underreporting. Employment Insurance data shows that more than 4 out of 10 insured persons are stuck at the ceiling, while 13% fall into the lowest tier.

In the same year in Taiwan, the government released two different "average wages."

One is the average insured wage for Labor Insurance: NT$36,095. [^1] The other is the average total monthly wage per employed person from the DGBAS: NT$63,386. [^3]

Both are official figures, with a gap of NT$27,291. Because these two statistical measures track different things, they must be analyzed separately.

## Two Averages

At the end of December 2025, the total number of people insured under Labor Insurance in Taiwan was 10,481,213, with an average insured wage of NT$36,095. [^1]

In the same year, the average number of employed persons in the industrial and service sectors reported by the DGBAS was 8.523 million, with an average annual total wage per person of NT$760,632, which converts to NT$63,386 per month. [^3]

The timeframes also differ. Labor Insurance is a snapshot of the month ending in December, whereas the DGBAS figure is an annual average, and the annual total wage includes year-end bonuses.

## How Insured Wages are Reported

Article 14 of the Labor Insurance Act stipulates that the monthly insured wage is the amount reported to the Bureau of Labor Insurance by the employer "based on the total monthly wage of the insured person, in accordance with the Insured Wage Grading Table." [^4] Article 27 of the Enforcement Rules adds that the total monthly wage "shall be based on the wages stipulated in Article 2, Subparagraph 3 of the Labor Standards Act"; for those with irregular monthly income, it is calculated as the average of the most recent three months. [^5]

Under Article 2, Subparagraph 3 of the Labor Standards Act, "wages" include base salary as well as "bonuses, allowances, and any other regular payments under any name." [^6] Therefore, fixed monthly payments such as perfect attendance bonuses, meal allowances, and fixed overtime pay should all be included in the insured wage. What is excluded are non-recurring payments, such as year-end bonuses and festival bonuses.

Reporting only the base salary is a common practice, but it is not what the law requires. Article 40 of the Employment Insurance Act states that the monthly insured wage for employment insurance shall follow the Labor Insurance Act, meaning these rules govern both systems. [^7]

## The Floor Rises, the Ceiling Stays Put

In the Insured Wage Grading Table implemented on January 1, 2025, Tier 1 is for wages below NT$28,590, resulting in a monthly insured wage of NT$28,590. The highest tier is for wages above NT$43,901, with a monthly insured wage of NT$45,800; the table contains 12 tiers in total. [^2]

This NT$45,800 cap was raised from NT$43,900 on May 1, 2016. At the time, the Ministry of Labor stated that the NT$43,900 level "had not been adjusted for over nine years" and expected 2.13 million insured persons to fall into this new tier. [^8] That version of the grading table had a Tier 1 of NT$20,008 and contained 20 tiers in total. [^9]

In the decade since, Tier 1 has risen alongside the minimum wage: it was NT$29,500 in 2026 and NT$30,900 in 2027. However, the highest tier has remained stuck at NT$45,800. As the floor is pushed up while the ceiling remains stationary, the number of intermediate tiers has shrunk from 20 to just 10 in 2027. [^9][^10]

## Forty Percent Stuck at the Ceiling

The Bureau of Labor Insurance's statistical monthly report includes a table listing the number of persons insured under Employment Insurance by monthly insured wage tier. At the end of December 2025, there were 7,450,861 persons insured under employment insurance. [^11]

Of these, 3,182,602 people had a monthly insured wage of NT$45,800, accounting for 42.7%. The highest tier has become the most populous tier. [^11]

The total monthly wages of these individuals are at least NT$43,901—they could be NT$45,000 or they could be NT$200,000—but in the statistical table, they all appear as NT$45,800. This cutoff is legal; it is a feature of the grading table's design. It also drags down the average insured wage: any salary exceeding NT$45,800 is completely invisible in the NT$36,095 average.

## The 13% on the Floor

At the other end of the same table, 967,760 people (13.0%) have a monthly insured wage of NT$28,590. Below that, various tiers for wages below NT$27,600 account for 654,251 people (8.8%); these tiers are reserved for part-time workers, vocational trainees, and sheltered employment participants. [^11][^2]

NT$28,590 is the minimum wage for 2025. Some people in this tier truly earn the minimum wage. Others might earn a monthly salary of NT$40,000, but their employers only report them at the lowest tier—this is what is commonly known as high-salary underreporting. The statistical table places both types of people in the same bracket, making it impossible to distinguish between them.

High-salary underreporting is illegal. Article 14-1 of the Labor Insurance Act stipulates that if a report is inaccurate, the Bureau of Labor Insurance may adjust it to the equivalent level within the same industry; "if the adjusted insured wage does not match the actual wage, the actual wage shall prevail." [^12] Article 72, Paragraph 3 provides that employers who report insured wages by "reporting more as less or less as more" shall be fined four times the amount of the underreported insurance premium, and any losses suffered by workers must be compensated by the employer. [^13]

The Bureau of Labor Insurance's primary tool for checking underreporting is comparing data with the Ministry of Finance's wage and income records. In the 2016 batch, the Bureau used 2015 tax data to unilaterally raise the Labor and Employment Insurance wages for over 40,000 employers and 150,000 individuals. The 2019 batch used 2018 data, with an estimated unilateral adjustment for over 30,000 employers and 120,000 individuals. [^14][^15] Each batch involves hundreds of thousands of people, and this only covers the portion that could be identified through tax data comparison and was subsequently adjusted.

## The Ceiling Cutoff and Underreporting Push in the Same Direction

The average insured wage of NT$36,095 is NT$2,311 lower than the median regular wage reported by the DGBAS (NT$38,406). [^1][^3] While the different populations of these two statistics mean the gap cannot be attributed to a single cause, at least three forces are pushing the figure downward.

The ceiling cuts off the right tail, leaving 40% of people with only NT$45,800. Underreporting pushes a portion of people from the middle down to the floor. The third force is the members of occupational unions in the Labor Insurance roster, which we will discuss next.

## Occupational Unions and Commercial Firms

The 8.523 million people calculated by the DGBAS are all employed workers, but the 10.48 million in Labor Insurance also include members of occupational unions, Class A Fishermen's Associations, and voluntary insured persons. Among them, the 1.79 million occupational union members have an average wage of only NT$31,679, which is nearly NT$5,000 lower than employees in commercial firms. This is a difference in population composition, which is distinct from the issues of the ceiling and underreporting. [^1][^3]

## The Gap Between Industries

High-salary industries are most affected by the ceiling cutoff, making the gap between industries appear smaller than it actually is. The highest insured wage is in the electricity and gas supply industry at an average of NT$42,976, while the lowest is in the accommodation and food services industry at NT$29,465—a difference of only NT$13,512. Naturally, there are people in the electricity and gas industry with monthly salaries exceeding NT$45,800, but that excess is invisible in the statistics. [^1]

## Not Every Insurance System Caps at NT$45,800

NT$45,800 is only the cap for Labor and Employment Insurance. Other systems that also report based on monthly wages have much higher ceilings.

| System                          | 2025 Highest Tier | Basis                                                    |
| :------------------------------ | :---------------- | :------------------------------------------------------- |
| Labor Insurance                 | NT$45,800         | Labor Insurance Insured Wage Grading Table [^2]          |
| Employment Insurance            | NT$45,800         | Follows Labor Insurance Act [^7][^11]                    |
| Occupational Accident Insurance | NT$72,800         | Occupational Accident Insurance Wage Grading Table [^16] |
| National Health Insurance       | NT$313,000        | NHI Insured Amount Grading Table [^17]                   |
| Labor Pension                   | NT$150,000        | Labor Pension Monthly Contribution Grading Table [^18]   |

In January 2025, National Health Insurance raised its highest tier from NT$219,500 to NT$313,000. [^17] The caps for Labor and Employment Insurance will still be NT$45,800 even in the 2027 grading tables. [^10]

To see the actual distribution of wages, the monthly contribution wage for Labor Pensions is more useful than the Labor Insurance insured wage: its cutoff point is at NT$150,000, which is above the salaries of most employees. However, since Labor Pension contributions are also reported by employers, the problem of underreporting has not disappeared.

## What Can This Number Be Used For?

The purpose of the insured wage is to calculate premiums and benefits. Article 19 of the Labor Insurance Act stipulates that cash benefits are calculated based on the average monthly insured wage; for old-age pensions, it takes the average of up to the highest 60 months during the insurance period. [^19] The NT$45,800 cap simultaneously suppresses both the premiums and the benefits for high earners, while those who are underreported pay fewer premiums and subsequently receive lower future benefits.

This also makes the insured wage a lens through which to observe the system. In December 2024, the average insured wage was NT$35,347; in December 2025, it was NT$36,095, an annual increase of 2.1%. [^1] During the same period, the DGBAS reported that the average total wage grew by 3.91% annually, and the average regular wage grew by 3.09%. [^3]

With the ceiling unmoved for a decade and the floor pushed up every year, an increasing portion of wage growth falls into places where Labor Insurance cannot see it. Part of the distance between these two averages is a result of institutional design, and part is a result of illegal reporting. Anyone citing the NT$36,095 figure should at least know that it is being pushed down by both forces. Whether the cap should be adjusted is a matter of Labor Insurance financial stability and income replacement rates; this article does not answer that.
