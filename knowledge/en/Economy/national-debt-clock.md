As of November 6, 2026, the central government's outstanding debt with maturity of one year or more was NT$5.8364 trillion, and debt with maturity under one year was NT$35 billion.[^1]

The third figure on the same announcement: average per capita debt burden of NT$253,000.[^1]

## The November Numbers

The National Treasury Administration updates the "National Debt Clock" every month, publishing three items without fail: debt of one year or more, debt under one year, and average per capita burden.[^1]

The early November 2026 version: NT$5.8364 trillion, NT$35 billion, NT$253,000.[^1]

```tw-stack
National Debt Clock as of November 6, 2026
Item | Value | Note
Debt ≥ 1 year | NT$5.8364 trillion | Primary debt
Debt < 1 year | NT$35 billion | Short-term cash management
Average per capita burden | NT$253,000 | Debt balance ÷ population
Source: Ministry of Finance National Treasury Administration "Latest Debt Information"
```

## NT$253,000 Per Person Is Not Your Personal Debt

This figure is most commonly misread as "every citizen carries NT$250,000 of debt." The actual algorithm is simple: debt balance divided by population.[^1]

It is not personal debt, nor is it allocated to individuals. Government debt exists in the form of bonds and loans; when due, it can be rolled over. What truly affects public finance is interest expenditure and creditworthiness, not the result of this division.

Media explainer articles also flag the same point: average per capita burden is a communication indicator, not an accounting-level personal liability.[^3]

## The 2026 Curve

Lay out the 2026 monthly series and a curve emerges — spiking first, then falling back.[^2]

January: debt ≥ 1 year NT$5.8979 trillion, short-term debt NT$140 billion, per capita NT$259,000.

February: NT$5.9929 trillion, NT$270 billion, NT$269,000.

April: NT$5.9929 trillion, short-term debt NT$360 billion, per capita NT$273,000 — the year's peak.

June: NT$5.9064 trillion, NT$195 billion, NT$262,000.

September: NT$5.8664 trillion, NT$100 billion, NT$257,000.

Early November: NT$5.8364 trillion, NT$35 billion, NT$253,000.[^1][^2]


Short-term debt fluctuates far more than long-term debt: from NT$140 billion in January, rising to NT$360 billion in April, then dropping to NT$35 billion in November.[^2]

This segment is key to reading the Debt Clock. Short-term debt is a cash-management tool; it rises in the first half of the year due to expenditure rhythm, then falls as tax revenue flows in. Adding short-term and long-term debt produces a highly volatile number, but that number does not represent a deterioration of long-term debt.

## The Ten-Year Long-Term Trend

Extend the horizon to ten years: debt ≥ 1 year grew from NT$5.3129 trillion at end-2015 to NT$5.858 trillion at end-2024.[^2]

A ten-year increase of roughly NT$545.1 billion, averaging about NT$54.5 billion per year.

Average per capita burden rose from NT$232,000 to NT$252,000 in 2024 and NT$253,000 in 2025.[^2]

This line is not straight: 2020 to 2021 saw a larger jump (NT$5.4778 trillion to NT$5.6948 trillion), then the pace eased.

## Short-Term Debt Seasonality

Observing short-term debt reveals it oscillates in nearly the same band every year.

End-2015: NT$134 billion; end-2017: NT$43 billion; end-2020: NT$110 billion; end-2023 and end-2024: both NT$30 billion.[^2]

In 2026, short-term debt surged to NT$360 billion in April — a rare high in the past decade — but returned to NT$35 billion by November.[^1][^2]

This is why the Debt Clock cannot be read from a single month: within the same fiscal year, the highest and lowest points can differ by a factor of ten.

## Two Definitions — Do Not Mix Them

The Ministry of Finance's tables are clear: debt ≥ 1 year and short-term debt occupy separate columns; average per capita burden counts only the former.[^1][^2]

Adding both and dividing by population yields a higher number; that number exists but represents a different definition, usually called "debt balance including short-term debt."

Likewise, the internationally common "debt-to-GDP" ratio does not appear on this table. Three algorithms answer three different questions; mixing them defocuses the discussion.

## How to Read This Number

First, watch the trend, not a single month. The 2026 curve spiked in the first half, fell in the second; looking only at April suggests deterioration, only at November suggests improvement.

Second, watch the long term, not the short term. A ten-year increase of NT$545.1 billion is the true axis of this line.

Third, watch the definition, not the headline. NT$253,000 is "debt ≥ 1 year ÷ population" — not personal debt, and not the figure including short-term debt.

The Debt Clock's value lies in its monthly updates, fixed definition, and traceable source tables. It does not answer "is this too much" — that requires debt-to-GDP, interest burden, and tax structure together.[^1][^2]
