In 2025, total national electricity generation was 289.7 billion kWh. Of this, gas-fired power accounted for 47.7%, coal 35.3%, renewables 13.3%, and nuclear 1.1%. [^1]

In the same year, liquefied natural gas (LNG) imports totaled 23.1 million metric tons; the average load factor of the three receiving terminals over the past five years was approximately 117%. [^1]

## Proportion of the Four Power Sources

Gas is the largest single power source, accounting for nearly half; coal follows at approximately one-third; renewables stand at 13.3%, and nuclear at 1.1%. [^1]

```tw-stack
2025 National Electricity Generation Structure
Item | Proportion | Description
Gas | 47.7% | Largest single power source
Coal | 35.3% | Second largest
Renewables | 13.3% | Increased by approx. 4.5 billion kWh compared to 2024
Nuclear | 1.1% | —
Source: Ministry of Environment Energy Information Platform, citing Ministry of Economic Affairs Energy Administration statistics
```

Renewable energy generation increased by approximately 4.5 billion kWh compared to 2024. [^1] While this is a significant increase, the proportion remains at only 13.3% due to the low base year.

## 99% Dependent on Imports

The issue with natural gas lies not in power generation technology, but in its source. Approximately 99% of Taiwan's natural gas supply relies on imported LNG. [^1]

In 2025, 23.1 million metric tons were imported, primarily from Qatar (33.7%), Australia (33.5%), and the United States (9.9%). The remainder came from the Middle East, Southeast Asia, the Americas, Africa, and Europe. [^1]

```tw-bars
2025 LNG Import Sources (%)
Qatar | 33.7 | Largest source
Australia | 33.5 | Nearly identical
USA | 9.9 | Third largest
Others | 22.9 | Middle East, SE Asia, Africa, Europe, etc.
Source: Ministry of Environment Energy Information Platform, citing MOEA Energy Administration "Energy Statistics Handbook—Natural Gas" (Others = 100% minus top three, calculated by author)
```

The top two sources combined account for over two-thirds. The Ministry of Environment's page states that diversifying gas procurement sources can ensure a stable supply of natural gas. [^1]

## Three Receiving Terminals and 117%

Imported LNG must be unloaded and regasified at receiving terminals before being sent to users via pipelines. Taiwan has terminals in the north, central, and southern regions; currently operational terminals include Yong'an, Taichung, and the Guanyuan (Third) Receiving Terminal. [^1]

The Guanyuan (Third) Receiving Terminal began supplying gas in mid-2025. The Ministry of Environment's page states that this would allow for regionalized supply across the north, center, and south by utilizing the existing "figure-8" land-sea gas pipeline network for backup and transfer; however, later in the same page, "achieving regionalized supply for the northern, central, and southern regions" is listed as a goal to be met _after_ expansion is completed. [^1]

Capacity remains another issue. The combined annual design operating capacity of the Yong'an, Taichung, and Guanyuan terminals for 2026 is 23 million metric tons; however, the average load factor from 2021 to 2025 was approximately 117%. [^1] This 117% is the average across those five years, not a single-year load factor under the 2026 design capacity (the 23 million metric ton design capacity for 2026 already includes Guanyuan).

The Ministry of Environment defines the terminal load factor as annual operating volume divided by design operating capacity; a figure exceeding 100% means actual throughput is higher than the design value. The page also explains that a higher load factor increases operational risk, noting that 117% indicates equipment has been in an overloaded state for a long period. [^1]

## 80% After Expansion

To reduce risk, Taiwan is promoting the construction of the Guanyuan Third and Intercontinental receiving terminals, as well as the expansion of the Taichung and Yong'an terminals. [^1]

Once all are completed, the total design operating capacity is expected to reach 37 million metric tons per year, with the average load factor projected to drop to approximately 80%, achieving regionalized supply for the northern, central, and southern regions. [^1]

It is important to distinguish: 117% is the current situation based on the five-year average, while 80% is the planned value after expansion is complete. Between these two points lie the construction and expansion projects.

## Gas-for-Coal

Why is the proportion of gas so high? The Ministry of Environment describes this pathway as "gas-for-coal": coal-fired units are gradually replaced or retrofitted into gas-fired units to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. [^1]

It is generally understood that gas-fired power has lower carbon emissions than coal, and these units can ramp up or down quickly, require less land, and have shorter construction timelines, making them suitable for pairing with intermittent renewable energy.

While the logic of this pathway is sound, it pushes the gas supply system close to full capacity. When gas-fired power accounts for nearly half of generation, any single gas disruption or terminal failure affects more than just electricity prices—it threatens the stability of the entire power supply.

## How to Read These Three Charts

The first chart describes the current power structure: gas at 47.7% is the largest single power source.

The second chart describes source concentration: Qatar and Australia combined account for 67.2%. Diversification in procurement has been achieved, but not in terms of balanced distribution.

The third chart describes infrastructure margin: with an average load factor of 117% from 2021 to 2025, the Ministry of Environment states that equipment is in a long-term overloaded state; the 37 million metric ton expansion plan aims to bring this back down to 80%.

Taken together, these three figures illustrate the current predicament of Taiwan's natural gas system: power generation relies on it, imports rely on ships, unloading relies on receiving terminals, and those terminals are operating in a long-term overloaded state.
