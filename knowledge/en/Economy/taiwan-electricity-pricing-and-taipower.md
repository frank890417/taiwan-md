# Taiwan's Electricity Pricing Mechanism and Taipower: That 12.83% Didn't Rise, but the Bill Became a NT$71.1 Billion Supplementary Budget Request

> **30-Second Overview:** On September 18, 2026, the Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA) convened the second Electricity Rate Review Committee of 2026, resolving not to adjust electricity rates from October to December. Taipower's current review calculated a necessary adjustment of 12.83%, raising the average price per kWh from NT$3.7823 to NT$4.2675. This hike was not rejected but deferred for discussion at the third committee meeting in December; instead, the priority has shifted to securing a NT$71.1 billion government subsidy.

**Keywords**: Electricity rates, Taipower, Electricity Rate Review Committee, supplementary budget, energy

---

## The Afternoon of the Adjournment

On the afternoon of September 18, 2026, the MOEA held its second Electricity Rate Review Committee meeting of the year. At the post-meeting press conference, MOEA Deputy Minister Lai Chien-hsin concluded that electricity rates for October through December will remain at current levels. The adjustment was "not yet decided," to be discussed in December during the third review meeting alongside transmission and distribution rates. If an adjustment is made in December, the new rates will apply starting January 1, 2027. [^1][^2][^4]

A set of numbers was originally on the table. According to Taipower's current review, the necessary adjustment is 12.83%, raising the average price per kWh from NT$3.7823 to NT$4.2675—an increase of NT$0.4852 per kWh. Another figure in Taipower's presentation, NT$0.4174, represents the increase compared to the previous rate of NT$3.8501. [^13] The MOEA stated that if calculated with a reasonable profit margin of 5%, Taipower could expect to earn an average of NT$7.75 billion per month over the next three months following an adjustment. [^1]

These numbers were not rejected; they were simply left on the table. The committee resolved to prioritize seeking a different sum: a NT$71.1 billion government subsidy for Taipower, which must be approved by the Legislative Yuan as part of a supplementary budget. [^1][^2]

## Behind one electricity bill stands a company paying the difference upfront

To understand what "not yet decided" implies, one must look at Taipower's income statement this year. In 2025, Taipower recorded a pre-tax profit of NT$75.6 billion, ending three consecutive years of losses; Chairman Tseng Wen-sheng and General Manager Kuo Tien-ho reported this to shareholders during the June annual general meeting. [^8][^9] At that time, the company's public stance was that international fuel prices had stabilized and, combined with cost-cutting measures, losses were expected to narrow.

Then, conflict in the Middle East disrupted those plans. The MOEA explained that following the outbreak of hostilities, international oil prices fluctuated between US$68 and US$132 per barrel. To stabilize consumer prices, Taipower has continued to absorb fuel costs, absorbing approximately NT$71.1 billion from March to August this year. As of the end of July, Taipower's losses stand at NT$21.4 billion; based on current international energy price volatility, full-year losses are projected to exceed NT$71.1 billion. [^1][^2] In other words, losses over the next five months could expand by another NT$50 billion, averaging more than NT$10 billion per month. [^1]

Taipower Deputy General Manager Wang Yun-chun added two reasons after the meeting: as of July, natural gas costs for the power industry are still climbing, and in the coming months, the return to non-summer electricity rates will lead to decreased sales revenue alongside rising fuel costs, expanding losses in the remaining months. [^1]

## How that 12.83% was calculated

Electricity prices are not figures the Executive Yuan can announce at will; there is a formula acting as a safeguard. According to Article 49 of the _Electricity Act_, electricity sales prices are determined by the central competent authority. The currently announced "Calculation Formula for Electricity Rates of Public Electricity Sales Businesses" was implemented by the MOEA on November 6, 2017. [^6] Under this formula's review mechanism, rates are reviewed twice a year, with increases or decreases generally not exceeding 3% per instance. However, when supply costs rise or fall significantly, the Electricity Rate Review Committee may make appropriate adjustments to the magnitude of change based on the operation of the Electricity Price Stabilization Fund. [^5]

Thus, the 12.83% figure is the result calculated by Taipower after factoring in fuel costs, exchange rates, and the electricity generation/purchase structure. Whether a hike can occur and by how much is left to the committee's decision. During the September 18 meeting, the committee reviewed Taipower's various cost-control measures and deemed the relevant expenditures reasonable. Lai Chien-hsin relayed the members' judgment: if there had been no Middle East conflict this year, Taipower would have had the opportunity to maintain a surplus and gradually improve its finances. However, as the conflict continues and international oil and gas prices fluctuate wildly, members believe priority should be given to seeking government subsidies to reduce Taipower's financial pressure. [^1] According to the MOEA Energy Agency's presentation on decision-making methods, the committee generally decides by consensus; voting only occurs when opinions differ, using a roll-call system where meeting conclusions only record the vote ratio. [^14] No formal vote was taken in this session; the "not yet decided" status resulted from discussions held in a closed-door session after Taipower members had left. [^15]

## From 2.6253 to 3.7823

This mechanism was last heavily tested in 2022. After the Russia-Ukraine war drove up fuel prices, the committee decided on March 29 to "defer discussion," setting a temporary period of two to three months. The special session on June 27 resulted in the first tiered hike: no adjustment for residential users consuming under 1,000 kWh per month, small shops, or low-voltage users; residential users above 1,001 kWh saw a 9% increase, while high-voltage and above users saw a 15% increase. Six industries—agriculture/fisheries, food, department stores, catering, cinemas, and gyms—as well as schools below the high school level, were excluded. [^5]

In September of that same year, the second review session did not change electricity prices, maintaining an average of NT$2.8458 per kWh, while the government planned a NT$150 billion capital injection for Taipower. [^5] The March 2023 committee set the average price adjustment at 11%, with no adjustment for residential users under 700 kWh; in September, the rate remained unchanged at 3.1154. [^5]

A turning point occurred during the March 22, 2024 session. A report from the Legislative Yuan Budget Center recorded the figures faced at the time: in 2022 and 2023, Taipower lost NT$1.2 and NT$0.9 per kWh sold, respectively, with cumulative losses reaching NT$382.6 billion by 2023. Given such a balance sheet, the committee—on the premise of a "NT$100 billion government subsidy"—resolved to raise the overall average electricity price to NT$3.4518, effective April 1, accompanied by three resolutions, one of which requested Taipower to review the rationality of user household classifications. [^5]

Prices moved twice more after March. The September 30, 2024 session resulted in no adjustment for residential and small shop users (totaling 14.52 million households, or 95.2%) across all progressive tiers; industrial rates used a 14% baseline, while industries with declining electricity use and output saw either a freeze or a 7% adjustment. [^16] In October 2025, the average adjustment was 0.71%; industrial rates were unchanged, and residential users under 700 kWh saw an increase of NT$0.1 per kWh. [^17]

This year's rhythm has returned to a familiar pattern: the first session on March 27 resolved not to adjust, maintaining an average of NT$3.7823 per kWh, [^4][^7] while the second meeting on September 18 resulted in "not yet decided," leaving the third December meeting to consider transmission and distribution rates, international energy prices, government subsidies, and Taipower's finances collectively. [^1][^4]

## Three failed subsidies and four capital injections

On October 22, Taipower provided a more detailed breakdown. Deputy General Manager Wang Yun-chun explained the budget handling of recent years: the NT$100 billion supplementary budget for 2024, the NT$100 billion public service budget for 2025, and the NT$100 billion from the Resilience Special Act—"none of these budgets passed"; regarding capital injections, there was NT$100 billion in 2024 and NT$150 billion in 2025, increasing Taipower's capital from NT$330 billion to NT$580 billion. [^19]

The figures provided by Accounting Department Director Tang Hui-lien explain why they are seeking a supplementary budget now. Natural gas prices for electricity users have risen cumulatively by over 70% since April 2026; Taipower's natural gas costs from March to August increased by approximately NT$71.1 billion compared to before the conflict. If current gas prices persist through year-end, annual fuel costs will increase by over NT$160 billion. Taipower lost NT$20.3 billion in the first eight months of this year, with cumulative losses currently at NT$371.2 billion; without a subsidy, losses could exceed NT$420 billion by year-end, returning to the historical high of NT$421.1 billion seen at the end of 2024. [^19]

On the same day, another set of figures illustrated the company's scale: Taipower's annual capital expenditure reaches NT$300 billion, its debt has reached NT$1.85 trillion, and interest is estimated at approximately NT$30 billion this year. Deputy General Manager Tsai Chih-meng was blunt: even if the NT$71.1 billion subsidy is secured, compared to an annual fuel cost increase of over NT$160 billion, there remains a gap of over NT$80 billion. [^19]

## Eight business groups speak up first

On November 3, eight major industry and commerce groups issued a joint statement calling on the Legislative Yuan to support the subsidy, but with conditions. The statement argued that the government can reasonably subsidize Taipower and CPC Corporation for policy-driven losses incurred while stabilizing prices; however, it emphasized the need to clearly distinguish between policy costs and corporate management responsibilities by establishing transparent review and oversight systems. It also required both companies to present medium-to-long-term financial improvement plans and regularly disclose implementation results. [^20]

The most notable part of the statement was this: the business groups demanded a reasonable compensation system for policy expenditures to avoid using price freezes and cross-subsidies that force the costs of energy transition disproportionately onto industrial users, and insisted that major price adjustments should involve full communication with industries. [^20] On the same day, Premier Cho Jung-tai told the Legislative Yuan that subsidizing CPC and Taipower is "extremely important," hoping the legislature would act with the welfare of the people in mind. [^21]

These remarks clearly mark the center of the controversy. A price hike places costs on the user's electricity bill; a subsidy places them on the government's budget; but what the business groups want is a third thing: separate accounting for "what are policy requirements" versus "what is management efficiency." This is precisely the issue that this mechanism has failed to resolve over the long term.

## Who pays upfront, and what is the difference?

Looking at the three different approaches side-by-side clarifies the pattern: in 2022, the government planned a NT$150 billion capital injection for Taipower; in 2024, an 11% hike was implemented on the premise of a NT$100 billion subsidy; and in 2026, they are first seeking a NT$71.1 billion supplementary budget while leaving the 12.83% hike to be discussed in December. [^1][^5] The commonality among these three methods is that the debt does not disappear; it is simply recorded under different accounts—price hikes are recorded on user bills, capital injections and subsidies on government budgets, and the time gap between the two falls onto Taipower's income statement.

During the September 18 meeting, members also discussed another way of sharing the burden: only raising electricity rates for AIDC (AI Data Centers) and high-tech industries. Lai Chien-hsin relayed that the committee believed a comprehensive review should be conducted rather than targeting one specific sector first, as different needs and industrial development opportunities must all be considered. [^1]

During the same period, the MOEA also made decisions on other energy prices; domestic natural gas and bottled LPG will be frozen until year-end, for the same reason of stabilizing prices. [^2] Regarding rumors that "electricity price hikes are certain," fact-checking platforms clarified in September that the committee's resolution was to not adjust rates for now; the rumored 12.83% hike and NT$4.2675 per kWh rate were discussion topics, not finalized decisions. [^11] This distinction is worth remembering—"how much Taipower's review calculated" and "what the committee finally decides" are numbers in two different positions within this mechanism. [^1]

## That December table

Only three things can be confirmed moving forward: a third Electricity Rate Review Committee will be held in December to review various transmission and distribution rates, [^4] if an adjustment is made, new rates will apply from January 1, 2027, [^1] and before that, whether the NT$71.1 billion supplementary budget passes rests with the Legislative Yuan. [^1] On October 16, party caucuses negotiated and agreed to invite Premier Cho Jung-tai to the Legislative Yuan on October 23 to report on this supplementary budget, with review to begin after questioning. [^18] By early November, the budget was still under review, and both business groups and the Executive Yuan urged for the same thing from the perspectives of industry and public welfare, respectively. [^20][^21]

For a household or a small shop, the most practical part of this story is: your electricity bills will look the same as they did in March until December; as for next year's bill, it depends on whether a December meeting and a Legislative Yuan vote happen first. During this period, Taipower continues to absorb costs upfront, while the formula and the committee continue to separate "how much should rise" from "whether it should rise" into two steps—this is exactly the design left by the 2017 formula, and the reason why every electricity price controversy in recent years returns to this same scene. [^5][^6]
