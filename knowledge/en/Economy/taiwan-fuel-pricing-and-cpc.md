# Taiwan's Oil Price Mechanism and CPC: The 36 Yuan Saved, Recorded Under the Name of a Company Standing at the Front

On Sunday, March 22, 2026, at 8:30 p.m., every entrance to the Yahoo Gas Station beside the Yunlin Science and Technology Industrial Park was lined with vehicles. Station attendants used traffic cones to mark out a lane; motorcycles had to queue just like cars. A small delivery truck loaded with four or five oil drums pulled in to fill up at once. A woman who had just pumped twenty liters said happily: "Saved 36 yuan—saving is earning."[^1]

Everyone was rushing to beat the midnight price hike the next day, when gasoline would jump 1.8 yuan per liter.[^2] At the same gas station, a woman in her eighties surnamed Chen had seen her natural gas bill rise by 100 yuan the previous month; when she heard the news about the oil price increase that day, she hurried over on her motorcycle, only to discover her tank was already full.[^1]

That same afternoon, Premier Cho Jung-tai made a remark in Changhua that drew criticism for days afterward. He said that according to international oil prices, gasoline should have risen by fifteen yuan; after government calculation, CPC absorbed 13.2 yuan, leaving only a 1.8 yuan increase, so "everyone passing a gas station should thank CPC."[^3]

The 36 yuan that woman saved and the company Premier Cho asked everyone to thank are two sides of the same coin. Taiwan's oil price follows a formula calculated once a week, but the price the formula produces rarely appears directly on the pump sign. In between, for the past twenty years, CPC has mostly borne the burden. What this article discusses is how this design of "having CPC stand at the front" has saved money for whom, who ultimately pays, and something no one has done in twenty years: track who uses the most.

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's gasoline and diesel prices are calculated by a formula once a week, but the numbers on the pump sign rarely match the formula price: the government collects less commodity tax, CPC absorbs a portion itself, and what remains is what you pay. Six months into the Middle East conflict, the portion CPC has absorbed is officially estimated at around 20 billion yuan; in the supplementary budget the Executive Yuan submitted to the Legislative Yuan, subsidies and capital increases for CPC exceed 300 billion yuan, still pending review. Japan subsidizes businesses through budgets, Hong Kong subsidizes taxis, South Korea distributes money per capita—Taiwan has a state-owned company with a 93% debt-to-asset ratio absorb the cost first. The savings are divided equally per liter among everyone, yet who uses the most has never been tracked from the 2008 price spike to today.

## The Bigger the Storms Outside, the More CPC Stands at the Forefront

"The bigger the storms outside, the more CPC stands at the forefront. When prices outside surge most fiercely, CPC absorbs the most." At CPC's 80th anniversary celebration on June 1st, Taiwan Petroleum Workers' Union President Chen Chia-lin described his company this way.[^4] The statement carries both pride and complaint. That day, the union was demanding budget allocations, wage increases, and the incorporation of night shift allowances into regular wages. Those standing at the forefront know the cost better than anyone.

Over two successive chairmen, this company has told the same story. At a media lunch in late 2022, Li Shun-chin began with self-deprecating remarks about the year's corruption and workplace safety issues, then pivoted: "I know today the media will all ask one question, so let me break the news myself!" The news he broke was that after absorbing natural gas price differences that year, the company's annual loss could set a historical record.[^5] Two years later he reported again: borrowings approaching 700 billion yuan, with interest alone exceeding 100 billion per year.[^6] His successor Fang Chen-jen said in early 2025 that the debt-to-asset ratio had reached nearly 93%, with cumulative losses exceeding half the capital base.[^7]

So in late November 2025, CPC did something it rarely does: it stepped back. It announced that starting in December, when comparing oil prices with neighboring countries, it would first deduct their governments' subsidies, and would also raise the activation thresholds for its oil price stabilization measures by one level each. The rationale was stated plainly: from 2018 to that point, the two price-dampening mechanisms had cumulatively absorbed over 100 billion yuan, and "the current debt-to-asset ratio remains as high as 93%."[^8] It was a modest step back. Three months later, when the US and Iran went to war, the third week's oil price press release introduced a new term: "special stabilization mechanism," with CPC absorbing 60%, then raising it to 75% a week later.[^9] It was pushed back to the forefront.

Cho Jung-tai's remark to "thank CPC" was made from this position. The moment the words left his mouth, PTT's stock board erupted in pushback, all asking the same thing: "The whole public is footing the bill—what's to thank?" "Shouldn't we be thanking taxpayers instead?" "Isn't the money CPC is subsidizing taxpayers' money?"[^10] Some defended the government: "Price stabilization—people complain about it, but if we don't stabilize prices they'll complain anyway."[^10] Taipei Mayor Chiang Wan-an said the next day that in a democratic system, it should be the government thanking the people for their trust, not the people thanking the government.[^11] Cho Jung-tai responded two days later that CPC's employees are also the nation's people, and more gratitude, blessings, and care among the people are all good things—"CPC fuels Taiwan, and Taiwan fuels Taiwan."[^12]

This quarrel over a single word of thanks laid bare the entire design: a company stands at the forefront, everyone pays, but the method and timing of payment are split apart. To see how it's split, you first have to see how a liter of oil is divided.

## How One Liter of Oil Gets Split Three Ways

Taiwan's state-owned oil company CPC publishes next week's fuel prices every Sunday at noon. It takes the weekly average price of Dubai and Brent crude, multiplies by the exchange rate, reflects 80 percent of the price movement in what CPC charges gas stations, then adds taxes and fees to arrive at the retail price. This formula was piloted in 2006; the benchmark crude and adjustment frequency have changed a few times, but since 2008 it has remained largely as it is now.[^13] Two brakes follow the formula. One is called the "Asia-neighboring minimum price"—the pre-tax price of 92-octane unleaded and diesel cannot exceed the lowest price that week in Japan, South Korea, Hong Kong, or Singapore. All gasoline grades adjust by the same amount each week; if 92 is capped, 95 and 98 are capped proportionally.[^13] The other is the "oil price stabilization mechanism": when 95-octane unleaded rises above a certain threshold, CPC absorbs part of the increase, absorbing more as the price climbs higher, and rising less proportionally when prices fall.[^14]

The press release from September 12 can serve as a template. That week, the formula-calculated price was 11.2 yuan higher than the previous week's posted price. The government forgoes 3.7 yuan in commodity tax, CPC absorbs another 6.8 yuan under the Asia-neighboring minimum price and the special stabilization mechanism, leaving 0.7 yuan to post on the pump—95-octane unleaded becomes 32.7 yuan per liter.[^15] The last few lines of the press release read the same every week: "Total absorption of 10.5 yuan and 10.8 yuan respectively for gasoline and diesel," "Domestic gasoline and diesel prices each increase by 0.7 yuan and 0.6 yuan respectively."[^15]

Of the three shares, the commodity tax portion is paid out first: in mid-March, the Executive Yuan issued a directive halving the gasoline commodity tax—for every liter sold, the national treasury loses over 3 yuan that week, and this requires no legislative approval.[^16] CPC's share is first recorded on its own balance sheet; after six months of the conflict, official estimates put combined gasoline and diesel losses at roughly 20 billion yuan.[^15] These two sums combined keep the posted number nearly flat: after peaking in late March, it held steady for eleven consecutive weeks, dropped three times from late June to early July, rose 0.7 yuan in late July due to the Red Sea crisis, then held flat for six more weeks, and rose another 0.7 yuan in mid-September.[^17] The Executive Yuan's own tally is "frozen for 20 weeks, reduced 3 weeks," while over the same period Brent crude rose more than 80 percent from pre-war levels.[^18]

The flat portion of this line is the 36 yuan that woman saved, and the reason Premier Cho Jung-tai asked everyone to say thank you. It is the easiest of the three shares to calculate and the least controversial, because it divides evenly per liter: however much you pump, that is how much you save. In 2008, it weathered a crisis the same way.

## Lin Mao-wen Remembers Those Seven Months in 2008

![Price board at CPC's Tianwei Station in Changhua, January 24, 2012: 98 Unleaded 32.8, 95 Unleaded 31.3, 92 Unleaded 30.6, Super Diesel 28.8 yuan.](/article-images/economy/cpc-tianwei-station-price-sign-2012.webp)

_On January 24, 2012, the sign at Changhua's Tianwei Station showed 95 Unleaded at 31.3 yuan. Two months later, the Ministry of Economic Affairs announced "oil price rationalization" and cancelled the price-smoothing mechanism. Photo: Chi-Hung Lin / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0_

Lin Mao-wen, former chairman of CPC Taiwan, looked back at the first major test of this mechanism in a 2018 article in the company's own publication. In 2008, as international oil prices climbed, the government ordered CPC to freeze prices—which, by his account, lasted "seven months"—and CPC posted a pre-tax loss of 138.7 billion yuan that year.[^19] On May 28, the government raised prices all at once, with 92 Unleaded jumping from 30 yuan to 33.9 yuan.[^20] That August, the formula reverted to weekly adjustments and established the framework that appears in today's press releases—those two columns: prices rise when they should, with the increase absorbed through commodity tax cuts and CPC absorption, and the absorbed portion "will be deducted in installments when oil prices fall later."[^13]

Those four words—"deducted in installments"—are the most frequently forgotten phrase in the entire design. When Economic Minister沈榮津 announced the three-tier smoothing mechanism in 2018, each bracket included the phrase "rise less when falling," meaning that the portion CPC did not raise during price increases would be recovered through smaller decreases when prices fell.[^14] CPC's announcement that day explained its own role in a single sentence: "China Petroleum Corporation, following Ministry of Economic Affairs directives, will implement the oil price smoothing mechanism starting next week."[^14] Decision-making authority rests with the Ministry of Economic Affairs, and the Petroleum Administration Act is written the same way: when oil prices fluctuate sharply, the central competent authority implements price controls.[^21]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> In the same article, Lin Mao-wen left behind one sentence: "The oil price smoothing mechanism embodies the spirit of 'stabilization,' not government subsidy," because when oil prices fall, CPC can fall less, and "can recover previous losses."[^19] This explains why for two decades this has been called "absorption" rather than "subsidy": the money is only temporarily held at CPC and will be recovered from future price decreases. Yet from 2018 to October 2025, cumulative absorption under two smoothing mechanisms exceeded 10 billion yuan,[^8] a figure that itself shows the amount recovered through smaller decreases falls far short. As long as it cannot be recovered, "absorption" becomes a year-on-year loss on the balance sheet, and eventually still requires a supplementary budget. A difference in terminology determines what question the opposition asks when the supplementary budget goes to the Legislative Yuan: "what kind of major incident."

The Control Yuan issued a corrective measure against the Ministry of Economic Affairs in 2010, noting that the weights of the two crude oil types in the formula did not match actual import ratios, and that low-sulfur fuel oil was priced too cheaply, creating cross-subsidization.[^22] The 2012 case was more direct. According to Lin's account, on April 1 that year the Ministry announced "oil price rationalization" and cancelled the smoothing mechanism, citing among its reasons the need to address "irrational phenomena such as the poor subsidizing the rich."[^19] CPC still posted a loss of over 300 billion yuan in its final accounts that year. Government documents from fourteen years ago already acknowledged that per-liter discounts flow to those who consume more. Then the 30-yuan line returned in 2018, thresholds were raised in 2025, and special smoothing came in 2026—the same design bearing more weight each time. CPC has posted losses for six consecutive years since 2020, a period during which the Middle East had not yet gone to war.[^7]

## Uber Driver Jian Xiuzhi Parked on the Roadside Waiting for Rides

![Taiwan CPC gas price sign at Caoggang Station, January 14, 2021: 95 Unleaded 26.2, 92 Unleaded 24.7, Super Diesel 22.3 yuan per liter.](/article-images/economy/cpc-caogang-pump-prices-2021.webp)

_The numbers on the sign represent the visible portion per liter. Taiwan CPC Caoggang Station, January 14, 2021. Photo: Kai3952 / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

In May, Uber driver Jian Xiuzhi told a reporter that last year his monthly fuel costs were only a few thousand yuan, but this month he spent several thousand more. "I can only park on the roadside waiting for rides and don't dare keep driving around," he said.[^23] Liu Hongzhang, chairman of the National Automobile Drivers' Rights Alliance, calculated for his colleagues that drivers who work diligently add thirty liters a day, which means their monthly fuel costs increase by three to five thousand yuan.[^24] The government raised the oil price subsidy for taxis from six thousand yuan per vehicle to a maximum of fifteen thousand. When a public television reporter asked a driver what he thought, Mr. Qi's answer was just three words: "That's great."[^25]

Liu Hongzhang wasn't as pleased, because the subsidy is deducted at five yuan per liter, and to use up fifteen thousand yuan, "a driver would have to drive two hundred kilometers a day," he said. What he wanted was a fare adjustment—"it shouldn't be tied to subsidies."[^25] In early March, the owner of a noodle stall in Banqiao had just ordered a tank of cooking gas for nearly six hundred yuan. The gas shop owner told her the next tank would cost forty yuan more. She didn't dare pass the increase on to customers, fearing she'd scare them away.[^26] Since the war began, CPC announced it would not adjust bottled cooking gas prices, and in September announced a freeze through year-end.[^27] That tank of gas at the noodle stall was also partly absorbed by CPC.

This is what "dividing equally per liter" looks like in people's lives. The savings follow the fuel tank: drivers who add thirty liters a day save the most, families who fill up once a month save the least, and people who don't drive save nothing—unless you count fares that didn't increase. The biggest advantage of this design is that you don't have to ask who anyone is; the biggest problem is also here. In May, National Central University professor Liang Qiyuan said that CPC's absorption of oil prices is an across-the-board subsidy with no targeting. Fuel costs represent a very small share of high-income households' spending, so subsidies should be redirected to public transportation.[^28] Zhang Qiongting from National Sun Yat-sen University used an analogy at a Legislative Yuan press conference: "If a tank of oil costs one hundred yuan and the government subsidizes sixty yuan, the subsidy makes everyone think they're living on forty yuan."[^29] Hsiao Tai-chi from Academia Sinica called it "subsidy addiction."[^29]

So who used the most during these six months? The Ministry of Economic Affairs Energy Bureau has oil sales statistics, but the breakdown figures for household gasoline versus industrial oil for this period haven't been compiled into a public table.[^30] You can see a driver parked on the roadside saving fuel. Whether all of Taiwan drove less as a result, how much of those twenty billion yuan went to households that add one hundred liters a year, and how much went to fleets and factories—the Energy Bureau's breakdown figures were never laid out. No one knows. From the single price hike in 2008 to today, public discussions have included formulas, brackets, and absorption amounts, but there's never been a single table showing you whose hands these savings ended up in.

## Japan Subsidizes Wholesalers, Hong Kong Subsidizes Taxis, Taiwan Lets CPC Bear the Load

Behind the September 12 press release was an attached table listing neighboring countries' subsidized oil prices: Japan's gasoline converted to NT$34.8 per liter, South Korea NT$52.9, Singapore NT$84, Hong Kong NT$134.5, and Taiwan NT$32.7.[^15] Different countries sell different fuel grades—Japan has no 95-octane, Hong Kong has neither 92 nor 95—so the table compares the closest equivalent in each market. It appears weekly as proof of "Asia's lowest prices," and it's a table Taiwanese people take some pride in seeing. What it doesn't show is how each country pays for it.

```tw-bars

*Taiwan 95 Unleaded | 32.7
Japan 92 Unleaded | 34.8 | Japan does not sell 95-octane
South Korea 95 Unleaded | 52.9
Singapore 95 Unleaded | 84.0
Hong Kong 98 Unleaded | 134.5 | Hong Kong does not sell 92 or 95-octane
Source: CPC Taiwan September 12, 2026 press release, Table 2, prices include government subsidies
```

Japan's government restarted subsidies in mid-March, channeling money directly to oil wholesalers to keep retail gasoline prices around 170 yen per liter.[^31] Hong Kong began subsidizing commercial vehicle diesel in late April, then extended subsidies to taxis, minibuses, and school buses' liquefied petroleum gas in May, targeting commercial vehicles.[^32] South Korea started distributing high-oil-price relief payments directly in late April, calculated per person, with larger amounts for low-income households and those outside the capital region.[^33] Three neighbors each chose a group to subsidize: wholesalers, commercial vehicles, or individuals sorted by income tier. Taiwan's approach was to have the state-owned CPC absorb the costs first, then allocate supplementary budgets and capital increases after the fact. TVBS English in April pointed out this difference: it protected the government budget in the short term, but concentrated the risk on this state-owned company.[^34]

Former Economic Minister Wang Mei-hua laid it out more bluntly in May at an energy forum. She said Taiwan's government simply takes exceptional care of its people—no electricity price hikes, no oil price hikes, no natural gas hikes—with CPC absorbing everything. During her tenure, she fought the Legislative Yuan for Taipower's budget; "the Legislative Yuan wouldn't give a single cent," and "the whole problem was solved through politics." Her conclusion: price freezes have political considerations, but political considerations or not, you can't let the company go bankrupt.[^35]

A Taipei Times editorial in July argued this is exactly what Taiwan should do: the over 100 billion yuan CPC absorbed protected Taiwan's entire economy, because it sits upstream of nearly every enterprise.[^36] Liao Hui-chu of Tamkang University warned in March in the same newspaper that CPC's balance sheet had been weakened to the point where refinancing was unclear. In September, she wrote again arguing these subsidies were a necessary cost to preserve the semiconductor supply chain.[^37][^38] The same economist published two articles with opposing positions within six months, perfectly framing the two poles of this debate: the cost of absorbing first is real, and what absorbing first protects is also real. The difference is that neighbors wrote the cost into their budgets; Taiwan wrote it into one company's profit-and-loss statement, and only sent it to the Legislative Yuan when there was nowhere left to write.

## Legislative Yuan Question: Which Category of "Major Incident" Is This?

On September 3, the Executive Yuan approved a supplementary budget exceeding 600 billion yuan. The funds allocated to CPC came in two tranches: one of over 100 billion yuan to compensate for the price differential it absorbed over the year, and another of 2.338 billion yuan in capital injection, enabling this company with a 93% debt-to-asset ratio to continue investment in Taichung Plant Phase 3 and the intercontinental receiving station.[^39][^40] The subsidy covers the price differential already absorbed, while the capital injection purchases its future—the two tranches have different destinations. Deputy Minister of Economic Affairs Lai Chien-hsin said the capital injection was "investing in the future and enhancing national defense resilience."[^40]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> This section contains three figures: CPC's weekly report showing approximately 1.985 billion yuan absorbed over half a year of conflict, the supplementary budget subsidizing CPC by 10.14 billion yuan, and capital injection into CPC of 23.38 billion yuan. They cannot be subtracted from or added to each other. The 1.985 billion yuan covers only gasoline and diesel, calculated from February 28 onward. The 10.14 billion yuan is an estimate for the entire fiscal year; the Ministry of Economic Affairs press release says "oil and gas," while the deputy minister's press conference says "gasoline and diesel"—official statements on whether natural gas is included are not aligned. The 23.38 billion yuan is capital and has no direct relationship to how much was absorbed over this half year.[^15][^39][^40] These three figures each use different periods and product categories; cross-checking them against each other will yield false conclusions. What they collectively demonstrate is only one thing: the company that bore the burden first can no longer sustain it.

The supplementary budget required Legislative Yuan approval. Article 79 of the Budget Act lists four circumstances permitting supplementary budgets, one being "when an enterprise's expenses for a major incident exceed the statutory budget."[^41] Democratic Progressive Party Caucus Whip Chen Ching-lung asked that day: "After six consecutive years of losses, suddenly requiring a supplementary budget this year—which category of 'major incident' is this?"[^42] Kuomintang Caucus Secretary-General Hsu Yu-hsien said the next day that the Executive Yuan did not include the capital injection in next year's general budget but instead chose to have the Legislative Yuan "jump the queue" with only three months of review time, and the "Kuomintang Caucus does not rule out deleting it entirely."[^43] Lee Yen-hsiu said the government's responsibility is to maintain price stability, "not to use taxpayers' money to subsidize the government's long-standing errors in energy policy."[^44] Democratic Progressive Party legislator Wu Szu-yao countered that the general budget passed third reading on August 3, yet "it remains frozen in the Legislative Yuan's freezer until now."[^45]

The opposition's questions concerned procedure, and also that unresolved question: ultimately, who pays for what was borne first. At the Legislative Yuan's Economic Committee meeting in May, Kuomintang legislator Hsieh I-feng asked CPC Chairman Fang Chen-jen whether the company would fall below net worth; Fang said it was hard to estimate by year-end, "but it absolutely cannot fall below net worth." She then asked Economic Minister Kung Ming-hsin whether CPC would fall below net worth if no allocation were made this year; Kung said "it probably won't, but capital injection must be completed."[^46] As of mid-September, the supplementary budget had been submitted to the Legislative Yuan, but the committee had not yet announced a review date. The Executive Yuan's position is that this year's revenue exceeded the original budget by over 600 billion yuan, and after the supplementary allocation, borrowing actually decreased by 900 million yuan.[^39] The money still comes from the national treasury; it just happens that revenue collection was higher this year.

## The Three Return Routes of Those 36 Yuan

Back to the Yahoo gas station on March 22. The 36 yuan the woman saved will return to her through three routes, some visible and some not. One route runs through the Legislative Yuan, where the supplementary budget for subsidies and capital increases to CPC comes from the national treasury—whether it passes review and how much gets cut still has no timeline. Another route emerges the next time oil prices fall; the 2018 mechanism explicitly states "when prices drop, they drop less," and those few cents of the smaller drop are recovered—they'll never appear in any news headline about a price cut.[^14] The third route stays with CPC: over a billion yuan in annual interest,[^6] not included in the weekly report's 2 billion or the supplementary budget's 10 billion—it will remain on the company's profit-and-loss statement under a different line item, waiting for the next board chairman to leak it first at a media lunch.

The Ministry of Economic Affairs estimated that without these absorptions, 95-octane unleaded could have climbed straight from 28.9 yuan the week fighting began all the way to 45.4 yuan.[^40] This figure shows how much the price freeze protected. What it doesn't say is who got what: that grandmother's fuel tank was full that evening—she didn't save a cent. How much was distributed between a driver filling 30 liters a day and a household filling 100 liters a year remains unbroken down in the Energy Bureau's itemized statistics.

Cho Jung-tai said "Taiwan's CPC fuels everyone, everyone fuels Taiwan," and PTT said "shouldn't you be thanking taxpayers?" The blank space between those two sentences is the question no one has filled in for twenty years. Next Sunday at noon, CPC will release the numbers again, the figure on the sign will move a few cents, or it won't. There will probably still be a row of traffic cones in front of the gas station.
