The Executive Yuan originally planned to achieve two goals by 2025: a 20% share of renewable energy in the total power mix and a solar photovoltaic (PV) installed capacity of 20GW. [^1]

As of the end of 2024, the actual installed capacity for solar PV was 14.28GW. [^1]

That is a deficit of 5.72GW.

## 14.28 vs. 20

This gap was not created overnight. An investigation report by the Control Yuan categorized the causes into several groups: insufficient coordination between agencies, cumbersome and non-transparent review procedures, inconsistent review standards, and difficulties in land acquisition. [^1]

The report concludes that these factors have led to prolonged development periods for large-scale sites, prompting some foreign parent companies to evaluate downgrading their investment ratings for Taiwan. [^1]

In other words, the problem is not a lack of desire to invest, but rather that investors cannot calculate a predictable timeline.

## Where is the Bottleneck?

From application to grid connection, solar PV projects must undergo multiple stages of administrative procedures: electricity business preparation permits, land use permits or changes, construction permits, road use permits, and the issuance of electricity business licenses, among others. [^1]

Operators often invest significant capital upfront to lease and consolidate land, only to potentially face rejection due to inconsistent review standards or regulatory changes. [^1]

Land is another variable. The report notes that the annual increase in land required for ground-mounted solar PV is approximately 0.2% of the nation's total farmland—a figure frequently used in debates regarding "competition with agriculture for land." However, the report also cautions that factors such as food security, an aging agricultural population, labor shortages, and idle farmland are all interconnected. [^1]

## Illicit Forces

The report includes a section specifically addressing "green energy cockroaches." According to the Ministry of Justice, between 2021 and 2024, local district prosecutors investigated a total of 71 cases involving interference with green energy, resulting in the indictment of 114 individuals. These cases involved corruption, intervention by local factions, and extortion. [^1]

The investigation committee observed that because solar PV investments involve massive sums of money and procedures lack transparency, they are easily targeted by local interest groups. [^1]

The weight of this statement lies in the fact that it is not mere rumor; reports of foreign operators exiting the market due to intimidation were among the starting points for this investigation. [^1]

## The 23% Progress of Fishery-Solar Co-location

If one only looks at ground-mounted solar PV, they miss another even slower path: fishery-solar co-location (_yú-diàn gòng-shēng_).

The policy target is to reach 4.4GW by 2025, but as of the end of 2024, there was only 997.2MW, representing approximately 23% progress. [^1]

The situation on the ground is even more stark. An audit by the Tainan City Audit Office regarding fishery-solar co-location projects in 2023 found that the approval rate was less than 10%. [^1]

During symposiums, operators reflected that most currently rely on electricity revenue to subsidize aquaculture operations; without solar income, fishermen generally lack the incentive to operate, but if solar operators are forced to engage in aquaculture, the learning curve is too steep. [^1]

This point highlights the fundamental contradiction of fishery-solar co-location: it is designed as "aquaculture-first, with solar adding value," but the actual economic incentive is the reverse.

## Small Stakes, Big Gains

The report also points out a structural issue: solar PV project development is often led by land developers who simultaneously undertake construction and electricity business license applications. Some operators lack sufficient technical capability and capital, employing a "small stakes, big gains" strategy—obtaining the electricity business license and immediately selling their equity for profit. [^1]

The Ministry of Economic Affairs stated that the current Electricity Act does not impose restrictions on re-investment, equity transfers, or investment structures, making it difficult to track the number of projects where equity is transferred after completion. [^1]

## Feed-in Tariffs and Gray Areas

The final issue concerns the flow of money. Solar feed-in tariffs include farmland conversion fees and electricity development assistance funds, but the report points out that the actual proportion returned to the local project sites is quite low. [^1]

Additional donations provided by operators to gain local support easily create gray areas, which are then exploited by criminals for extortion. [^1]

The investigation committee recommends establishing clear principles for distribution and scope of use, prioritizing local vulnerable groups' care, public facility improvements, and the synergistic development of agriculture and green energy. [^1]

## Conclusion: Coordination, Enforcement, and Clarification

The Control Yuan's recommendations can be condensed into three points, all directed at the Executive Yuan: the Executive Yuan should strengthen coordination and integration among green energy agencies; the Ministry of Justice should research effective enforcement strategies; and the Executive Yuan should supervise the Ministry of Economic Affairs and the Ministry of Agriculture to clarify review standards and establish reasonable transition periods during regulatory amendments. [^1]

None of these three points are new, but the report places them on the same progress chart: the target is 20GW, the reality is 14.28GW, and the 5.72GW gap cannot be filled by a single policy tool alone.
