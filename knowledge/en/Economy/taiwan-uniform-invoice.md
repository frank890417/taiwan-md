# Invoices: The Paper from 1951 That Turned All Citizens into Tax Inspectors

> **30-Second Summary:** Every person in Taiwan has done this: receiving a thin invoice at checkout and slipping it into their pocket, or having it directly stored on a mobile device. Few people have considered that this paper simultaneously does three things: it is proof of transaction, it is a free lottery ticket, and it informs the state whether the business was honestly reported. This design—"turning all citizens into tax inspectors"—was initiated in 1951 by Finance Director Ren Hsien-ch'un, leading to a seventy-five percent surge in tax revenue within one year, and has been operating for over seventy years. Its secret for seven decades is turning "taking the invoice" into an unconscious reflex.

## Three Things That Paper In Your Hand Does

At the checkout counter of a convenience store, the clerk asks a question that hardly needs answering: "Do you want to load it [onto a mobile device]?" You scan the barcode on your phone or shake your head and take a thin slip still warm from the thermal paper. The whole action takes less than three seconds; you don't even look down at it.

![Unified Invoice receipt issued by Cukong 3C Anle Store, invoice number VX84870460.](/article-images/economy/invoice-tk3c-receipt-2007.webp)

_A Unified Invoice receipt from 2007. That eight-digit number is the proof for citizens to play every two months._ Photo: Solomon203 / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0\_

But that paper does three things simultaneously. It is a receipt, proving the transaction occurred. It is a lottery ticket; drawn on the 25th of every month twice a year, matching the last three digits wins a small prize redeemable at a convenience store, and winning the grand prize requires another trip to the bank[^1b]. It is also a report submitted to the state, telling the tax authorities that this store indeed recorded and reported this business[^1].

Some people put them in their wallets or toss them into the small box next to the cash register, piling them up in drawers, waiting for the bi-monthly draw to win two hundred dollars by matching the last three digits. Most Taiwanese people repeat this action tens of thousands of times in a lifetime, yet rarely wonder: why would buying something result in a piece of paper worth ten million? This phenomenon does not exist in the vast majority of places in the world. When foreigners first hear "your receipt might win ten million," their reaction is often to freeze, then regret having thrown the invoice in the trash bin.

Older generations remember guarding the television on draw day, watching the numbers spin out one by one. But how much of that collective memory of the TV drawing is real is difficult to verify today: the historical materials of Unified Invoices compiled by the Ministry of Finance contain no words from those hosts or programs, merely mentioning in passing "using variety shows to enhance promotional effects." The only thing that has truly never been broken is the action itself—"reaching out for the invoice."

> 💡 **Did You Know**
>
> Every line of a batch of invoices secretly hides an "inside joke" from the Ministry of Finance printing plant, usually involving intentionally misspelled or omitted characters, and each batch is different. In January and February 2019, the character for 'silver' on the cash register was missing a stroke; in March and April, the character for 'ticket' was missing a stroke. This is a visible anti-counterfeiting design, and some people online specialize in "nitpicking" these invoices. Interestingly, the Ministry of Finance printing plant's official website does not explicitly state this mechanism; it was verified only through cross-media corroboration by investigative personnel interviewed by Central News Agency and records from TVBS[^2].

How was this convenient reflex designed? The answer lies more than seventy years ago, on an island standing at the edge of a fiscal cliff.

## A Seventy-Five Percent Tax Surge Driven by Incentive on a Fiscal Cliff

In 1950, Taiwan was running out of money. The Kuomintang government had just retreated to Taiwan; American aid was intermittent, hyperinflation turned banknotes into waste paper, and small eateries and general stores conducted small cash transactions without issuing receipts or keeping books, creating a tax black hole the government could not collect from.

Where the money would come from was the daily problem Finance Director Ren Hsien-ch'un faced. He was only thirty-eight years old that year. The solution he devised was counterintuitive: instead of dispatching more tax inspectors to patrol the streets, he chose to exploit human greed.

![Ren Hsien-ch'un's university graduation photo from 1933, published in the eighth issue of 'Chizhi Yearbook'.](/article-images/economy/ren-xianqun-portrait-1933.webp)

_A university graduation photo of Ren Hsien-ch'un, taken in 1933. Seventeen years later, he printed a set of lottery numbers onto receipts across all of Taiwan._ Photo: Chizhi Yearbook Issue 8 / Wikimedia Commons, Public Domain\_

At the end of 1950, the Provincial Government of Taiwan issued two regulations, which officially came into effect on New Year's Day of the following year: from then on, every Unified Invoice issued by a store would print a set of numbers that could be used for lotteries[^3]. The government effectively outsourced the inspection work to consumers across the island: you naturally ask the owner for an invoice because of the free lottery ticket. The owner keeps the business recorded because they gave you the invoice. The Ministry of Finance's own records state plainly: in that year, operating tax revenue totaled over fifty-one million NTD, an increase of seventy-five percent compared to the two ninety million NTD of the previous year[^1]. One incentive increased tax revenue by seventy-five percent in one year.

Early invoices did not even conduct their own draws; they directly used the special prize numbers from the Patriotic Lottery. These two designs—"governance based on the dream of getting rich"—were twin creations of Ren Hsien-ch'un during the same period[^1]. Later, for promotion, the Ministry of Finance also composed a catchy promotional song[^1c]:

```tw-quote
Invoices are great and patriotic prizes, if the boss doesn't issue it, the law will be ashamed.
Unified Invoice Promotional Song Lyrics | A promotion by the Ministry of Finance; creation date is hard to verify.
```

These four short lines captured both faces of this paper: one side was the sweetness of "patriotic prize," and the other was the threat of "if the boss doesn't issue it, the law will be ashamed." In that era, the paper carried more than just tax matters. Flipping through official English historical materials from the Ministry of Finance reveals that Unified Invoices in the 1950s often printed slogans such as "Reclaim Mainland and Destroy the Communists Party"[^4]. Every receipt received at checkout carried an ideological message from the nation. From its first day, it was both smart behavioral economics and a hand reaching into daily life by an authoritarian state.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> The "governance based on the dream of getting rich" designed by Ren Hsien-ch'un was actually a pair of twins: one was the Unified Invoice, and the other was the Patriotic Lottery issued at the same time, which initially shared the same winning numbers. The older brother, the Patriotic Lottery, later suffered backlash from the greed it inspired: starting in 1985, people gambled on its last two digits in "Dajiale," leading to rampant display of lottery tickets and social disorder; the government was forced to discontinue it in 1987 after thirty-seven years and one thousand one hundred seventy-one issues[^5]. Both relied on the psychology of chance—one was consumed by gambling culture, the other was domesticated into a daily reflex. The difference might be: the greed associated with invoices can only be redeemed in small amounts, two hundred dollars at a time.

From day one, this paper carried two missions: one as an intelligent incentive engineering, and one as a mobilization medium for an authoritarian state. And the man who designed it soon experienced firsthand what it was like when the national machine he built turned against him.

## The Finance Director Was Later Imprisoned by His Own Country

In 1953, Ren Hsien-ch'un resigned from public office and opened a law firm. Two years later, on April 11, 1955, he was arrested by the Provincial Security Command of Taiwan under the charge of "knowing spies but failing to report": his cousin, Ren Fang-hsu, was accused of being a spy, and Ren Hsien-ch'un had guaranteed him without reporting[^6]. This accusation could be fatal in the era of white terror.

There is no definitive consensus on why he was truly imprisoned. Several stories circulate: one says that both Chiang Ching-kuo (蔣經國) and Ren Hsien-ch'un were interested in the opera singer Gu Zheng-qiu, and Ren Hsien-ch'un marrying her made Chiang Ching-kuo perpetually unhappy, leading the secret police to retaliate. Another theory suggests that he was highly valued by Chen Cheng, Chen Yi, and Wu Kuo-chen (吳國楨), making him a target for struggle that needed to be eliminated. A third story claims that while he was Finance Director, he had blocked funding from the National Salvation Corps founded by Chiang Ching-kuo[^6b]. None of these accounts hold overwhelming dominance, much like most political cases of that era, where the truth is locked in a drawer without a key.

What is certain is the outcome. He was sentenced to seven years and released on January 13, 1958—earlier than the unsubstantiated 1959 circulating in popular rumor; this date is explicitly written in the official archives of the National Human Rights Memory Bank[^7]. After his release, he stopped engaging in politics, went to Jinshan to farm and make jam, and passed away in 1975.

True exoneration came very slowly. In September 2011, the Compensation Foundation determined that the original verdict lacked evidence and restored his honor; on July 15, 2012, then-President Ma Ying-jeou personally apologized to Ren's family and awarded a certificate of restoration of honor; it was only in May 2019 that the Legislative Yuan Investigation Committee formally announced the revocation of that verdict[^7]. These three stages spanned two administrations—that of Ma Ying-jeou and Tsai Ing-wen—and were separated by eight years.

Here lies the sharpest contrast in the entire story. The system designed by Ren Hsien-ch'un survived the entire martial law period, proceeded into democratic Taiwan, drawing lots regularly without missing a day; yet the man who designed it did not have his innocence officially recognized by this country until forty-four years after his death. The system lived longer than the person—this is the first piece of evidence, and there are more to come.

## The Paper Gets Heavier and Heavier, But Inspection Power Never Keeps Up

On April 1, 1986, Taiwan's operating tax changed from the old system to the value-added type, adding another layer of mission to this paper[^8]. From then on, the paper carried an additional task: one side proved you bought something, and the other became proof for corporations to cross-check each other. The invoice issued by upstream manufacturers was the basis for downstream manufacturers to deduct tax amounts; the tax of an entire supply chain was thus linked together. In 1951, the same paper only needed to match one number (whether it would win); after 1986, it also had to match a hidden transaction chain (whether upstream companies issued invoices honestly).

In 1993, this paper gained another identity. The Creation Social Welfare Foundation launched "donate invoice casually, save the vegetative," connecting a small personal pleasure with collective public welfare[^9]. You did not need to spend money; you just threw the un-drawn invoice into the transparent box at the entrance, and if it won, the prize went to the foundation. Over thirty-two years, the invoices thrown in by all Taiwanese people accumulated to over one point seven five billion, earning nearly eighteen ninety million NTD in prizes for Creation, supporting about eight hundred poor vegetative patients; the monthly care cost for each person is fifty to seventy thousand NTD[^10].

![Invoice donation box outside Puta Temple in Daxi, Taoyuan.](/article-images/economy/putian-temple-invoice-donation-box-2016.webp)

_Throwing un-drawn invoices into a temple's donation box is a unique Taiwanese "casual public welfare."_ Photo: Solomon203 / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0\_

But this channel of goodwill is slowly drying up along with the digitization of the invoice itself. When the invoice changed from a piece of paper held in hand to a string of numbers stored on a mobile device, the action "casually throwing it into the donation box" disappeared. The invoices received by Creation dropped from over one hundred ten million at its 2008 peak to over forty-one million in 2024, a decrease of sixty percent; in the same year, eighty-four point eight billion electronic invoices were issued across Taiwan, with less than one percent donated to social welfare organizations[^10].

Yet, the system itself continues to create millionaires. Official figures from the Tax Agency of the Ministry of Finance show six hundred and eight as of 2021; by June 2026, a total of nine hundred fifty-seven people have become millionaires through an invoice[^11].

It sounds like this "citizen inspection" operates flawlessly, but there is another side to the truth. What truly sustains this system has never been someone proactively reporting tax evasion; it is the reflex of everyone passively reaching out for an invoice because of the lottery ticket. The path of proactive reporting is actually very difficult: according to the Tax Collection Act, the maximum reward for reporting and apprehending tax evasion is twenty percent of the fine, with a limit of four point eight million NTD[^12], but very few people actually receive it. A self-media account quoting the Taipei City National Taxation Bureau stated that in Taipei City, there are about seven thousand reports annually, with only about six hundred successful claims, a success rate of less than ten percent; a TVBS report from 2017 cited an overall success rate of about twenty percent for all types of reports[^13].

The two sets of numbers may not be the same population base, but they both point to the same thing: relying on reporting to catch tax evasion is far less effective than imagined. More ironically, one analysis calculated that the prize a reporter can ultimately receive from reporting a one-hundred-dollar underreported invoice is only about one dollar. This system turned two thirty million people into tax inspectors, but if an inspector were to seriously handle a case, their hourly wage would probably not even reach one dollar. Not to mention that a 2008 census cited in a 2012 report indicated that less than ten percent of all street vendors in Taiwan actually paid operating tax[^14].

Ironically, even the prize money intended for this system is not secure. The budget for invoice prizes is categorized under the Ministry of Finance's "agency commission," which has been cut by the Legislative Yuan almost every year—in twelve years from 2014 to 2025, there was no year where it was zero[^15].

In 2025, the People's Party group proposed a ten percent reduction, and the Ministry of Finance rarely protested on Facebook, saying this would "rob people of their small pleasures." Professor Chen Kuo-liang from the Political Science Department at National Chengchi University criticized the Ministry of Finance instead: the government's own budget documents had long listed this money as an "obligatory expenditure," yet the Ministry of Finance still treated it as a discretionary agency commission to cut, which is like "a thief shouting that he is chasing thieves." There is always a gap between ideal and reality. These are all old problems. The real new question starts with "who will win," quietly rewritten by a line of code.

## A Year When the Official Side Couldn't Explain If It Was Fair

First, let's clarify one thing: the controversy in 2024 was related to a separate lottery run by the Ministry of Finance called "Cloud Tree Fun e-Start Collection," and it is different from the main draw of the bi-monthly Unified Invoice. Over ninety thousand people participated, but four people repeatedly won major prizes like iPhones and iPads[^16b]. Some started asking: is this truly random? This controversy remains important because both the Cloud Tree and the main invoice draw rely on the same premise: entrusting "who will win" to a piece of code that no one can see, trusting that the government wrote this code honestly. The manipulation of the Cloud Tree was like a public stress test for this entire logic.

The answers revealed by the investigation struck at the core assumption of the entire system. The lottery program from the handling vendor was not purely random. The official investigation report from the Tax Agency of the Ministry of Finance is technical and honest: the program "increased the weight value of active participants and gave them priority drawing opportunities, adjusted the range for random number generation, and compared this random number with the participant's weight value"[^17]. In plain language, it means: the more diligently you participate, the higher your chance of winning. The "equal opportunity for all" proclaimed by the paper in 1951 was quietly rewritten into a loyalty program through lines of code in the cloud version.

But even the official side is unclear about whether there was a problem. The administrative investigation by the Ministry of Finance and the Tax Agency used the phrasing "still meets the drawing conditions for this activity"; the subsequent judicial investigation transferred to the Investigation Bureau of the Ministry of Justice, as reported by the media, simplified it to "no wrongdoing found"[^18]. Between these two stages and two sets of terminology, there is a blurry area. The four people who repeatedly won and caused controversy were initially stated by the Ministry of Finance: the prizes would be temporarily withheld pending investigation results—if the vendor's program was deemed problematic, the prizes would not be awarded; otherwise, they would proceed according to the original drawing results. Since the final investigation did not find any intentional profiteering or list tampering, the premise for awarding the prize was met, and all the National Taxation Bureau could do was file a civil lawsuit against the handling vendor and claim an additional twenty-four million NTD[^18].

The goodwill channel of "casually donating invoices" also relied on "anti-corruption mechanisms replacing human trust." In 2025, the case involving the Keelung branch of the Creation Foundation was exposed: a volunteer embezzled four thousand forty-five winning invoices donated by the public and claimed over one million NTD in prizes—the North District National Taxation Bureau had noticed this person's unusually high frequency of winning as early as 2020 and reported it to the foundation for investigation[^19]. The foundation later announced that prize redemption would no longer be handled by volunteers, and they promoted the electronic donation code "919" with greater emphasis—even goodwill needed to be digitized to be safe.

Putting these together reveals something that happened for the first time in seventy-five years. In the past, this system functioned because of an unspoken consensus: I believe this string of numbers is drawn randomly; I believe the invoices I throw into the box will be handled honestly. But when "who will win" becomes a program with adjustable weights, and when "goodwill" must rely on anti-corruption mechanisms to exist, this system that lived for seventy-five years based on "I believe" was asked for the first time to answer specifically: why should I still believe?

## Taiwan Sustained This Design as the Longest Marathon in the World

To even be worthy of such questioning, it had to last long enough. Taiwan is not the only place that came up with this trick, but based on verification, no second case has been seen running for seventy-plus years as a daily routine. Behavioral economist Joana Naritomi studied a similar mechanism in São Paulo, Brazil, finding that sales reported by vendors increased by at least twenty percent within four years: turning consumers into tax supervisors is indeed effective[^20]. But "effective" and "lasting long" are two different things.

Georgia brought a similar idea home in 2012; the official reason for termination was "ineffectiveness," but the think tank that proposed this study itself stated that this phrase had never been publicly analyzed to support it—the real reason appeared to be money: the daily reported volume of qualified receipts dropped from over two million at its peak to only three hundred thousand before closing, and the new government did not allocate budget funds to maintain an attractive winning rate, causing it to shut down in less than a year[^21]; Korea abandoned the lottery from 2005 to 2011, retaining only the prize mechanism for reporting non-issuing merchants[^22]; while Tanzania, far in Africa, only started piloting in 2022 and was preparing a national version in 2026[^23]. Other places often couldn't last long; Taiwan lasted seventy-five years without a single break.

```tw-bars
Taiwan: Seventy-five years without a break; no second case has lasted this long.
*Taiwan | 75 | From 1951 to present, never broken
Korea | 6 | 2005–2011, closed and retained reporting prizes
Georgia | 1 | Implemented in 2012, shut down in less than a year
Source: Ministry of Finance, ISET Policy Institute, Korean Tax Office
```

Tanzania is not included in this chart—we only have records of one three-month regional pilot in 2022; whether it operated, paused, or restarted in the intervening years has no public data, so it cannot be compared with the complete life cycle numbers like Korea and Georgia.

What accumulated over seventy-five years is a long string of numbers. As of June 2026, two thousand six hundred forty ten million special prizes have been drawn, but three hundred seven were never claimed, lost forever after the redemption period expired[^11]. Most of these unclaimed millions are simply because people forgot to look down for the draw—but some people were unbelievably lucky: in the latest batch of winners in 2026, one was a millionaire who won with just two dollars spent on a shopping bag at McDonald's in Tucheng, New Taipei City[^24].

So next time you check out at a convenience store and the clerk asks you "Do you want to load it [onto a mobile device]?" and you reach out to take that thin slip still warm, or let it float into the cloud—what you are completing is never just a transaction. You are continuing a reflex action that has lived for seventy-five years, longer than any person who promoted it, and even longer than the regime that gave birth to it. But this time, this action quietly adds a question that didn't need an answer before: will that string of numbers win you something, or was it all calculated by someone long ago?
