On January 13, 2026, the Ministry of Finance announced the national tax revenue for 2025: NT$3.7515 trillion. [^1]

A little over a month later, on February 24, an analysis of tax collection by the Department of Statistics of the Ministry of Finance recorded the same year as NT$3.7879 trillion. [^2]

Both are official figures, differing by NT$36.4 billion. The discrepancy is not due to a statistical error, but rather "when the books were closed."

## Two Totals, One Year

The January report was a preliminary statistic. The press release noted that the figure did not yet include tax payments received during the reconciliation period from January 1 to January 15, 2026, which primarily consisted of corporate income tax, individual income tax, business tax, and commodity tax. [^1]

The February report was the final statistic. With the reconciliation period payments included, the total actual net collection for the year became NT$3.7879 trillion, an increase of NT$26.1 billion (+0.7%) over 2024, accounting for 99.6% of the budget. [^2]

The shortfall amount also changed accordingly. The preliminary statistic stated a shortfall of NT$50.5 billion compared to the budget; the final statistic stated a shortfall of NT$14.3 billion. [^1][^2]

Tax revenue for the same year was first described as "NT$50.5 billion short," then changed to "NT$14.3 billion short" a month later. Both numbers are correct; the difference lies in the cutoff date.

## Where the Money Comes From

In the final statistical structure, the top eight tax items accounted for nearly 90% of the total.

Corporate Income Tax (CIT) at 30.3% (NT$1.1482 trillion), Individual Income Tax (IIT) at 23.1% (NT$873.7 billion), Business Tax at 16.3% (NT$615.9 billion), and Securities Transaction Tax at 7.7% (NT$292.8 billion). [^2]

Following these were Customs Duty at 4.1% (NT$156.6 billion), Commodity Tax at 3.8% (NT$144.0 billion), House Tax at 2.7% (NT$100.5 billion), and Land Value Tax at 2.6% (NT$98.2 billion); Land Value Increment Tax at 1.8% (NT$68.3 billion), License Tax at 1.8%, Tobacco and Alcohol Tax at 1.7%, and Inheritance Tax at 1.2%. [^2]

The remaining tax items totaled less than 5%, including Stamp Tax (NT$20.3 billion), Deed Tax (NT$16.0 billion), and Special Commodity and Service Tax (NT$5.6 billion). [^2]

CIT and IIT combined for 53.4%, an increase of 1.6 percentage points over 2024. If Land Value Increment Tax were also categorized under the income tax system, the proportion would be 55.2%. [^2]

## Who Pays the Heaviest Taxes

The two income tax categories determine more than half of the sources within the NT$3.8 trillion.

CIT reached NT$1.1482 trillion, an increase of NT$26.2 billion (+2.3%) over 2024; IIT reached NT$873.7 billion, an increase of NT$44.6 billion (+5.4%), with its 23.1% share being the highest in recent years. [^2]

The growth in CIT came from increases in both settlement tax payments and provisional tax payments; however, to respond to US reciprocal tariffs, the Ministry of Finance implemented installment payment plans for certain taxes, which offset some of the growth. The growth in IIT was driven by profit distributions and withholding taxes on wage income. [^2]

Another perspective is direct versus indirect taxes. In 2025, the proportion of direct taxes was 70.8%, breaking the 70% threshold for the first time (compared to 69.8% in 2024). Among major economies, the US has the highest at 79.0%, while Germany is the lowest at 56.9%. [^2]

## Which Tax Items Lagged Behind

Out of 18 tax items, 11 met their budget targets, while 7 fell below budget. [^2]

The largest gap was in CIT, which fell short by NT$31.9 billion (97% achievement rate). This was followed by Commodity Tax, which fell short by NT$25.5 billion (85% achievement rate). Land Value Increment Tax had the lowest achievement rate at 79% (NT$17.7 billion short); Business Tax fell short by NT$9.9 billion (98%); and Customs Duty fell short by NT$5.9 billion (96%). [^2]

On the side that exceeded targets, Securities Transaction Tax was NT$23.4 billion over (109% achievement rate), Inheritance Tax was NT$19.6 billion over (179%), Gift Tax was NT$10.2 billion over, and House Tax was NT$8.5 billion over (109%). [^2]

The 179% for Inheritance Tax is a classic example of an "opportunistic tax": whether large-scale inheritance cases are recorded in a given year can cause massive swings in the achievement rate of such a small tax item. Gift Tax follows the same logic, with NT$26.7 billion collected in 2025, which was NT$10.2 billion more than budgeted. [^2]

## Securities Transaction Tax Supported by the Stock Market

Securities Transaction Tax reached NT$292.8 billion, an increase of NT$4.7 billion (+1.6%) over 2024, and was NT$23.4 billion higher than the budget. [^1][^2]

Trading volume supported this growth. In 2025, the average daily turnover for listed and OTC stocks was NT$489.4 billion, a 2.5% increase over the NT$477.4 billion in 2024. [^1]

International economic uncertainty suppressed trading capacity in the first half of the year, but following news such as semiconductor tariff exemptions and Federal Reserve interest rate cuts, the average daily turnover from August to December exceeded NT$550 billion. [^2] In December alone, the daily average was NT$576.3 billion, a 36.0% increase over the same month last year. [^1]

Securities Transaction Tax is an opportunistic tax; when trading volume is good, tax revenue follows. This also means it is not suitable as a stable, long-term source of revenue.

## Two Negative Signs in Real Estate and Auto Markets

Taxes related to real estate and vehicles were the two most obvious gaps in 2025.

Land Value Increment Tax was NT$68.3 billion, a decrease of NT$20.9 billion (-23.5%), primarily due to tightening mortgages and continued credit controls by the Central Bank, which cooled the real estate market and reduced both transaction volumes and large-scale tax cases. [^2]

Commodity Tax was NT$144.0 billion, a decrease of NT$17.3 billion (-10.7%), with the vehicle category seeing the largest decline. Variables such as US reciprocal tariffs created a "wait-and-see" atmosphere in the auto market, leading consumers to delay purchases. [^2]

Within the same real estate sector, House Tax showed a positive sign: NT$100.5 billion, an increase of NT$7.9 billion (+8.5%), crossing the NT$100 billion threshold for the first time. This was due to the implementation of the "House Tax 2.0" system, which applies differential tax rates based on property use and adopts a nationwide unified household registration system while increasing tax rates for non-owner-occupied properties. [^2]

The House Tax crossing the NT$100 billion threshold also changed the structure of local taxes: at NT$100.5 billion, it has already surpassed Land Value Tax (NT$98.2 billion) and Land Value Increment Tax (NT$68.3 billion). [^2]

## A 13.2% Tax Burden Rate

The tax burden rate is the ratio of tax revenue to GDP and is the most commonly used indicator for international comparison of taxation levels.

Taiwan's tax burden rate in 2025 was 13.2%, a decrease of 1.4 percentage points from 14.6% in 2024. The Ministry of Finance Department of Statistics stated that this was mainly because the growth in tax revenue did not keep pace with the nominal GDP growth rate. [^2]

If social security contributions are included, the burden rate for 2024 was 20.3%, with social security contributions accounting for 5.6% of GDP. In comparison, South Korea is at 25.3%, the US at 25.6%, Japan at 33.7%, and European countries range between 34% and 45%. [^2]

Another ratio is the position of tax revenue within government revenue and expenditure: in 2025, tax revenue accounted for 85.4% of total net revenue across all levels of government, and 84.9% of total net expenditure. The proportion of expenditure fell by 2.3 percentage points, primarily because major policies such as universal cash handouts and housing rent subsidies increased the scale of expenditures. [^2]

## 8.7% Growth vs. 0.7% Tax Revenue

While the economic growth rate in 2025 was 8.7%, tax revenue only increased by 0.7%, an 8-percentage-point gap. [^2]

The Ministry of Finance Department of Statistics categorized the reasons into three types: price factors (economic growth is measured in real GDP, while tax includes both price and volume); calculation definitions (GDP is the sum of added value, whereas taxes are levied on various sources based on specific tax items); and institutional factors (lagging income tax payments, changes in tax rates, and collection measures). [^2]

In 2025, there were also two temporary factors: the implementation of installment payments to respond to US reciprocal tariffs, and sluggish transactions in the real estate and auto markets. [^2]

The years 2021 and 2022 serve as counterexamples: in those two years, actual collections exceeded budget estimates by over NT$400 billion and NT$500 billion respectively, driven by the replenishment of provisional CIT payments and profit growth in listed companies. [^2]

Looking at a longer timeframe, in 2024, total national tax revenue exceeded the budget by NT$528.3 billion, the highest in history; in 2025, it turned into a shortfall of NT$14.3 billion. This reversal within a single year demonstrates how much "surplus" and "shortfall" depend on the economic climate and the base year. [^2]

## How to Read Surpluses and Shortfalls

Budget figures are estimates made in the previous year, not the total amount owed.

Therefore, a "surplus" does not mean the government collected extra money from the people, nor does a "shortfall" mean the government needs to raise taxes. They are simply the difference between actual net collections and budget estimates, reflecting the distance between estimation and reality. [^1][^2]

What is truly worth observing are the structure and achievement rates: income tax accounting for 53.4% means the tax base is concentrated in corporate profits and wages; business tax at 16.3% follows consumption and exports; and securities transaction tax at 7.7% follows stock market turnover.

The next specific point of observation is whether CIT and Business Tax can return to their budgeted levels. In 2025, these two taxes combined for a shortfall of NT$41.8 billion, which was the primary source of the gap. [^2]
