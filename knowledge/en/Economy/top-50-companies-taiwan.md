> **30-Second Overview:** After trading on May 19, 2026, TSMC's market capitalization reached NT$57.18 trillion. This single company accounts for 31.51% of the Taiwan Weighted Index, approximately 40% of the total Taiwan stock market capitalization, and 71% of the global foundry market. The remaining 49 positions are divided among 12 financial holding companies, 11 semiconductor firms, 6 electronic contract manufacturers, and 5 traditional industry veterans. While there is a so-called "tripartite stability" between electronics, finance, and traditional industries, in reality, the entire body is propped up by a single leg: the electronics industry. Since 2024, the AI wave has propelled companies like Quanta, Wistron, Wiwynn, and Hongjing from the middle tier to the top, further increasing market concentration. Reading this Top 50 table is equivalent to reading an anatomical map of Taiwan's economy: where the spine lies, where the single points of failure are, and where the internal organs hidden outside the table reside.

On the evening of May 28, 2024, five people sat at plastic tables and chairs in Taipei's Ningxia Night Market. Jensen Huang, Morris Chang, C.C. Wei, Bruce Lee, and Archie Chao—some of the most important names from the semiconductor and construction industries—were eating at roadside stalls and drinking beer that night[^1]. The following day, COMPUTEX 2024 opened at the NTU Sports Center. On Jensen Huang's projection screen was a map of Taiwan, marked with the names of TSMC, Foxconn, Quanta, and 16 universities, before an audience of five thousand people[^2]. After he uttered the words, "Taiwan is an unsung hero, yet the pillar of the world," the stocks of all the companies named on that map rose at the market opening the day after his speech.

The names on that map are almost a microcosm of Taiwan's Top 50 corporate rankings. A single slide pinned a nation's economic structure to a screen[^3].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Reading this Top 50 table is like reading a map. As you read from top to bottom, the number of seats held by semiconductors, electronic contract manufacturing, finance, traditional industries, and telecommunications reflects the industrial skeleton of this island of Taiwan. Readers can discern three things from this skeleton: which bone is the spine, where the soft underbelly lies, and whether the heart beats with regularity.

## Pinning a Nation onto a Map

The after-hours rankings on May 19, 2026, look like this[^4]: first place is TSMC with a market capitalization of NT$57.18 trillion ([2330](/en/economy/tsmc)); second is MediaTek at NT$5.06 trillion (2454); third is Delta Electronics at NT$4.97 trillion ([2308](/en/economy/delta-electronics-taiwan-power-giant)); fourth is Hon Hai at NT$3.43 trillion ([2317](/en/economy/foxconn-precision-industry)); and fifth is ASE Technology Holding at NT$2.11 trillion (3711). These five companies alone have a combined market cap of NT$7.2 trillion, accounting for more than half of the total Taiwan stock market capitalization.

From sixth to thirtieth place, three distinct layers can be observed. The semiconductor camp includes UMC ([2303](/en/economy/united-microelectronics-corporation)), MediaTek (3443), Winbond (2344), Nanya Technology (2408), and Chicilon (2360). The financial camp includes Fubon Financial ([2881](/en/economy/taiwan-enterprise-fubon-financial)), Cathay Financial ([2882](/en/economy/taiwan-enterprise-cathay-financial)), CTBC Financial, Yuanta Financial, Mega Financial, Taishin Shin Kong Financial, and E.SUN Financial ([2884](/en/economy/esun-financial-holding-digital-banking-pioneer)). The electronic manufacturing and AI peripherals group includes Quanta ([2382](/en/economy/taiwanese-companies-quanta-computer)), Accton (2345), Wiwynn (6669), Auras (3017), Hong Jing (7769), Unimicron (3037), Formosa Microelectronic (2383), and Yageo (2327). Traditional industries and telecommunications are left with only Chunghwa Telecom (2412) and Formosa Plastics ([1303](/en/economy/formosa-plastics-group)) squeezing into the top thirty[^5].

The positions from 31st to 50th are even more crowded. Old veterans that sustained the economic miracle of the 1970s and 80s, such as China Steel ([2002](/en/economy/taiwan-companies-china-steel)), Formosa Plastics Group (6505), Evergreen Marine ([2603](/en/economy/evergreen-marine)), and Uni-President ([1216](/en/economy/taiwan-enterprise-uni-president)), hold onto the latter part of the list. Electronic manufacturers and financial holdings like Wistron ([3231](/en/economy/wistron-global-manufacturing-transformation-pioneer)), ASUS ([2357](/en/economy/asus-computer)), Lite-On, Advantech (2395), and Alchip (3661) rotate through these positions; their market caps all sit in the NT$400–500 billion range, with rankings shifting by just a single percentage point[^6].

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> The investment community has long used the mnemonic "Electronics, Finance, and Traditional Industries" to describe the three-legged tripod of the Taiwan stock market. However, looking at the Top 50 in 2026, Electronics and Semiconductors already account for 36% of the seats, Finance takes 25%, and Traditional Industries have been squeezed down to 10%. This "tripod" is actually one leg supporting half the body, another leg (Finance) supporting a quarter, while the remaining toes are divided among traditional industries, communications, and passive components.

## The Two Sides of the "Protector Mountain"

Focus the lens on number one: TSMC has a market capitalization of NT$57.18 trillion, accounting for 31.51% of the entire weighted index[^7]. Think of the Taiwan Stock Exchange as a scale: if you place TSMC in one basket and all the remaining 1,000+ companies combined in the other, neither side will tip over.

Zoom out one level further: it accounts for approximately 42% of the total market capitalization of the Taiwan stock market[^8]. It holds a 71% share of the global foundry market (as of Q3 2025) [^9]. In advanced processes (below 10nm), TSMC and Samsung together monopolize the world; however, Samsung holds only 6.8% while TSMC holds 71%, a tenfold difference. The positions for 5nm, 3nm, and 2nm advanced processes exist only on one particular street—that street is Baoshan, Hsinchu.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The metaphor of the "Protector Mountain" (_hùguó shénshān_) can be read in reverse. TSMC is a mountain because it is high enough, heavy enough, and indispensable enough; but it is also a mountain situated on a fault line, with all the Taiwanese people whose lives depend on this supply chain standing in its shadow. The height of the mountain and its fragility share the same geology on this island.

On January 16, 2025, C.C. Wei's speech topic at the NTU EMBA was titled "If There Were No TSMC" [^10]. He shared a case study: after the Arizona plant began mass production, they could not find sulfuric acid of the chemical grade required for IC manufacturing locally in the United States. Consequently, it was cheaper to ship sulfuric acid from Taiwan to the Port of Los Angeles and then truck it to Phoenix than to buy it in the U.S. He mentioned more than once, "Setting up a factory in the U.S. is a process of tears and snot." TSMC's entire supply chain is not portable; what moves to Arizona are the equipment and processes, but what cannot be moved is the "micro-universe" ranging from chemicals to photomasks to EUV maintenance—that micro-universe remains in Taiwan.

The other side of this concentration is taxes and exports. The semiconductor industry accounts for 34.7% of Taiwan's total exports (according to 2024 Ministry of Finance data) [^11], and the top 50 companies combined account for 51.5% of total exports (2023 data) [^12]. A single company, TSMC, accounts for 8.1% of the actual net corporate income tax revenue (as disclosed by the company in 2022) [^13]. These figures are often simplified and circulated on social media as "TSMC accounts for 7% of GDP, 15% of tax revenue, and 36% of exports." Of these, only the 7% GDP figure is close to reality (estimated at 7.94% added value in 2022); the 15% tax revenue figure has never been found in an official source, and the 36% export figure mistakenly writes "the semiconductor industry" as "TSMC alone." Readers should remember to parse these numbers carefully next time they see them.

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**
> The "Protector Mountain effect" is also a kind of curse. Academia generally refers to this structure as the _silicon shield_, meaning that because Taiwan's semiconductors are important to the entire world, the cost for China to take action increases and the motivation for the U.S. to protect Taiwan strengthens[^14]. However, the opposing view argues: entrusting national security to a single company, a single supply chain, or a single industry is itself a structural weakness. If TSMC's competitiveness declines, or if the U.S. CHIPS Act subsidies eventually cause the supply chain to disperse to Arizona and Kumamoto, will the shield grow thinner? This is one of the sharpest debates facing Taiwan over the next decade.

## The AI Wave Has Doubled the Length of Electronics Legs

Since the moment ChatGPT ignited the demand for AI computing power in 2022, the reshuffling speed among Taiwan's top 50 companies has begun to accelerate.

Wistron (3231) saw its stock price rise from NT$23.1 in 2022 to a high of NT$161.5 in 2025, a sixfold increase in three years[^15]. Quanta Computer (2382) rose from NT$66.2 to NT$345, a 4.2-fold increase in three years. Wistron's spun-off subsidiary focused on hyperscaler cloud servers, Wiwynn (6669), recorded a full-year 2025 EPS of NT$275.06, up 117% year-on-year, with its market capitalization surging into the top 20[^16]. Quanta's full-year 2025 revenue reached NT$2.12 trillion, and its Q1 2026 revenue hit a new high of NT$809.2 billion, with March monthly revenue reaching NT$362.8 billion, breaking the NT$300 billion mark for the first time[^17].

It is not just contract manufacturers being pulled up by the AI wave. The "three giants" of PCB/ABF substrates—Unimicron (3037), Taiwan PCB (2383), and Nan Ya PCB (8046)—thermal management leader AVC (3017), testing interface provider Hongjing (7769), ASIC design services providers Alchip (3443) and \_\_\_\_(3661) [Note: Source text says 世芯-KY], network equipment provider Accton (2345); most of these companies were hovering around the 30th to 50th rank before 2022, but collectively squeezed into the top 20 in 2025. As one layer of an industry moves, the entire supply chain is pulled up by NVIDIA's GB200 and GB300 orders.

On May 19, 2025, the opening day of COMPUTEX, Jensen Huang took 36 Taiwanese tech executives to a banquet at Taipei Zhaoyuan[^18]. The combined market capitalization of the people at that table exceeded NT$100 trillion.

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> Every time Jensen Huang visits Taiwan, he visits a night market. In 2024, he ate at a roadside stall in Ningxia Night Market with Morris Chang. On January 17, 2025, he originally intended to go to Feng Chia Night Market in Taichung, but because the crowds were too large, his bodyguards called for a halt, and Jensen Huang left 10 minutes later[^19]. The CEO of one of the world's top ten companies by market cap could not last 10 minutes in Taiwan's most famous night markets—this image illustrates "why Taiwan is important to AI computing power" more concretely than any GDP figure.

The AI wave has stretched the "electronics legs" longer while also increasing concentration. The combined market capitalization of the top five electronics-related companies (TSMC, MediaTek, Delta Electronics, Hon Hai, and ASE Group) accounts for over 50% of the total Taiwan stock market capitalization. The share of semiconductors and electronics manufacturing has risen from approximately 30% in 2020 to 36% in 2026. The proportion of finance and traditional industries has been relatively squeezed, even though their absolute market capitalizations have also grown.

## The Seven Great Families and a Succession Map

The ownership structures behind the Top 50 are another map entirely.

The Wang family of Formosa Plastics is the first established empire. After Wang Yung-ching passed away in 2008, Wang Wen-yuan took the helm as president for 17 years before stepping down in August 2025[^20]. His successor, Wu Chia-chao, is the **first non-Wang family president** in the history of the Formosa Plastics Group, marking the group's entry into the era of professional management. In December 2025, Wang Yung-ching's youngest son, Wang Wen-hsiang, filed another lawsuit in a U.S. court requesting that an overseas trust worth NT$600 billion be changed to joint management by 17 people—a dispute that has spanned oceans for 17 years.

The Guo family of Hon Hai is the second case. Terry Gou publicly promised in 2016 that he would "only retire when Hon Hai's share price hits NT$200"; in June 2024, he reached that milestone and fulfilled his word[^21]. Young Liu has served as chairman for six years; at the 2025 shareholders' meeting, Terry Gou abstained from voting on 1.74 million shares, yet Young Liu was still elected, signaling Hon Hai's entry into a practical "post-Guo era."

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> For Taiwanese family businesses, the term "generational handover" is usually accompanied by keywords like "number of children," "sibling division," "will litigation," and "overseas trusts." The Chang family of Evergreen is the most acute case. After Chang Yung-fa passed away in 2016, he left a handwritten will leaving sole inheritance to his second family's son, Chang Kuo-wei; in August 2024, the Supreme Court's ruling took effect, granting the second family sole inheritance of NT$14 billion plus overseas assets[^22]. In September of the same year, Chang Kuo-wei filed a countersuit against his elder brother for breach of trust. One will, two branches of the family, eight years of litigation, and the distribution of overseas assets—this represents one of the most concrete instances of internal liquidation in Taiwan's capitalism.

The Tsai family of Fubon and the Tsai family of Cathay both originate from Tsai Wan-chun (1906–1986), with two branches of brothers splitting to support two major financial holding companies. Fubon is led by the elder branch under Tsai Ming-chung (who also heads Taiwan Mobile [3045]), while Tsai Ming-hsing manages Fubon Financial. Cathay is governed by the third generation of the lineage descending from Tsai Wan-lin. The Ku family of CTBC underwent a "peaceful separation" in 2001: Ku Chung-liang took CTBC Financial, Ku Chung-ying took KGI (formerly Kaohsiung Development Bank), and Ku Chung-li took CTBC Leasing[^23]. In 2025, Ku Chung-liang's investment in Taiwan Molybdenum was interpreted as the "reunion of the century-old Ku family," as the business empires of the three brothers intersected again after twenty years. The Hsu family of Far Eastern was founded by Hsu Yu-hsiang and is currently helmed by Hsu Hsueh-tung, linking Far EasTone (4904), Asia Cement (1102), and Far Eastern New Century (1402); the third generation, Hsu Kuo-an, took over as Vice Chairman of Yumin in August 2023[^24].

While the proportion of companies with no family control is not high, they are all top-tier: TSMC (Morris Chang retired in 2018; C.C. Wei and魏哲家 [Wei Zhe-jia] served as co-heads; after June 2024, Wei Zhe-jia serves as sole chairman), MediaTek (founded by Cai Ming-jie but with no concentrated ownership and a professionalized board), and Delta Electronics (Cheng Chong-hua retired in 2012; Hung Ying-chun took over for 12 years; in May 2024, Cheng Ping succeeded him—viewed by outsiders as a textbook case of "handing over to a professional manager for 12 years before passing it to his son")[^25]. Chunghwa Telecom (2412) transitioned from the Directorate General of Telecommunications under the Ministry of Transportation and Communications prior to its privatization in 1996; its chairman is appointed by the Ministry's directors and supervisors, representing state-owned interests.

> **⚠️ Controversial Perspective**
> "Family concentration" is a double-edged sword in Taiwan's capitalism. On one hand, long-term family ownership allows companies to make 10–20 year long-term investments (in capital-intensive industries like semiconductors and shipbuilding) without being disturbed by short-term shareholder pressure. On the other hand, when the second or third generations take over, two axes are simultaneously scrutinized: "socialization of equity" and "transparency of governance." The first non-Wang president of Formosa Plastics, Terry Gou's abstention at Hon Hai, the sole inheritance of Evergreen's second family, and Delta Electronics' 12-year professional manager buffer—all four events share a single structural pressure: Taiwan's capital market is entering a hybrid era where "family and institutions coexist," and this process is far from over.

## The Invisible Other Half of the Top 50

Rankings only account for publicly traded companies. Looking at the top 50 is like looking at 70% of Taiwan's economy, but there is another 30% that remains invisible.

Since its founding in 1934, _I-Mei Foods_ has **never gone public**. The four Kao brothers have managed the business across three generations, with estimated revenues exceeding NT$10 billion, but because they do not issue stock, it does not appear on any ranking[^26]. Hotai Motor (2207) ranks around 51st with a market capitalization of NT$264 billion; whether it enters the top 50 depends on the daily stock price, but the fact that Toyota's agency business in Taiwan has seen stable profits for ten years will not change regardless of its ranking. Shin Kong Life Insurance only entered the top 30 after merging into Taishin Financial Holdings to become Taishin Shin Kong Financial (2887) at the end of 2025; before that, for an entire century, it was the private domain of the Wu Tung-chin family[^27].

The main entity of Ding Shin International is listed in China (Master Kong), while the Wei family's business operations in Taiwan are unlisted. Prince Construction, Chang Chun Petrochemical (Kaohsiung), and most other century-old establishments are not among the top 50. Chang Chun Petrochemical is Taiwan's largest private chemical enterprise; however, the party responsible for the 2014 Kaohsiung gas explosion was **Lee Chang-jung Chemical** (please note that the names Lee Chang-jung Chemical and Chang Chun Petrochemical are often confused) [^28].

> **💡 Did you know?**
> Publicly traded companies have disclosure obligations, so they appear on rankings, their market caps can be calculated, and their boards include independent directors. Unlisted companies are not required to disclose information, so can you compare the scale of I-Mei with Uni-President? The answer is that they cannot be compared directly because their financial statement structures differ. However, if discussing employee headcount, influence on Taiwanese household diets, or local employment, I-Mei has distributors and factories in every county and city, making it an invisible giant on the same level as the food companies within the top 50.

## PM2.5 on Kaixuan 3rd Road

While the Top 50 companies prop up a GDP, they also leave other accounts to be settled on this island of Taiwan.

From late night on July 31, 2014, to the early hours of August 1, Kaixuan 3rd Road in the Qianzhen District of Kaohsiung was blasted into a trench[^29]. The incident resulted in 32 deaths and 321 injuries. The accident originated from a 4-inch propylene pipeline leaking due to corrosion inside and outside a drainage culvert. **This pipeline belonged to Lee Chang Yung Chemical**; the 12 defendants included executives from Lee Chang Yung Chemical, Huayun Warehousing, and the Kaohsiung City Government Public Works Bureau. By the time the verdict took effect, eight years had passed since the gas explosion. That section of Kaixuan 3rd Road was later repaved, making it difficult to see any traces of the disaster on the surface. But for residents living in the Qianzhen District, the reality that "the petrochemical industry is right at our back door" has never faded since that night.

Formosa Plastics Group's Sixth Naphtha Cracker in Mailiao, Yunlin, has been operating since 1998. A 2012 study by Professor Chan Chang-chuan of National Taiwan University's Department of Public Health pointed out that the concentration of heavy metal metabolites in the urine of residents within 10 km was 5.5 times higher than that of residents living more than 10 km away; the Sixth Naphtha Cracker plant emits 132 types of toxic and carcinogenic substances, with annual PM2.5 emissions reaching the level of 140,000 tons[^30]. In 2015, 75 Yunlin residents filed a joint lawsuit seeking NT$70 million in compensation, but the Yunlin District Court dismissed it due to the "difficulty of establishing a causal relationship." From January to September of that same year, Yunlin was the county with the dirtiest air in Taiwan.

China Steel in Siaogang, Kaohsiung, also carries a heavy bill. Data from the Earth Citizen Foundation indicates that 80–90% of air pollution from the steel industry in Kaohsiung originates from China Steel; within Kaohsiung's fixed pollution sources, the steel industry accounts for 36% of PM emissions and 32% of SOx emissions[^31]. Between 2017 and 2020, China Steel invested NT$4.756 billion to improve equipment, reducing PM by 15.6%, SOx by 18.9%, and NOx by 11.3%. While the numbers have improved, the structural reality that "the steel industry as a whole impacts Kaohsiung's air quality" remains unchanged.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The other side of the Top 50 is 50 sets of external cost ledgers. What is included in the GDP is the output value of the factories; what is not included are the medical expenses of households surrounding the factories, the long-term care burden faced by Yunlin residents, and the relocation costs for Qianzhen residents in Kaohsiung. Economics calls this "internalizing externalities": when society forces companies to shoulder the costs they previously spilled onto others, that is the true meaning of ESG. Among Taiwan's Top 50, the semiconductor and financial holding sectors generally lead in ESG ratings; traditional manufacturing and petrochemical industries, meanwhile, are the ones under scrutiny. This reshuffling of scores will change rankings more profoundly over the next decade than any single quarterly earnings report.

## Ningxia Night Market and Kaixuan 3rd Road

Connecting the beginning to the end reveals two extremes within the same coordinate system.

On that night at Ningxia Night Market, five of the most important people in semiconductors were drinking beer on plastic tables and chairs; by the next day, the market capitalization of three of their companies had increased by hundreds of billions overnight. On that night on Kaixuan 3rd Road, 32 residents of Qianzhen were blown out of their homes by an explosion; after eight years of trials and sentencing, no one can bring their lives back.

Between these two images lies the entire span of this "Top 50" list.

The "Protector of the Nation" (TSMC) sits at the very top, propping up the entire industry and the entire country; at the foot of that mountain lies the PM2.5 of Mailiao, the smell of propylene in Qianzhen, and the influence of the I-Mei factory and Hotai showrooms that aren't written into the rankings. These 50 companies combined form the spine of Taiwan; a single company is like the thickest lumbar vertebra within that spine, but beyond the spine, there are muscles, nerves, and skin that GDP fails to grasp.

After reading this list, what readers should take away is not "who is ranked where" or "whose market cap is how much." It should be an anatomical diagram: where the spine is, where the single points of failure lie, where the external costs are, and where the internal organs hidden outside the table reside. When the next AI wave washes through, when the next round of US-China geopolitics shifts, or when some company challenges TSMC's position in a specific process, this structural diagram will be redrawn. The names in those 50 positions will change, decrease, or increase, but this list will always be recording one thing: which companies decide how this generation of Taiwanese people eats, works, and worries about tomorrow.

> **✦** "Semiconductors have placed Taiwan at the center of the world. But what is not written on this Top 50 list are the internal organs and skin of this island; they are also a part of Taiwan, they are just invisible to GDP."
