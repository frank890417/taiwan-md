# Zhongli: Another City Within Taoyuan — Why Some Say Zhongli Isn't Taoyuan?

> **30-second overview:** Administratively part of Taoyuan City, Zhongli often functions as the urban core of southern Taoyuan. This seemingly contradictory claim stems from layers of long-accumulated differences: Zhongli had already formed a relatively stable administrative boundary in the 1920s, was once a county-administered city of Taoyuan County with its own station, commercial district, schools, and municipal memory. Located in the middle reaches of the Taoyuan Tableland, it connects Pingzhen, Longtan, Yangmei, Xinwu, and Guanyin, forming a south–north dual-core with Taoyuan District. Thus, saying "I'm from Zhongli, not Taoyuan" is often not a rejection of the Taoyuan municipality but a way to avoid conflating Zhongli with Taoyuan District.

## First, let's clarify: "Taoyuan" has two meanings

"Taoyuan" has two common meanings in daily conversation. The first is Taoyuan City, upgraded to a municipality in 2014, comprising Taoyuan District, Zhongli District, Pingzhen District, Bade District, Dasi District, Yangmei District, Luzhu District, Dayuan District, Guishan District, Longtan District, Xinwu District, Guanyin District, and Xingfeng District. The second is Taoyuan District within Taoyuan City — the district where the city government is located and what many refer to as "Taoyuan city center."

So when a Zhongli resident says "I'm from Zhongli, not Taoyuan," they may be answering the second meaning, not denying that they live in Taoyuan City. This statement sounds like an administrative contradiction to outsiders, but is often a matter of geographical identification within Taoyuan. Local writers' records of "I'm from Zhongli" and "Zhongli isn't Taoyuan" are examples of this living language context, though that is personal observation, not a statistical conclusion of all residents.[^1]

To understand this distinction, one cannot look only at today's administrative map. Zhongli and Taoyuan District have long each been capable of providing work, education, shopping, faith, and transportation. Both belong to Taoyuan City, yet need not share the same daily center.

## Zhongli's geographical outline: a city in the middle of the tableland

Zhongli District covers approximately 76.253 square kilometers, with an east-west span of about 10.64 kilometers and a north-south span of about 7.38 kilometers. The Zhongli District Office points out that Zhongli's administrative boundaries are winding, totaling about 56 kilometers in length, covering roughly 6.26% of Taoyuan City's area.[^2]

Zhongli lies in the middle reaches of the Taoyuan Tableland, with the Laojie Creek and Xinjie Creek flowing through it. This location makes it neither simply a northern commuter residential area nor merely an agricultural settlement on the tableland. To the north it connects to Taoyuan District and Dayuan; to the south it links to Pingzhen, Yangmei, and the Hsinchu living circle; to the west it reaches Xinwu, Guanyin, and the coastal areas. Zhongli's ease of forming its own urban sense is related to its orientation toward multiple directions simultaneously.

The Taoyuan City Government's English district introduction divides Zhongli into four geographical areas: Zhongyang, Neili, Longgang, and Dacun.[^3] This is not a strict administrative division name but a convenient way to understand local life. Zhongyang centers on the station, Zhongping Road, Zhongzheng Road, Laojie Creek, and the traditional commercial street. Neili is near the railway and industrial zones. Longgang connects diverse communities, military settlements, and campuses. Dacun retains more agricultural landscapes and western settlement characteristics.

The geographical scale of Zhongli cannot be understood by area alone. The smallest Zhongli Village, Zhongjian Village, and Puyi Village are located in the old business district, each under 0.05 square kilometers. In contrast, Shandong Village, Zhiba Village, and Yuegui Village are in the northwestern rural areas, each over 5 square kilometers. The Zhongli District Office uses this contrast to explain that within the same administrative district, both high-density old city and large rural areas coexist.[^2]

## From Jiaozuozu to Zhongli District: the administrative memory behind the name

Zhongli's early place names were related to Jiaozuozu. The Zhongli District Office's administrative history records state that after Han Chinese immigrants moved in and developed the area during the Qing Dynasty, scattered villages and settlements formed. Later, due to the development of market towns, the name was changed to Zhongli Street. During the Japanese colonial period, administrative systems changed, and the Zhongli area first became Zhongli Town in Zhongli County of Hsinchu Prefecture, then in 1931 was upgraded to Zhongli Street.

After 1945, the administrative name changed to Zhongli Town, Hsinchu County. In 1950, the administrative region was reorganized, and Hsinchu County was divided into Taoyuan, Zhu, and Miao counties, making Zhongli a town directly under Taoyuan County Government. In 1967, due to traffic location and industrial and commercial development, Zhongli was upgraded to a county-administered city. In 2014, when Taoyuan County was upgraded to a municipality, Zhongli City was changed to Taoyuan City's Zhongli District.[^4]

Therefore, Zhongli's "city center" and "Zhongli District" are not the same thing. The former refers to the old city center of the station, commercial street, and historical buildings. The latter also includes Neili, Longgang, Dacun, Qingpu, and the northwestern rural areas. A complete introduction to Zhongli must place these nearby but differently paced places on the same urban map.

This historical evolution explains why the sense of place attached to the name "Zhongli" is so strong. Zhongli was once a town, then a county-administered city, with its own municipal office, urban construction, and city image. It was not a peripheral street of Taoyuan District but another city center officially recognized by the administrative system. After being reorganized as Zhongli District, the administrative level changed, but the urban memory did not disappear.

## Taoyuan and Zhongli have always been two cores

The Taoyuan City Government's regional introduction describes Zhongli as an important city in Taoyuan City, introducing it as the southern Taoyuan business center, educational center, and transportation hub.[^3] The Zhongli District Office also directly states that although Zhongli is not where Taoyuan City Government is located, due to its central location, industry, and population scale, it has long been on par with Taoyuan District.[^2]

The "dual-core" here does not mean Taoyuan City has two city governments, but that residents' daily activities need not all converge toward the same direction. Taoyuan District has the city government and the administrative and commercial resources of northern Taoyuan. Zhongli serves the living circle of southern Taoyuan and the middle reaches of the Taoyuan Tableland, forming its own station commercial district, school network, medical services, traditional markets, and industrial employment areas.

The differences between the two places are therefore not as clear as county and municipal boundaries, but more like two overlapping magnetic fields. Some people live in Pingzhen and work or study in Zhongli. Some people live in Dayuan and commute between the high-speed rail and Qingpu. Some people live in Taoyuan District but often go to Zhongli for shopping, medical treatment, or visiting relatives and friends. Being the same Taoyuan City administratively does not eliminate the multi-centered living circle.

## Station, Qingpu, and Laojie Creek: three different entrances to Zhongli

The first entrance to Zhongli is the Taiwan Railway Administration's Zhongli Station. Around the station, Zhongping Road, Zhongzheng Road, and the traditional market form the old city center, with shop density, dining options, and pedestrian flow still maintaining a different street rhythm from the newly planned areas. This city is not a straight boulevard but a patchwork of arcades, alleys, temple fairs, cram schools, rice shops, and small businesses.

The second entrance is Neili. Neili connects with the railway, industrial zones, and residential living circles. Residents' commuting directions may be Taoyuan, Taipei, Zhongli, or Hsinchu. It makes Zhongli not just a traditional city center but also a city that develops together with industrial employment, technical labor, and family housing. Bus 208 connects the high-speed rail, Neili Industrial Zone, Neili Station, and Yuanzhu University, making Neili an indispensable middle section in Zhongli's multi-core transportation.[^14]

Huaxun is another residential living circle. It is not centered on high-speed rail or station tourism but composed of markets, parks, schools, medical care, and community roads for daily services. Including Huaxun in the introduction of Zhongli can avoid representing the whole of Zhongli only by station, Qingpu, and university. The completeness of a city often hides in life nodes that won't appear in tourism promotion titles.

The third entrance is Qingpu. Taoyuan High-Speed Rail Station is located in Qingpu, Zhongli District, and is officially introduced as an important transportation hub connecting Taoyuan International Airport with other cities.[^3] Qingpu's high-speed rail, airport MRT, shopping malls, performances, and large residential developments present the scale of a newly developed city. Zhongli Station retains the walking density of the old city street. Both belong to Zhongli, yet they resemble entrances to cities from different eras.

These nodes are not isolated from each other. The 208 bus route in the Taoyuan City Government's transportation data runs from Taoyuan High-Speed Rail Station through Da Jiang Shopping Center, Wanan University of Science and Technology, Neili Industrial Zone, Neili Station, Yuanzhu University, and Huaxun to Bade.[^14] The significance of this route is not only in "convenient transfers" but also in placing high-speed rail passengers, industrial labor, student commuting, and residential life within the same public transportation context. Zhongli's urban scope is therefore not a box on the map but a city stitched together by people moving every day.

Laojie Creek is the fourth entrance and the place that best explains how Zhongli handles urban issues. Laojie Creek got its name from flowing through Zhongli Old Street. Its main stream is about 36.7 kilometers long. In 1989, the urban section of the creek was covered and a shopping mall was built. Later, due to low utilization, flood control, and dike problems, it was demolished. The opening project was completed in 2011, releasing waterfront space and barrier-free walkways.[^5]

The Laojie Creek Riverside Education Center was rebuilt from two Hakka historic houses. The Ministry of Environment's certification data indicates that it is the first environmental education center in the country themed around a river, and it has established a collaborative network with the water environment patrol team.[^6] Here, Zhongli's river management, Hakka settlements, environmental education, and public leisure are brought together, reminding people that Zhongli is not just about streets and transportation but also about water environments that require long-term maintenance.

## Zhongli's old city is not just about the station

The streets in front of Zhongli Station are not only about modern commerce. Yuanzhu University's field survey records show that the Zhongping Road Story House is a preserved Japanese-style dual-linked building that was once used as a government official's residence. The Yishitang in Zhongzheng Park was built in the Qing Dynasty and commemorates the deceased in the Minnan-Yue conflict. Yuanhua Court is hidden in a traffic-heavy block. Renhai Temple was built in the Qing Dynasty and is locally known as "Zhongli Mama." The Sacred Inscription Pavilion next to Xin Street Elementary School preserves the ancient belief objects of respecting calligraphy paper.[^20]

The value of these places does not lie in each becoming a check-in spot but in how they divide Zhongli's history into several different memories. Yishitang preserves the memory of conflict and reconciliation. Renhai Temple represents a generational belief network. The Zhongping Road Story House presents the Japanese colonial urban architecture. The Sacred Inscription Pavilion connects education, writing, and belief. Walking through this small section of the old city, one discovers that Zhongli's history is not a straight line from ancient to modern but many eras piled up in the same block.

## Xinjie Creek, Art Park, and Central Park

If Laojie Creek represents how Zhongli returns the covered river to the city, Xinjie Creek presents another waterfront route. The Hakka Small Journey data indicates that Xinjie Creek and Laojie Creek are the two major rivers in Zhongli. Xinjie Creek plans about 3 kilometers of waterfront green belt and sets up about 1.5 kilometers of riverside walkway. The Zhongfu Section has the Zhongli Art Park and the cherry blossom path. The Xizhou Section connects Renhai Temple, the Sacred Inscription Pavilion, and the waterfront walking route.[^18]

Central Park is located near Zhongli Station and forms part of the daily life of the old city with cram schools, clothing streets, and restaurants. The Taoyuan City Government News Office records that after the renovation of Central Park, it is divided into the Central Stage Area, the Moonlight Circular Corridor, and the Installation Art Area. The renovation also addressed the issue of underground parking leakage caused by park ponding.[^19] It is not a park like Qingpu with large-scale open space and new architecture but one that reopens the originally closed corners of the old city.

Looking at the Zhongli Art Park and Central Park together, one can see two directions of Zhongli's public space. One follows the river, connecting walking, exercise, and ecology into a green belt. The other is embedded in the old city, using stages, corridors, and installation art to allow places that were originally just passing through to stop. Zhongli's urban renewal is not only about large-scale construction but also about how to let residents stay a little longer in familiar blocks.

## The density of Zhongli's campuses and how it changes the city's character

The Taoyuan City Government's English introduction lists multiple universities and colleges in Zhongli, including National Central University, Chung Yuan University, Yuanzhu University, Wanan University of Science and Technology, and Jianxing University of Science and Technology.[^3] Different official pages may have slightly different school names and statistical times, so this article only treats them as evidence of concentrated educational resources in Zhongli, not as a fixed and final statistical list.

The impact of campus density is not only about more students. Universities attract teachers, researchers, service industries, rental populations, and families from other places. Zhongli therefore has stronger tutoring, dining, rental, printing, performance, and night consumption needs than ordinary traditional towns. The Qingpu and Shuanglian Slope around National Central University, and the Zhongyuan commercial district around Chung Yuan University, each form different campus life nodes.

Educational resources also change residents' imagination of the city. School choices are often influenced by commuting time and living circles. A study by National Taiwan Normal University on the north–south regionalization of Taoyuan takes the student source of Taoyuan High School and Zhongda Affiliated Middle School as an example, pointing out that the geographical location and traffic network of the two schools make students tend to choose schools according to the north–south living circle.[^7]

This is also one of the reasons why some people regard Zhongli as different from Taoyuan District. When students, parents, and teachers have a set of educational networks centered on Zhongli, the city they come into contact with every day does not have to pass through Taoyuan District. Local identity is often not a declaration but the route one walks every day.

## Hakka, Minnan, and more mobile Zhongli

Zhongli cannot be simplified as a "Hakka city," but Hakka history is indeed an important clue to understanding southern Taoyuan. Both the Zhongli District Office and the Taoyuan City Government data mention that Hakka immigrants from Guangdong developed the Zhongli area during the Qing Dynasty.[^3] The Hakka faith, settlements, food, and language memory around Zhongli still remain part of the local culture.

On the other hand, Zhongli is also a city with a high concentration of mobile populations. Stations, industrial zones, universities, military settlements, and new residential areas make Minnan, Hakka, mainland Chinese, indigenous, new immigrants, and foreign workers meet in the same city center. The Yunnan-Burma food and immigrant memory in Longgang are often seen as representatives of Zhongli's diversified culture, but they should not be packaged as a single tourism label. These communities are first of all living people, not just features for tourists to take photos.

A study on the north–south Taoyuan points out that the immigrant distribution, Hakka-Minnan relationship, religious references, and settlement history since the Qing Dynasty together form the "northern Minnan-Hakka" regional phenomenon. The study also reminds that this is a structural analysis of population and local history, not equivalent to each person's ethnic identity today.[^7]

Therefore, saying Zhongli is "more Hakka" can be a general summary of historical structure, but it cannot be used to label all residents. The true face of Zhongli is closer to a city with deep Hakka history that continuously absorbs immigrants.

## What Zhongli eats: not a list of specialties, but the intersection of living circles

Zhongli's food memory usually starts from the station, Zhongyang West Road, Zhongping Road, Zhongxiao Road, Longgang, and the night market. Beef noodles, rice noodles, rice vermicelli, Hakka snacks, braised snacks, meal boxes, breakfast shops, and Southeast Asian cuisine appear side by side, reflecting the population and transportation nodes rather than just local specialty competitions.

The reason Zhongli beef noodles are often mentioned is related to the station commercial district, mainland Chinese food memory, immigrants, and urban consumption. The Longgang rice noodles and rice vermicelli are connected to the Yunnan-Burma immigrant community and their descendants' food heritage. The two cannot be mixed into the same "Zhongli flavor," nor can it be assumed that all stores follow the same traditional story. A more reliable way to introduce is to put the stores, communities, and blocks back into their respective historical contexts.

When introducing the Laojie Creek Riverside Education Center on the Taoyuan Tourism Guide website, it also places the fruit street, Zhongli Market, beef noodles, and Zhongli's oldest elementary school in the display content of Zhongli's local stories.[^8] This arrangement is interesting because it does not separate the river and food. Markets, food, schools, and waterfront together constitute the way residents know Zhongli.

To fully introduce Zhongli, one cannot simply list three famous stores. More importantly, it is to see how people eat breakfast near the station, look for rice noodles in Longgang, dine around the campus, buy meal boxes after night shifts in the industrial zone, and then walk back along the Laojie Creek path. Food is the timetable of local life.

## The first reason "Zhongli is not Taoyuan": administrative names differ from daily place names

The most direct reason for this saying is that "Taoyuan City" and "Taoyuan District" are often abbreviated to the same word in speech. The Zhongli District Office is under the Taoyuan City Government, so administratively Zhongli is naturally part of Taoyuan City.[^4]

But when Taoyuan people say "go to Taoyuan," they sometimes refer to Taoyuan District's station, shopping mall, government office, or city center. When Zhongli people say "I'm from Zhongli," they often provide a more precise location to avoid being mistaken for Taoyuan District residents. This difference is similar to how people in Taipei City might say they live in Da'an or Beitou, which does not mean they deny being Taipei people but reflects a different scale of place.

## The second reason: Zhongli was once a county-administered city with its own urban identity

Before 2014, Taoyuan County and Zhongli City were different levels of local administrative units. Zhongli was upgraded to a county-administered city in 1967 and was not changed to a district under the municipality until 2014.[^4]

For those who grew up before the reorganization, "Zhongli City" was not just an address but a set of municipal memories. Schools, roads, stations, commercial districts, local elections, and the municipal office all operated under the name of Zhongli. After the reorganization, "Zhongli District" still inherited these memories, so when residents say Zhongli, they often refer to a city that was once officially recognized by the administrative system, not just a village or suburb.

## The third reason: both places have complete living circles

Neither Taoyuan District nor Zhongli District is merely a satellite area dependent on the other. Zhongli has its own TRA station, traditional business center, schools, medical care, industry, and large-scale development zones. Taoyuan District is where the city government is located and has the administrative and political-economic functions of northern Taoyuan. The two cores compete with each other and also have commuting, schooling, medical treatment, and consumption exchanges.

A local article based on personal observation writes that Taoyuan District and Zhongli District can each provide complete functions in commerce, schools, hospitals, libraries, shopping malls, and commercial districts, so residents do not need to move across districts for every leisure activity.[^1] This phenomenon of "living separately" explains better than any slogan why the two places form different local identities.

## The fourth reason: the north–south regionalization of Taoyuan is not just a joke

A study by National Taiwan Normal University treats Zhongli as the core of southern Taoyuan and Taoyuan District as the core of northern Taoyuan, analyzing how ethnicity, religion, politics, education, and transportation jointly form the north–south regionalization.[^7]

The study points out that the immigrant distribution and settlement history since the Qing Dynasty created different ethnic structures in northern and southern Taoyuan. The administrative divisions during the Japanese colonial period also divided Taoyuan, Zhongli, and Dasi into different counties. The political traditions after the war, the distribution of Matsu and Bopeng temples, and the high school student source further extended these regional differences to modern times.

This does not mean Taoyuan people are always divided into two camps, nor does it mean there is an insurmountable boundary between northern and southern Taoyuan. A more accurate understanding is that administrative regions, transportation directions, and cultural networks have long converged toward different cores, and residents gradually learn to position themselves by north and south.

## The fifth reason: Zhongli's "difference" also includes competition and jokes

"Zhongli isn't Taoyuan" is sometimes serious local identification, sometimes a distinction from Taoyuan District, and sometimes just a joke between the two city cores. Local writers divide this context into several situations, including the need to subdivide locations within Taoyuan District, explaining where they come from to people in other counties and cities, and performing regional jokes among friends.[^1]

Therefore, one should not treat a slogan as proof of Zhongli residents collectively opposing Taoyuan. Nor should it be reversed to say that local identity is just a joke, because the reason the joke works is based on the real differences in history, transportation, and urban competition.

## Are Zhongli and Taoyuan really different?

The answer depends on which "difference" you are asking about. If asking about administration, Zhongli is Zhongli District of Taoyuan City, no doubt. If asking about daily place names, Zhongli and Taoyuan District are often separated by residents. If asking about history, Zhongli was once an independently developed county-administered city within Taoyuan County. If asking about living circles, Zhongli takes southern Taoyuan as its hinterland and has its own transportation, education, commerce, and cultural nodes. If asking about identity, the answer varies with generation, length of residence, family background, and language context.

## A river, a station, and a city still in flux

The most notable thing about Zhongli is not whether it can be summed up by a word "southern Taoyuan" but that it has always been in flux. The area around the station preserves the density of the traditional city. Neili connects industry and residence. Longgang preserves the diverse immigrant and military dependents' lives. National Central University and Chung Yuan University expand educational and population networks. Dacun retains rural landscapes. Qingpu brings high-speed rail, performances, and new housing into the city.

The story of Laojie Creek is especially illustrative of this change. In 1989, Zhongli once used a mall and covers to hide the river. After 2011, the local government, through opening projects, waterfront walkways, sewage treatment, the Riverside Education Center, and the patrol team, allowed the river to become public space again.[^5] The Riverside Education Center was rebuilt from Hakka old houses, placing the history of the project and local life in the same space.[^6] This is not about restoring Zhongli to an imagined ancient form but acknowledging that the city must constantly correct itself.

Zhongli is therefore not a reduced version of Taoyuan City, nor is it a southern copy of Taoyuan District. It is an urban district within Taoyuan City with its own historical layers, sharing the administration with Taoyuan while maintaining its own blocks, transportation, schools, food, communities, and waterfront for identification.

When someone says "I'm from Zhongli, not Taoyuan," the best response is not to immediately correct them that "Zhongli is obviously Taoyuan," nor to take this statement as a political declaration. First, ask which scale of "Taoyuan" they mean, and then it will be found that both statements can be true at the same time: Zhongli is part of Taoyuan City, and Zhongli is indeed a place that many people recognize through their own living experience.
