> **30-Second Overview:** Aiyu jelly is formed by rubbing aiyu seeds in water, allowing the pectin and enzymes within the seeds to react with divalent ions in the water at room temperature to create a gel. It requires no boiling and no added coagulants[^1][^4]. The most widely circulated written account is found in Lian Heng's _General History of Taiwan_ (_Taiwan Tongshi_), which states that during the early Daoguang era, a man from Tong'an discovered it by a stream in Hou-dapu, Chiayi; his daughter was named Aiyu[^3]. The seeds of the aiyu plant rely on a specific tiny bee, the aiyu small bee, for pollination to grow[^2]. As wild harvesting has declined annually, the Miaoli District Agricultural Improvement Station introduced two new varieties in 2012 and 2013 that yield approximately three times as much as wild species[^6].

An educational resource titled "Aiyu" published by the Biodiversity Research Institute of the Ministry of Agriculture in September 2024 includes a "blender version" recipe: combine one gram of aiyu seeds with 40 to 80 times the volume of boiled, cooled water or mineral water; stir at low speed for about two minutes, taking care not to break the seeds. Pour the slurry into a container lined with a cotton cloth bag and squeeze it out; let it sit for ten minutes, and it will fully set into aiyu jelly[^1].

This recipe involves no fire, no sugar, and no gelatin or agar. Water poured in is liquid, but ten minutes later, it becomes jelly. While most fruit pectins require heating, sugar, and acid to set, aiyu gel forms simply by adding water at room temperature[^4]. How the jelly forms lies hidden within that bag of rubbed seeds.

## Enzymes Released from a Handful of Seeds

The primary component of aiyu jelly is a long-chain polysaccharide on the seed surface called polygalacturonic acid. A 2022 report by Academia Sinica's _Research Matters_ describes it this way: the rubbing action extracts macromolecules, enzymes, and metal ions from the seeds into the water. Aiyu's unique pectin methylesterase replaces methyl groups on the long chains with carboxyl groups. Calcium ions in the water then link these carboxyl groups together, moving from temporary cross-linking to point-like connections, and eventually into long, stable linkage zones, causing the jelly to harden[^5].

This explanation is supported by academic research. A team led by Yeng-Long Chen from the Institute of Physics at Academia Sinica collaborated with the McKinley Laboratory at MIT to publish "Rheo-chemistry of gelation in aiyu (fig) jelly" in Volume 123 of _Food Hydrocolloids_ in 2022. They used rheometers and cryo-electron microscopy to track the gelation process and established a kinetic model for the binding of calcium and pectin. By incorporating the pectin methylesterase's conversion of binding sites into the model, they were able to calculate the inflection points on the elasticity and viscosity curves[^4]. The paper also notes something omitted in popular science reports: their experiments used deionized water, meaning the ions required for gelation come primarily from the seeds themselves; every 100 grams of dry matter contains approximately 0.95 grams of calcium and 5 grams of potassium. When made at home with tap water, the ions in the water act as additional helpers[^4].

The precautions in the Ministry of Agriculture's educational material are essentially layman's versions of this same mechanism. Stirring must be done at low speed because breaking the seeds will prevent the pectin from being released. Since water quality varies by region, if success rates are low, one can switch to mineral water, as "good water is rich in minerals" and helps the jelly set better[^1]. Real aiyu jelly will contain tiny fragments of seed coat or perianth, making it look cloudy; after about three hours, it will begin to "weep" (release water). Artificially synthesized aiyu is clear and transparent and will not weep even after ten-plus hours[^1].

The release of water is a sign that the reaction has stopped. The educational material states this directly: aiyu jelly relies on the bonding of enzymes and divalent cations to form a gel, so it cannot be stored for long; once the enzymes lose activity, the bonds disappear, and the jelly structure collapses[^1]. While the seeds can be stored in a cool, dry place, the jelly cannot.

## The Daughter of a Tong'an Man in the Early Daoguang Era

The most frequently cited origin of the name "Aiyu" is found in Volume 27 of Lian Heng's _General History of Taiwan_. The original text reads: "Aiyu seeds: produced in the mountains of Chiayi. Not recorded in old chronicles." It continues by saying that during the early Daoguang era, a man from Tong'an lived on Mazu Lou Street in the prefectural capital and frequently traveled to Chiayi to procure local goods. One day, while passing through Hou-dapu, he became thirsty from the heat and went to a stream to drink. He found the water surface had turned into jelly; he scooped it up and drank it, finding it incredibly refreshing. Looking closely, he saw seeds in the water; when he rubbed them, they produced a slurry. Thinking this was what the jelly was made of, he brought them home and washed them with water, and they instantly became jelly, which tasted wonderful with sugar. He had a fifteen-year-old daughter named Aiyu who, having nothing to do, began selling it. Customers loved it, "so it came to be called Aiyu jelly." Later, as more people harvested it, it was even transported to Fujian and Guangdong[^3].

This is a record, not an investigative report; Lian Heng does not state where the information originated. When later generations retell the same story, details often drift. The Ministry of Agriculture's material rewrites it as a Tainan merchant during the late Guangxu era of the Qing dynasty and claims the earliest record of aiyu seeds is Lian Heng's _General History of Taiwan_ from 1921[^1]. Looking back at the original book, it specifies "early Daoguang" and a man from Tong'an residing in the prefectural capital[^3]. The retold version is off by one era name; to cite accurately, one must return to the original text.

At the end of this passage, Lian Heng added a note: "Regarding Aiyu seeds, they are _peli_ (creeping fig); their nature is cooling and can relieve heat"[^3]. He treated the aiyu seed as the creeping fig. Modern classification places the two close together, but they are not the same thing: in 1904, Japanese botanist Makino Tomitaro published _Ficus awkeotsang_ in Volume 18 of _The Botanical Magazine, Tokyo_. In 1960, Corner merged it as a variety of the creeping fig (_Ficus pumila_), now written scientifically as _Ficus pumila_ var. _awkeotsang_[^7]. The Ministry of Agriculture's materials call it a Taiwanese endemic variety, distributed in mountainous areas at mid-to-low altitudes between 800 and 1,800 meters[^1]. A feature from Academia Sinica's digital archives reminds readers that the fruit of the creeping fig is smaller than aiyu and contains less extractable pectin, so it will not form jelly; one should not simply pick creeping figs to wash at home[^8].

## One Bee, One Fruit

![A whole aiyu seed fruit and one longitudinally sliced aiyu seed fruit; the cross-section shows dense yellow tiny flowers, with a one-yuan coin placed next to it for scale](/article-images/food/aiyu-fruit-cut-2022.webp)
_The syconium (hidden flower) of an aiyu seed, shown whole and longitudinally sliced, with a one-yuan coin for scale, October 2022. Photo: Shwabb1, CC BY-SA 2.0._

The flowers of the aiyu plant are hidden inside the fruit. The educational material states that it has a capitulum inflorescence containing unisexual flowers. Female and male flowers grow on different plants[^1][^6]. An article titled "Aiyu Pollination" from the Miaoli District Agricultural Improvement Station says the aiyu small bee (_Wiebesia pumilae_) is the sole pollinator of the aiyu seed: the adult bee lays eggs in the gall flowers of the male syconia; once the larvae hatch, they parasitize the ovules, and the mature small bees then pollinate the female flowers—a classic example of plant-animal mutualism[^2].

The process is illustrated in detail in the educational material. The small bee crawls out of the male fruit, inevitably passing through male flowers and becoming covered in pollen. When the female fruit matures, a small hole opens at the tip, releasing a special scent to attract the small bee. Once the bee lands on the female flower, pollination is complete. The pollinated female fruit swells and grows seeds—the very aiyu seeds that are later washed into jelly[^1]. Depending on the size of the fruit and the number of eggs laid, a single male fruit can contain anywhere from several thousand to tens of thousands of small bees. The peak time for these bees to emerge is between 10:00 AM and noon on sunny days; low temperatures and strong winds reduce the number of bees that emerge and their pollination efficiency[^2].

Thus, making a bowl of aiyu relies on the cooperation of two species: one provides the enzymes and pectin in the seeds, and the other ensures the seeds have the chance to grow. This is also where farmers face challenges. An article from the Miaoli District Agricultural Improvement Station in 2013 notes that farmers often struggle with inconsistent fruit yield and quality due to a lack of understanding regarding the pollination bees or improper cultivation ratios of male and female plants. Excessive use of pesticides for pests and diseases can also harm the aiyu small bees in the orchard, subsequently affecting pollination[^6]. The material also mentions that farmers have attempted to grow aiyu on low-altitude hillsides and flatlands, but suffered from severe fruit drop and unstable quality due to poor variety selection or cultivation management[^1].

## From Forest Land to Orchards

Aiyu was originally gathered from the mountains. The educational material states that in the past, harvesting was primarily done from wild plants in forest lands, but later, due to changes in forest composition and soil conservation efforts, production areas and yields plummeted[^1]. As demand did not decrease, farmers began cultivating it at lower altitudes.

In 2012 and 2013, the Miaoli District Agricultural Improvement Station introduced "Miaoli No. 1" (Plant Variety Protection No. A01379) and "Miaoli No. 2" (Plant Variety Protection No. A01443), respectively, with the goal of replacing wild aiyu seeds[^6]. The station's article lists: Miaoli No. 1 can yield over 20 to 25 kg per plant annually, approximately three times that of general wild species, with an average pectin content of 1.7%—42% higher than wild species. Miaoli No. 2 has a similar yield of about three times the wild variety, but with an average pectin content of 2.1%, which is 75% higher[^6]. The material also records that the station has collected a total of 115 male and female lines, completed cultivation management and pollination bee breeding/conservation technologies, and developed male lines that allow the small bees to inhabit and propagate year-round[^1].

Lian Heng wrote "produced in the mountains of Chiayi," and two hundred years later, the statistics still place Chiayi at the forefront. The educational material cites average values from the Ministry of Agriculture's Agricultural Commodities Administration reports for the years 109 to 111 (2020–2022): Chiayi County has a cultivation area of 228 hectares and an annual yield of 252 metric tons; Kaohsiung City has 208 hectares and 272 metric tons. Other major production areas include Nantou, Taitung, and Pingtung; among these, Kaohsiung and Taitung have aiyu seed production and marketing cooperatives, with larger scales of intensive artificial cultivation[^1]. These figures are read from charts in the educational material, which do not specify the survey scope for each county or city.

Returning to that ten-minute recipe: the reason the jelly cannot be kept for long is that the jelly itself is an ongoing reaction. And what provides the substance for this reaction to work on is a bee so small it is almost invisible. Every bowl of aiyu begins with a hand-wash, and also with a pollination inside a single fruit.
