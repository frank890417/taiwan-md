# Roast Meat Bento: How Skills Brought by Hong Kong Chefs Became Taiwanese Three Dishes and One Meat

At noon, before the glass display case of a roast meat shop is usually lined with customers. Some point at glossy char siu; some ask if today's roasted pork is crispy; and some don't ask anymore, just say: "Sanbao fan, add egg." This action seems so ordinary that we rarely ask: why did a roasting specialty that matured in Guangdong and Hong Kong become one of the most convenient lunches in Taiwan?

The answer lies not just in the meat, nor just in immigration. It is in an entrepreneur, a group of chefs who crossed the strait, and the Taiwanese insistence on "how many dishes should a bento have."

## Deconstructing "Roast Meat": It Was Never a Single Dish

"Roast meat" (_shaolà_) is often used as a shop name or bento category, but it is actually an amalgamation of two sets of techniques. "Roast" primarily refers to meats cooked by open flame or high heat, such as char siu, roasted pork, and roast duck. "Cured/Dried Meat" (_là_) relates to curing, air-drying, and preservation, such as cured sausages and preserved meat. The **siu mei (燒味)** commonly used in Hong Kong is closer to a "general term for roasted meats," not a fixed dish in a Taiwanese bento shop. [^1]

This difference is important. Because when roast meats left their original culinary environment, they took with them not just a roasting technique, but also the commercial language of "displaying meat, slicing it fresh, and selling by weight." The glass display cases, hanging meat hooks, and chopping sounds familiar in Taiwan are all production methods that put the craft directly on the street for people to see.

## 1967: A Roast Meat Shop Puts Hong Kong Flavor into Taipei Daily Life

The official history of Xindongyang places the starting point in 1967. At that time, Maixingfu operated "Dongyang Roast Meat Shop" on Wuchang Street in Taipei. This shop later became the predecessor to Xindongyang. The enterprise states that Xindongyang initially started with food items like roast meat and dried meat floss, placing Hong Kong-style roast meats within its retail and gift-giving business. [^2]

This starting point has an easily overlooked aspect: roast meat did not first enter Taiwan as high-end restaurant fare; it was linked very early on to retail, takeout, gift sets, and ritualistic needs. In other words, from the beginning, it was not just tasted at a dining table but also taken home, given as gifts in shops, or brought into another kind of ceremony.

This gave it the condition for popularization. High-end Cantonese cuisine requires seating, time, and a complete menu. Roast meat, however, can be transacted between a piece of meat, a knife, and a bento box. It retains the drama of displaying craftsmanship but does not require customers to understand the entire context of Cantonese cuisine.

## 1988: Xindongyang Turned "Chefs" into an Industry Chain

What truly increased the density of roast meat in Taiwan was 1988. Xindongyang officially claims that in ROC Year 77, they were the first to recruit roast meat chefs from Hong Kong and operate Hong Kong-style roast meats in Taipei's Wuzhong District, believing this action drove the trend of Taiwanese roast meat culture. [^3]

The report by StoryStudio provides a finer civilian version: in the late 1970s to early 1980s, Taiwan was not only sporadically receiving a few Hong Kong chefs who came for marriage or personal reasons, but enterprises were also recruiting roast meat chefs from Hong Kong. Xindongyang sold roast meat bento and gift sets, as well as roast meats used for worship or sacrificial offerings. After contracts ended, some chefs partnered with Taiwanese friends or hired Taiwanese people to handle the rental and opening of shops. [^4]

The key here is not that "Xindongyang invented Taiwanese roast meat." A more accurate way to put it is: the enterprise transformed an industry originally dependent on individual craftsmanship into a network that could be learned, replicated, and spread.

One chef brought a certain flavor. A group of chefs might bring an entry point to an industry. When the Taipei market became crowded, they moved to other counties and cities. StoryStudio's report documents this path of expansion from Taipei outwards, also mentioning that chefs used vacations to seek out old colleagues, relatives, and skilled people in Hong Kong, forming a cross-strait human network of "one pulling one." [^5]

## Why Did Hong Kong's Roast Meats End Up as Taiwanese Bento?

Because Taiwan did not accept things wholesale.

In Hong Kong, common ways to eat roast meats include roast meat rice, noodles, congee, or single platters. In Taiwan, it was placed into the bento logic. White rice became the base; the meat was the main course; and there needed to be some side dishes that made it feel like "a meal." Cabbage, braised eggs, pickled vegetables, tomato scrambled eggs, or other home-style dishes may not belong to a fixed Hong Kong roast meat tradition, but they found their place in the Taiwanese bento.

The industry classification by Taiwan's Ministry of Finance also defines "bento and self-service restaurants" as stores that provide fixed dishes like bentos or meal boxes, or offer customers choices via self-service. A characteristic is often paying before eating, and products being relatively affordable. [^6] This institutional definition shows that a bento is not merely a container but a method of serving: fixed, fast, suitable for takeout, and something the customer feels is worth the portion and dishes.

Therefore, the "Hong Kong style" of Taiwanese roast meat shops was never an untouched specimen. On one hand, it retained the curing and grilling of char siu, the crispiness of roasted pork, and the poaching of soy sauce chicken. On the other hand, it had to answer the questions of Taiwanese customers: is there enough rice? Are there too few side dishes? Can the sauce be mixed with the rice? Is the price suitable for a weekday lunch?

This is why _sanbao fan_ (three treasures rice) is so representative. It compresses the difficulty of choice into one name: char siu, roasted pork, or soy sauce chicken/duck, allowing customers to get several textures at once, and allowing the shop to place different parts and preparation methods in the same bento. Sanbao fan is not the only answer from Hong Kong tradition; it is a way that Taiwanese bento culture organized roast meat.

## The Sound of a Knife: Roast Meat Shops Also Brought Street Performance

What is most memorable about a roast meat shop might not be the taste, but the sound. StoryStudio recorded the shouts of Hong Kong chefs cutting meat, the tapping of knives on cutting boards, and the action of embedding large cleavers into cutting blocks. The report explains that these actions are related to the street culture of dense clustering and competition among roast meat shops in Hong Kong: the sounds serve both to call customers and to announce "there is business here" to neighboring shops. [^5]

This story may not represent every roast meat shop, nor is there enough data to prove all chefs followed the same tradition; but it offers a good perspective. Roast meat shops do not hide the kitchen in the back; they make the cutting of meat, hanging of meat, and part of the serving process public for people on the street to see. The glass case is a display case, and the cutting board is like a stage; what customers buy includes not just meat, but a visible and audible craft.

## An Immigration Story Cannot Be Simplified as "Everyone Arrived After 1997"

The video lists the wave of immigration around Hong Kong's return in the 1990s as one reason for the increase in roast meat shops. This direction has an intuitive appeal because 1997 was indeed a significant turning point in Hong Kong's history. However, based on the available data, it is not yet enough to conclude that "a large number of Hong Kong roast meat chefs immigrated to Taiwan" with clear numbers, a single time point, and direct causality.

A more stable explanation is: the expansion of Taiwanese roast meat included different paths such as corporate recruitment, individual migration, marriage, and introductions by acquaintances. StoryStudio's local memory emphasizes the recruitment in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and the subsequent network of shops formed by chefs and associates. Academic research understands the changes in Cantonese cuisine in Taiwan and Taiwanese cuisine in Hong Kong within the social, economic, and identity shifts between China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong. [^7]

This slower, less dramatic version is closer to how immigration truly changes a city. Culture is usually not delivered en masse on one day; rather, it is first demonstrated by a few individuals, then provided a place by an enterprise, then introduced to the next person by acquaintances, and finally becomes the streetscape that has "always been here."

## When "Hong Kong Flavor" Found Its Own Price in Taiwan

Today, there is a wide price gap among Taiwanese roast meat shops: traditional street-corner shops might be a weekday lunch, while department store or airport brands might become more expensive meals. Xindongyang officially describes Maiki Roast Meat as a brand that has been continuously operating since 1967 and relaunched in 2022 with a Japanese-style counter service fine dining restaurant. This shows that the same techniques can move between affordable bento, gifts, and fine dining. [^8]

On the other hand, statistics from 2024 showed that the number of operating locations for "bento and self-service restaurants" in Taiwan exceeded 10,000, reaching 10,176, with annual sales of approximately NT$31.52 billion, as reported by media citing the Ministry of Finance. This set of numbers covers bento and self-service overall, not just the number of roast meat shops. But it still provides useful context: bento has become a massive daily market capable of accommodating various immigrant flavors, local tastes, and chain models. [^9]

Roast meat survived not because Taiwanese suddenly became very knowledgeable about Cantonese cuisine, but because it found an extremely stable exchange: chefs provided the technique, enterprises provided the channel, bentos provided the price and speed, and customers fixed this cross-strait flavor with their daily meal.

Next time you stand in front of a roast meat shop, try to see that line of hanging meat as a brief immigration map. Char siu and roasted pork point to the culinary traditions of Guangdong and Hong Kong; Dongyang Roast Meat Shop points to the commercial streets of Taipei in the 1960s; Xindongyang in 1988 points to how enterprises brought chefs to Taiwan; and the white rice, braised eggs, and greens next to the glass case explain that this set of techniques has learned to complete a meal in a Taiwanese way.

So, why are there so many roast meat shops all over Taiwan? It is not because Taiwan was just waiting for an external food to be transplanted, but because after someone brought the skills, the Taiwanese market, bento culture, and daily life took over and rewrote it.

What you ultimately eat is neither purely Hong Kong-style roast meat nor a Taiwanese bento that has lost its origins. It is two histories stacked together: one of techniques crossing the strait, and another of an island's ability to turn foreign flavors into the everyday.
