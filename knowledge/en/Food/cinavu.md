# Cinavu: The Paiwan Leaf-Wrapped Millet and the _jiasuanjiang_ Leaves Meant to be Eaten

> **30-Second Overview:** _Cinavu_ (also written as _jinafu_) is a leaf-wrapped food of the Paiwan people: marinated pork wrapped in millet or taro powder, first encased in a layer of _jiasuanjiang_ leaves, then wrapped in an outer layer of shell ginger leaves or older leaves, tied tightly, and steamed or boiled. Food records from Taitung state that the Rukai people call this same type of food _abai_; however, whether one uses whole grains or powder, which leaves are used, and how different names are categorized varies across Pingtung and Taitung, as well as among different tribes and families. Traditionally served during full moons, weddings, millet harvest festivals, and banquets, it can now be found at tribal markets and urban Indigenous stalls. What outsiders most often miss is the layer of _jiasuanjiang_ leaves: because they are pressed against the rice, they must be eaten along with the millet.

![Cinavu from the Beiye tribe in Pingtung, cut in half on a white paper plate; dark leaves wrap golden millet with a piece of pork in the center](/article-images/food/cinavu-paiwan-beiyeh-cut-2024.webp)
_Cinavu from the Beiye tribe in Pingtung, showing the millet, pork, and the leaves wrapping the rice after being cut open. Photo: Sin-siōng, 2024, CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons._

In March 2019, Paiwan music teacher Camake Valaule brought his founded children's choir, the Taiwu Ancient Ballads Troupe, to the WOMADelaide music festival in Adelaide, Australia[^1][^23]. The choir was featured in the festival's "Taste the World" program, leaving behind a recipe with a single-word title: CINAVU[^2].

The recipe was for eight to ten servings. The ingredient list ran from millet and glutinous rice to pork and shiitake mushrooms; the penultimate line read "24 jiasuanjiang leaves," and the final line was "12 shell ginger leaves"[^2]. While shell ginger was translated into English, _jiasuanjiang_ was written directly in Hanyu Pinyin.

The recipe's method is as follows: lay shell ginger leaves on a plate, stack _jiasuanjiang_ leaves on top, spread a layer of millet, add pork, and cover with another layer of millet. Next, wrap it first with the _jiasuanjiang_ leaves, then pull the bottom shell ginger leaves up to form a second layer, tie it with cotton string, submerge it in water, and boil on high heat for one hour[^2]. Each of the two leaf layers has a specific function: the shell ginger leaves wrap the outside, while the _jiasuanjiang_ leaves remain inside against the rice and should not be peeled off when opening[^5].

The name, preparation, and occasions for _cinavu_ vary by tribe and family; outsiders often refer to it collectively as "Indigenous _zongzi_" (sticky rice dumplings). Following the habit of eating _zongzi_, people often peel away the shell ginger leaves and easily strip off the inner _jiasuanjiang_ layer along with the wrapping; food chroniclers in Taitung have cautioned, "Don't be in a hurry to tear the leaves off"[^5]. That layer must be eaten together with the millet—this is where it differs from _zongzi_.

## The Inner Layer of Leaves Must Be Eaten

The _jiasuanjiang_ used by Taiwan's Indigenous peoples to wrap _cinavu_, also known as _biguocao_, has the scientific name _Trichodesma calycosum_ (the Ministry of Agriculture's Q&A page uses its synonym _T. khasianum_), is a perennial shrub in the Boraginaceae family that can grow two to three meters tall, found in the plains and mountains of Taiwan's mid-to-low altitudes[^3][^24]. The Ministry of Agriculture notes that during Indigenous festivals, its leaves are used to wrap steamed pastries, which are eaten along with the leaves; the leaves contain high amounts of plant fiber and carry a light fragrance[^3].

![A jiasuanjiang plant under a forest canopy, with opposite elliptical long leaves spreading in all directions, surrounded by ferns and fallen bamboo on the ground](/article-images/food/cinavu-trichodesma-plant-2010.webp)
_Jiasuanjiang (biguocao). The Paiwan people collect its tender leaves to wrap millet; it is eaten along with the leaves after steaming. Photo: Shihchuan Ishikawa, 2010, CC BY-SA 2.0 via Wikimedia Commons._

In the 65th Pingtung County Science Fair, a group of Paiwan elementary school students documented this in great detail. Their findings showed that the leaves used to wrap _cinavu_ are divided into two layers: an inner layer of edible tender leaves and an outer layer of older leaves used for stability to prevent splitting during steaming[^4]. The primary inner leaf is _jiasuanjiang_, allowing one to "consume the tender jiasuanjiang leaves while eating," though some tribes use leaves from the _kè-lán_ (clans) tree as the edible inner layer. The outer layer varies by tribe and can be older _jiasuanjiang_ leaves, thicker/older _kè-lán_ leaves, or shell ginger; some also use _wǔjiémáng_ (five-knot grass) or tung leaves, finally tied tightly with cogon grass stems, cotton string, or shredded shell ginger sheaths[^4]. Even when using _kè-lán_ leaves, some tribes eat them pressed against the rice, while others use them only for the outer wrap.

The students asked their tribe's _vuvu_ (elders) to take them leaf-gathering and inquired about pairings. The elders told them that _kè-lán_ leaves are typically used for millet but not taro, as they pair better with millet; _jiasuanjiang_, however, can wrap both millet and taro[^4]. Which leaf is pressed against which type of grain is a flavor profile accumulated by each tribe over generations.

The Ministry of Agriculture also warns that identifying leaves is a matter of safety: since _jiasuanjiang_ is mostly wild-harvested, those unfamiliar with the plant occasionally mistake it for toxic Datura and suffer poisoning[^3]. Huang Chun-ming, a cultural worker from the Binmao tribe in Jinfeng Township, Taitung, noted that _jiasuanjiang_ looks very similar to Datura, and one must distinguish them by the color of the stems. He also mentioned that in earlier years, hunters heading into the mountains would carry _cinavu_ wrapped in _jiasuanjiang_ leaves, which wouldn't spoil for an entire day[^8].

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> English introductions sometimes mistranslate this leaf. There is also a plant in mainland China called "jiasuanjiang," which is a Solanaceae plant native to Peru, known in Taiwan as _dàběn pàozi cǎo_[^6], with the English common name apple-of-Peru[^22]. One English travelogue about the Tuban tribe in Taitung even wrote the Paiwan word _ljavilu_ as "apple of Peru leaf"[^7]. These are merely homonyms in Chinese; the plant used by the Paiwan in _cinavu_ is the Boraginaceae _biguocao_, known in English as _Khasya trichodesma_[^3].

## Under the Leaves: Millet Grains, Millet Dough, or Taro Powder?

_Cinavu_ is a Paiwan word. "Discovering Taitung Slow Food," a publication specializing in Taitung cuisine, states that in Rukai, the same type of leaf-wrapped food is called _abai_; in Taitung, these two names refer to the same thing: millet or glutinous rice wrapping tender marinated pork, encased in a layer of _jiasuanjiang_ leaves and then wrapped entirely in shell ginger leaves for steaming, mostly in rectangular shapes[^5].

Once you cross the Central Mountain Range into Pingtung, the terms diverge. According to the same article, in Pingtung tribes, _abai_ uses pounded millet or glutinous rice powder, resulting in a sticky texture, while _cinavu_ uses whole millet grains that can be bitten individually[^5]. The JIBAO educational database also makes this distinction when organizing Paiwan cuisine: _cinavu_ is wrapped with millet grains, while _avai_ is made with millet powder and water; both are listed under Paiwan foods[^9].

In a 2020 interview with FoodNEXT, the Paiwan singer A Bao (A-pao) held up two tribal foods; the magazine provided another version in its introduction: _abai_ is a grain-wrapped meat ball, while _cinavu_ is a long strip made of taro powder and _jiasuanjiang_ leaves[^10]. The Pingtung elementary students also noted that _cinavu_ fillings come in two types—millet and taro—with the taro version using _ragrag_, a powder made from dried taro[^4]. An English blogger recorded a journey to the Tuban tribe in Taitung, where they ate two types of _cinavu_ for lunch: one millet and one taro powder[^7].

![Seven or eight cinavu stacked in a stainless steel steamer, each tied tightly with coils of cotton string; the outer leaves are dark brown](/article-images/food/cinavu-paiwan-beiyeh-steamer-2024.webp)
_Tied cinavu in a steamer. Every tribe and family has its own way of choosing which grain and which leaf to use. Photo: Sin-siōng, 2024, CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons._

"Discovering Taitung Slow Food" also recorded a type called _qavay_, which looks almost identical but tastes different; in Paiwan, it means "millet cake." Millet is soaked for one or two days, fermented, drained, and pounded into a dough. A layer of _jiasuanjiang_ leaves is spread on shell ginger leaves, the millet dough is pressed flat over the filling, and it is steamed[^5]. The Laolao tribe in Taimali, Taitung, nicknames it _doremi_ because the fermented millet dough is so sticky that one must press it down with finger-tapping motions, like playing a piano. "Discovering Taitung Slow Food" wrote: "The fermented aroma is very intense; once the tribespeople smell it, they know their family from out of town is coming home to reunite"[^5]. The spellings _qavay_, _avai_, and _avay_ are similar; when PTS reported on the Binmao tribe in Taitung in 2024, they wrote the leaf-wrapped food there as "_abai_ _avay_"[^8].

## From Full Moons and Weddings to Weekend Markets in Taipei

Millet is a staple of traditional Paiwan life; the entire year's ritual cycle revolves around its growth, with the Millet Harvest Festival held during the harvest (July/August)[^9]. The Pingtung science fair report noted that in early Paiwan society, _cinavu_ was only made on important days: a child's one-month celebration, weddings, or festivals like the Annual Festival and the Millet Harvest Festival (_masalut_)[^4]. JIBAO is more direct: millet _cinavu_ and _avai_ are the "most noble and dignified foods" of the Paiwan, appearing during festivals, banquets, or as gifts[^9].

![Betrothal gifts laid on the ground at a wedding in Sandimen, Pingtung; bottom left shows a large bundle of millet ears tied with red string, next to strings of green bananas, sugarcane, and large taro leaves](/article-images/food/cinavu-sandimen-wedding-betrothal-2023.webp)
_A wedding in Sandimen, Pingtung, March 2023. The betrothal gifts brought by the groom include millet, bananas, sugarcane, and taro. The image shows the gifts; no \_cinavu_ is present, but millet and taro are the two most common bases for it. Photo: WEI, WAN-CHEN, CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons.\_

These occasions share one thing in common: many people are gathered, and food must be shared. "Discovering Taitung Slow Food" describes _cinavu_ and _abai_ as representing respect for Heaven and Earth, gratitude to ancestors, and the spirit of sharing with relatives and friends[^5]. When asked about the characteristics of Paiwan cuisine, A Bao smiled and replied, "I think it's balance." She cited _abai_ as an example: primarily grain-based, wrapped in leaves, with meat inside—providing both starch and protein. "The wisdom of the elders is truly wonderful"[^10].

Student reports also note the present day: with more resources available, tribespeople can buy ingredients to make _cinavu_ whenever they want without waiting for specific festivals; ready-made versions are also seen at markets[^4]. It has also entered cities and campuses. In May 2025, the Indigenous Student Resource Center at Chung Yuan Christian University held a "Millet Also Came to Dragon Boat Festival" communal kitchen event for _cinavu_; the announcement noted that students must be told that the _jiasuanjiang_ leaves "can be swallowed directly along with the inner food"[^11]. When serving this dish in the city, one must first explain that the leaf is edible. This announcement labeled the _jiasuanjiang_ as the "outer layer," which differs from most descriptions that place it against the rice[^11].

## The Snails Brought Back by a Mother

Artist Chang En-man's _cinavu_ creations began with snails. Her mother is Paiwan; between 2009 and 2013, she completed six works themed around the African giant snail. It started from a childhood memory, which she wrote in English: "my separated Paiwan mother used to collect and clean the snails then apportion to the kids she could not be with."[^12]

The African giant snail is not native to Taiwan; in the early 1930s during the Japanese colonial period, Japanese officials introduced them from Singapore as a food source[^12][^13][^14]. They later proliferated in fields as an invasive species harmful to crops[^14], but were also collected by tribespeople, who would feed them sweet potato leaves first to purge their systems before cooking them[^8]. Many tribal families make snail dishes[^13], and they are even included in some _cinavu_ and _abai_[^14][^17]. Chang En-man's 2021 work, _Snail Paradise Trilogy: Setting Sail or Final Chapter_, traces the origins of _cinavu_ through this lens[^14].

On February 3, 2024, this work brought _cinavu_ to Jakarta; Museum MACAN extended it into a cooking workshop called "Trails of the Tongue: Cinavu," limited to 15 participants and led by Lazy Susan, using ingredients available in Indonesia to wrap _cinavu_[^14]. On December 14 of the same year, Chiang Mai Design Week held a "Cooking Cinavu" workshop at MAIIAM Contemporary Art Museum, allowing participants to make this Paiwan leaf-wrapped dish using local Thai ingredients[^15].

Looking back at the recipe that Taiwu Ancient Ballads Troupe took to Australia, it was also a home-style version: millet mixed with glutinous rice, and pork and shiitake mushrooms seasoned with soy sauce and oyster sauce; the ingredient list even included a bit of rock sugar[^2]. Recipes adapt to local kitchens wherever they go; both the Jakarta and Chiang Mai workshops swapped in local ingredients[^14][^15].

## The Suspects Were Snails and _kè-lán_ Leaves, but the Culprit Was Seed Millet

On September 17, 2024, an elder from the Binmao tribe in Jinfeng Township, Taitung, made _abai_ using millet, pork, bamboo shoots, and snails. After eating it, she felt unwell and passed away that day[^17][^18]. While relatives were keeping vigil, they shared the remaining _abai_, and by September 18, several people had been hospitalized[^8][^18]. By the time the prosecution concluded its investigation in December, the poisoning incident, including the elder, had resulted in 4 deaths and 8 serious injuries[^8][^16].

When the news first broke, most reports focused on the snail as the ingredient of concern[^8]. The following day, the Ministry of Health and Welfare announced test results: highly toxic organophosphate pesticide chlorpyrifos was detected in the leftover _abai_, shifting public scrutiny toward the millet[^8]. The Food and Drug Administration screened 410 types of pesticides but only found chlorpyrifos; when they tested each component of the leftover _abai_ individually, the highest concentration was found in the millet[^17]. The _kè-lán_ leaves used for wrapping, the snails, and other separately collected ingredients showed no traces of pesticides[^8][^18]. On December 20, 2024, the Taitung District Prosecutors Office concluded the investigation: the elder had mistakenly used millet seeds—which had been treated with chlorpyrifos for preservation by a relative two years prior—alongside her household's edible millet. She died from poisoning; the case was found to involve no intentional or negligent criminal act[^16][^8].

During the period when the incident trended online, suspicion briefly fell on _abai_ itself. Chang Yu-chuan, a professor in the Department of Public and Cultural Affairs at National Dong Lieh University, said: "This was mainly because of the issue with _abai_ (_avay_), so some witch-hunting occurred online, targeting the components that make up the ingredients within _abai_. If we question _abai_, what we are actually challenging is not the ingredient, but the traditional dietary wisdom of the Paiwan people. I believe this causes great harm to the tribe."[^8] Tribal food businesses were hit hardest; stir-fried snails had once been a must-try delicacy for tourists in Jinfeng Township, and business suffered due to the misinformation spreading online[^8]. Assistant Professor Hsiao Hsiang-wei of National Dong Lieh University reminded that it is not only Indigenous people who eat snails; if the same ingredient were placed in the context of French cuisine, perhaps it would not attract such stereotypes and criticism[^8].

The Binmao tribe has fewer than 400 people, over 80% of whom are Paiwan[^8]. Huang Chun-ming said: "In Paiwan dietary culture, there is a very important spirit: the spirit of sharing. Our entire tribe views this case as an accident."[^8] Chen Chih-wei, a tribal youth who returned home to work in the food industry, believes that tribal youth can use that same online power to clarify the facts and reclaim the right to interpret their own culture[^8].

In the eyes of outsiders, everything wrapped around or pressed against the rice is just "leaves," so suspicion naturally falls on the leaves first. The Pingtung science fair project was written in response to this. In their research motivation, the students wrote: "The recent millet zongzi poisoning incident in Taitung has caused misunderstandings regarding _kè-lán_ leaves (_kataljap_). This is a misconception of Paiwan cuisine, leading to misunderstandings about these leaves."[^4] Their science fair instructor was Han Chinese and noted that while _zongzi_ and _cinavu_ are very similar, the leaves used are completely different. The students thus placed shell ginger, _jiasuanjiang_, and _kè-lán_ leaves alongside the bamboo leaves and bamboo sheaths used in Han Chinese _zongzi_ to compare their load-bearing capacity, antibacterial properties, and heat retention[^4].

After the experiment, the students concluded that _kè-lán_ leaves performed better than expected among natural leaves, possessing certain antibacterial abilities and heat retention. Shell ginger leaves had the highest final temperature for heat retention, though the difference between the five types of leaves was less than 2 degrees Celsius[^4]. An elementary school science project cannot reach a definitive conclusion, but what remains is a group of children who, when the outside world doubted _kè-lán_ leaves, followed their elders into the tribe to re-identify the leaves they eat.

## Tainan Says It Is Not Zongzi

The names outsiders have given to _cinavu_ and _abai_ are mostly borrowed from _zongzi_. Several news headlines regarding the 2024 Binmao incident referred to _abai_ as "millet _zongzi_"[^8][^16][^17]. As of November 2026, the Chinese Wikipedia term for "jinafu" redirects directly to the entry for "_zongzi_"[^20]. On May 24, 2025, the New Taipei City Indigenous Peoples Affairs Department displayed the Amis _alifongfong_ alongside _cinavu_; a Central News Agency English caption stated that _cinavu_ is a food eaten by the Paiwan, Bunun, and Rukai peoples, and that both are similar to Han Chinese _zongzi_, intended to show similarities between Taiwan's different ethnic groups[^19].

A year later, the Tainan City Government took a different approach. In June 2026, on the eve of the Dragon Boat Festival, the city government sent _Cinavu_ to officials at a municipal meeting for tasting, emphasizing that it is not _zongzi_: it is a traditional leaf-wrapped food of the Paiwan and Rukai peoples, primarily made of millet or dried taro powder, paired with marinated pork, and wrapped in materials like _jiasuanjiang_ or cogon grass leaves[^21]. Mayor Huang Wei-che stated that calling _Cinavu_ "Indigenous _zongzi_" simplifies its cultural meaning and easily leads to misunderstanding. The city also announced that the Indigenous Cultural Site "Tabe 札哈木樂原," opening on August 1, would regularly sell _Cinavu_[^21]. The city drew a distinction based on the primary grain: _zongzi_ is primarily glutinous rice, while _Cinavu_ is primarily millet or dried taro powder[^21]. While some home recipes include a bit of glutinous rice, the Taiwu Ancient Ballads Troupe recipe uses 600g of millet with 100g of glutinous rice[^2].

The similarities seen in New Taipei are real; visually, it uses leaves to wrap rice and meat just like _zongzi_, as noted by the Pingtung science fair instructor[^4]. The difference is only visible when you peel it open: bamboo leaves for _zongzi_ are peeled off and discarded, whereas the _jiasuanjiang_ leaves remain on the rice to be eaten. Which specific leaves are pressed against the rice and which wrap the outside depends on the tribe and family making it[^4][^5]. If outsiders only remember that it is like _zongzi_, they will miss exactly that layer of leaves meant to be eaten.

In Adelaide, that recipe wrote _jiasuanjiang_ in pinyin without finding an English name for it[^2]. In Pingtung, a group of Paiwan elementary students wrote the same leaf as _ljavilu_, with a Mandarin annotation, stating that this research allowed them to "move from just seeing it on the dining table to recognizing and gathering it in the tribe"[^4]. A recipe can be printed in English and taken abroad, but that layer of leaves meant to be eaten still requires someone to follow their _vuvu_ into the tribe to recognize it.
