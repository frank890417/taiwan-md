It was nearly nine in the evening when blogger "Baby's Playground" couldn't find Sakariba in Tainan and had to call the "old-brand eel noodle" shop they meant to visit. The owner told them to wait at the intersection of Zhongzheng Road and Hai'an Road, then came out to guide them in. Inside, the wall read "quick-fry eel in twenty-seven seconds." The blogger asked about it, and the owner said videos could be found online. This 2013 food journal noted that the wok was then manned by third-generation Liao Kuo-hsiung. [^1]

As early as 2007, Chiao Tung wrote about this shop in _Business Weekly_. He wrote that Sakariba's "Old-brand Eel Noodles" "boasted a twenty-seven-second quick-fry, meaning from the moment eel hits the wok to finishing the stir-fry takes only twenty-seven seconds, to preserve the eel's texture." Immediately after, he described the person at the wok: "Every time I watch the wok master quick-fry over high flames, oil smoke billowing, I sometimes associate it with the fierce character of Taiwan's early immigrants." [^2]

The dish brought to the customer: underneath lies a mound of pre-fried yi mein, topped with eel segments stir-fried over high heat, drenched in a sweet-sour brown starch-thickened sauce. Those who don't want the starch can order it dry-fried. [^3][^4][^5] Chiao Tung said eel noodles are a southern Taiwan street food flavor, and that Tainan might be the city with the highest density of eel noodle shops in all Taiwan. [^2] Wang Hao-yi wrote down each step that must be completed within those twenty-seven seconds.

> **30-second overview:** Tainan's eel noodles are a mound of pre-fried yi mein topped with high-heat quick-fried eel and sweet-sour starch sauce, also available dry-fried without starch. The Liao family's stir-fried eel passed to the third generation; Ah-yuan opened on Chenggong Road, his nephew Ah-jiang struck out on his own on Minzu Road, and non-Liao Ah-hui has run a shop in Tainan for over fifty years. That Sakariba shop with "twenty-seven seconds" on its wall later closed. Fewer eels in the field ditches; old shops say they now rely mostly on imports. How high the fire, how much sauce, how thick the starch — still in the hands of those at the wok, adjusting with the city and the times.

## Garlic Fragrance, Starch Thickening, Vinegar Added — Every House Competes on Fire

Wang Hao-yi wrote the old-brand's wok sequence in _Snack Research Institute_, saying this shop boasts the entire action cannot exceed twenty-seven seconds. Garlic first fried fragrant, eel segments immediately tossed in, scallions, onions, chili, and seasonings quickly stir-fried, starch thickened, a splash of 1.5-print vinegar, then scooped onto the plated yi mein. [^5] He listed four conditions for good stir-fried eel: freshness, fire control, seasonings, noodles. That entry-moment crisp-tenderness is the most crucial characteristic of stir-fried eel, so a big wok and high fire are essential, with fire control and timing precisely grasped. [^5]

The yi mein cushioning the plate bottom differs from the fresh noodles used in minced pork yi mein — it's a clump of noodle blocks pre-fried in a big wok with a big ladle of hot oil until fluffy and puffed. At a typical eel noodle shop, bowl-sized, dry-hard fried noodle blocks pile up on the prep counter, stacked like a small mountain. [^5][^6] Chiao Tung wrote that stir-fried eel noodles mostly use a noodle called "yi-fu mein." This noodle incorporates egg liquid when kneaded, cut into fine strands, cooked to seven-tenths done then oil-fried and dried; he described its entry into the wok: "Yi-fu mein at first glance resembles instant noodles, seemingly both parched and starved, hitting the wok it wildly absorbs the starch sauce, in a trice turning soft." [^2] Mao-ta-ye at Ah-yuan's on Chenggong Road also said that pile of fried yi mein on the counter looked a bit like instant noodles. [^7]

Ah-yuan, stir-frying eel at Chenggong Road, told Mao-ta-ye the ingredients for this dish are just wok-front starch powder, sugar, soy sauce, lard, then onion, garlic, chili, and pepper for seasoning; the key to deliciousness lies in fire control. [^10] As for vinegar, Chiao Tung recorded this dish mainly uses black vinegar, rice wine, and sugar for seasoning, balanced to sweet-sour, tasting very much like sweet-and-sour thick soup (wuliu geng); the most crucial is the vinegar — some particular shops use several vinegars to blend, which he called a secret not passed on. [^2] The stir-fried eel can be starch-thickened into a sweet-with-sour soup, or dry-fried into a salty flavor — both are found in Tainan. [^4]

Fu-sheng Snacks on Guohua Street, open over fifty years, saw its proprietress's stir-frying figure go viral on IG. _Foodie Player_ reporter Chiang Ting-yi wrote that when this shop stir-fries eel, roaring flames burst from the wok, the proprietress dodging flames while cooking, "her steps looking just like she's dancing the cha-cha." [^14]

Yu-fu, writing of Sakariba, said Tainan's eel Liao can stir-fry a piping-hot plate in twenty-seven seconds; that "Eel Liao" in the market "especially delights in performing the so-called 27-second stir-fried eel." Twenty-seven seconds is that shop's wall-written, customer-performed signature. [^4][^1] In 2022, a CTS reporter in Tainan watched an owner quick-fry over high fire, then add a house-secret sauce; the voiceover said twenty-odd seconds to finish. The wall's twenty-seven seconds and the voiceover's twenty-odd seconds measure the same fire: keeping the eel in the wok just short enough to retain that crisp-tenderness Wang Hao-yi spoke of. Yu-fu said the Liao brothers learned this hand from a Fuzhou master.

## The "Knife Skills" Taught by the Fuzhou Master

Yu-fu, having moved to the prefectural city for ten years, wrote Tainan's alleyway architecture and eats into _Inside Tainan's Alleys_. In the piece on stir-fried eel, he said: "In Tainan, those who stir-fry eel are mostly surnamed Liao; the legendary figures Liao Ping-nan and Liao Huo-tu brothers are two generations above my age." These brothers learned "knife skills" from a Fuzhou master; Yu-fu explained this means the way of cooking, and said stir-frying eel isn't actually difficult: "The hand-road for thickening soup is about the same; if you can integrate and comprehend it, you're a master." Liao Ping-nan later earned the nickname "Eel South"; the Liao brothers then passed the stir-fried eel technique down generation by generation. [^4]

Ah-hui stir-frying eel on Ximen Road isn't surnamed Liao. _Foodie Player_ wrote that Ah-hui's first-generation owner Hsu Yin was a head banquet chef — a master who runs the kitchen for banquet catering — who learned stir-fried eel from a Fuzhou master back then; this shop has been in Tainan for over fifty years. [^9]

According to local lore, Liao Ping-nan originally worked in a silver shop; his third brother started selling stir-fried eel first, and he followed in switching trades, setting up a stir-fried eel stall at You'ai Street Market. The third brother later changed careers, but Liao Ping-nan's high-fire quick-fry skill was remarkable, and with thin margins but high volume he made a name. [^10]

The market where Liao Ping-nan set up stall is Sakariba. [^11] This name comes from Japanese "盛り場" (mori-ba), meaning a lively place where crowds gather. In 1932, Tainan City Hall, to complete the Ximachi Roundabout project, moved the hundred-plus stalls that had been night-marketing at Ximachi Small Park to a filled-in fish pond behind the West Market; the bustling Denmachi Night Market was called "盛り場" in contemporary reports. A few years later stalls moved again to today's Kang-le Market location; postwar the Japanese pronunciation was retained, written in Chinese characters as Sakariba. [^12]

## Ah-yuan at Fourteen with His Father, Ah-jiang at Seventeen with His Third Uncle

Liao Ping-nan's youngest son took his mother's surname, birth name Sung Sung-wei; his nickname "Ah-yuan" is better known than his given name. Mao-ta-ye wrote of him in 2015: from fourteen he followed his father selling stir-fried eel; the shop originally opened on Cheng-yi Street, later renamed Hsin-mei Street, then moved to Chenggong Road. [^10]

Mao-ta-ye wrote of Ah-jiang on Minzu Road in 2022, saying he was the one who learned from Ah-yuan: "Ah-yuan's nephew is Ah-jiang; Ah-jiang at seventeen followed third uncle Ah-yuan to work and learn the craft; after military service he soon opened his own shop." [^13] He relayed Ah-jiang's proud words: he's been selling eel a long time; movements must be fast, sauce added precisely — this is real kung fu honed by practice. [^13] Yu-fu also recorded that Ah-jiang's younger brother recently started his own stove, opening Second Brother Stir-fried Eel; he placed Sakariba's Liao family stir-fried eel, Ah-yuan, Ah-jiang, and Second Brother together, saying they're largely the same family. [^4]

For the Sakariba branch, Wang Hao-yi recorded the succession in his book as first generation "Eel South," second generation "Eel Lu," to third generation Liao Kuo-hsiung. [^5] That 2013 blogger, after eating and chatting with the owner, noted a ladle used from ROC year 38 (1949) to the present. [^1] Counting from the Liao Ping-nan brothers' generation, the Liao family's wok divided across Sakariba, Chenggong Road, and Minzu Road.

Mao-ta-ye also wrote when covering Ah-jiang that stir-frying eel is physically demanding work. [^13] That address on Ximen Road Section 2 was originally Ah-tie Stir-fried Eel; after Master Ah-tie retired, Ah-hui took over the shopfront. [^10] The wok-side craft passes from person to person, generation to generation; the eel in the wok depends on how the fields and ditches change.

## Fewer and Fewer Eels in the Paddy Ditches

Chiao Tung's 2007 article wrote that lunar months five to seven are eel breeding season, also when fat eels call gourmets to the door. [^2] The eel's formal name is Asian swamp eel (黃鱔), scientific name _Monopterus albus_, commonly called field eel (田鰻). [^15] Academia Sinica's Taiwan Fish Database writes that it lives in mud-bottom waters like paddies, ponds, rivers, and ditches; also seen in swamps and flooded fields; likes to burrow, active at night; oral skin folds allow direct air breathing. Come winter and dry season, it burrows one to two meters underground, several sharing a burrow; distributed throughout western Taiwan's freshwaters. [^16] This burrowing, air-breathing fish makes its home in that layer of soft mud at paddy edges and ditch bottoms.

In May 1930, the Chianan Irrigation System completed; after water flowed, irrigated paddies increased, dry fields decreased. [^17] Senior travel-food journalist Chen Chih-tung described the ditches of that time to _The Reporter_: "1930, Wushantou and Chianan Irrigation completed; along the irrigation channels were all earthen ditches; eels lived happily in the ditches and paddy edges digging holes in the soft mud. Farmers planting rice would also catch eels and swamp eels to take home for nourishment." [^18] The same report continued: later earthen ditches became concrete channels, Taiwan's eels decreased, now mostly imported eels are used. [^18] Yu-fu wrote that wild eels once caught with a bamboo pole, hemp line, and hook are now "nowhere to be caught." [^4]

In 2015, Master Ah-yuan told Mao-ta-ye that southern paddies once had many eels; now there are none, all relying on imports, and prices naturally rose. [^10] A 2021 ETtoday piece on Fu-sheng Snacks on Guohua Street wrote that it selects imported wild eels. [^19] Academia Sinica's Taiwan Fish Database lists Asian swamp eel as an economic food fish; market eels are mostly farmed, more in the south; in Taiwan mostly sliced and oil-stir-fried. [^16] International fish database FishBase also lists it as a species with commercial fishing, commercial aquaculture, and even aquarium trade. [^20]

Wang Hao-yi placed freshness first among the four stir-fried eel essentials: fine tender flesh, fresh-sweet texture. He recorded the process: caught eels first "rested" in clear water several days to remove mud flavor; slaughtered by "piercing a short awl through the fish head, fixing the eel on a wooden board," then "with a sharp blade removing fish head and spine in one cut," the whole bloodied fillet spread flat awaiting the wok. [^5] Chiao Tung 2007 wrote eels must be kept in clear water one or two days to remove mud taste; freshly slaughtered is fresh-sweet. Eels are lively and slippery; the knife must be fast, depth precise, following the momentum to split the belly and remove the spine — a common slaughter scene at eel stalls. He also wrote many shops don't process eels; the eel meat carries a muddy stench. [^2]

Yu-fu's book leaves a then-and-now: "Slaughtering live eels early on had to be done by the shopkeeper; I heard Ah-yuan could still perform this master skill, but now it's rarely seen." [^4] Taiwan Fish Database records Asian swamp eel generally sold live. [^16] Mao-ta-ye 2015 wrote that because eels are hard to process and easily fishy, other famous eel shops don't sell eel soup; only Ah-yuan, with unique eel-processing skill, sells it. [^10] From live fish to wok, an eel must pass through several hands.

Chiao Tung, particular about fresh slaughter, left a heavy sentence in the same article: "Today's eels are mostly frozen imports, not worth taking as a standard." [^2] He then gave a 2007 exception: that year, Ching-hsiang Eel Noodles in Hsinying City, Tainan County, still used wild eels; Hsinying had many ponds and streams; farmers caught them at night, carried them to market next day. This shop even fired its eel stir-fry stove with rice chaff; he said chaff has low ignition point, catches fire fast, perfectly matching the fierce-fire explosive stir-fry scene. [^2] Chiao Tung put the fish's origin into the standard for judging a plate of stir-fried eel.

The fish's origin is mostly not something the wok-front person alone can decide. The few dozen seconds after eel hits the wok, that pinch of sugar and ladle of starch before lifting — those are the measures they adjust daily. In that CTS interview, the Tainan owner said Tainan eel noodles besides definitely adding a bit more sugar, the house-secret sauce and starch-thickening degree are also a craft. [^8]

## Friends Who Found the Soup Too Sweet, Taken to Eat Dry-fried

Yu-fu, treating friends to stir-fried eel in Tainan, often encountered the same reaction; he wrote in his book: "I treat them to stir-fried eel, most complain the soup is too sweet! So I take them to Ah-jiang's for dry-fried salty flavor, finally shut their mouths." [^4] _The Reporter_ 2019 also wrote dry-fried eel noodles as Ah-jiang's signature dish. [^21] Yu-fu also mentioned that besides yi mein, one can specially request oil noodles for another flavor. [^4]

Shih Jui-pin, raised in Tainan's West Central District, father Shih Hung-yao founded Tung-fu Electric in Tainan in 1973, doing small-appliance OEM. In 2019 he told _The Reporter_ about his favorite Minzu Road Ah-jiang, speaking of this shop's sweetness: "This place is actually already quite famous; their yi mein flavor leans sweet, suiting us Tainan people's love for sweet; it's what I must eat every trip back from Shenzhen to Taiwan." [^21]

Tainan-born director Wei Te-sheng said on a show in 2022 that he loves mud carp thick soup and eel noodles most; Taipei has them too, "I've eaten in Taipei but it's just not right, however I eat it it's not right." Asked if it's because Tainan people prefer sweet, he replied: "This is a prejudice that's wrong wrong wrong, so every time I hear people say Tainan people all eat very sweet, I have to argue and defend." [^8][^22]

Two Tainan people: one says hometown folks love sweet, one hears this claim and must argue. Same Ah-jiang: Yu-fu takes friends for its dry-fried salty; Shih Jui-pin says it leans sweet, suits Tainan taste. CTS reporter asked north and south shops; Tainan operators on the difference: "The method is just one dry-fried one starch-thickened; starch-thickened is the more traditional taste; northern people themselves don't eat that sweet." Taipei operators said: "Taipei people aren't used to this sweet, eat a bit saltier." [^8]

_Starch-thickened eel noodle soup, photographed 2010, location unrecorded. Photo: TACO Huang, CC BY-SA 2.0 via Wikimedia Commons_

ETtoday 2021 introducing Fu-sheng Snacks on Guohua Street said its soup has heavier sourness, packed with onions quick-fried over high fire, not too much Tainan sweet. _Foodie Player_ wrote its soup base sweet-sour, with a bit of starch-thickened feel; besides dry-fried there's also a braised-noodle version. [^19][^14] Cheng-pien Chen-wei by the East Gate Roundabout, founded 1970, a simple small shop, listed in Michelin Guide; the guide page writes its stir-fried eel sweet-sour, stir-fried eel noodles like stir-fried eel can choose whether to starch-thicken. [^23][^24]

North to Yunlin's Tuku, _The Reporter_ wrote a sixty-year-old shop; its soup starch-thickened more, salty greater than sweet; noodle used is oil noodles; proprietress Yao Li-chiu said farmers coming down from fields eat starch-thickened soup and get fuller, also more flavorful. There they also add 1.5-print vinegar and chili powder when eating; the reporter called this eating style "plains-style," saying its flavor is completely different from Tainan's eel noodles. [^18]

In 2015, Master Ah-yuan at Chenggong Road told Mao-ta-ye that people used to eat heavier flavors; now dry-fried has slight adjustments, a bit lighter. [^10] Taipei operators pull back the sweet a bit; Ah-yuan pulls the dry-fried lighter a bit; the ones adjusting by hand are all those standing at the wok.

## Ah-yuan Master and Son Stir-fry Together, Son Takes the Wok

July 2015, Mao-ta-ye posted "Ah-yuan Stir-fried Eel: History of Tainan Stir-fried Eel and Unique Eel Soup," writing of his visit to Ah-yuan on Chenggong Road. Small shop, few seats; Ah-yuan always insisted on selling only stir-fried eel, later added rice vermicelli; stir-fried eel divided into starch-thickened and dry-fried; also a bowl of eel soup. On the prep counter sat a basin of bright red, processed eels; also fried yi mein. [^10]

Chiao Tung in his year watched the wok-master's high fire and oil smoke; Mao-ta-ye that day at Chenggong Road recorded Ah-yuan master in the shop cooking with his son, mainly the son manning the wok. [^10]

2024, Mao-ta-ye reorganized Tainan's eel noodles once more: the Sakariba old-brand with "twenty-seven seconds" on its wall has closed; that Ah-yuan on Chenggong Road who from fourteen followed his father selling stir-fried eel, his shop has now passed the wok to his son. [^11]
