It was nearly nine in the evening when the blogger "Bao'er Playground" couldn't find Sakaribara (沙卡里巴) in Tainan and had to call the "old-brand eel noodle" shop they intended to visit. The owner told them to wait at the intersection of Zhongzheng Road and Haian Road, and he would come out to lead the way. Upon entering the shop, the wall read "Stir-fried Eel in 27 Seconds." When the blogger asked about it, the owner said videos could be found online. A 2013 food review noted that at that time, the person wielding the ladle was the third generation of the Liao family, Liao Kuo-hsiung. [^1]

As early as 2007, Jiao Tong had written about this shop in _Common Week_ (_Jin Zhou Kan_). He wrote that Sakaribara's "old-brand eel noodles" "claim to stir-fry in 27 seconds, meaning it takes only 27 seconds from the moment the eel enters the wok until it is plated, in order to maintain the texture of the eel." Immediately following this, he wrote about the person standing before the wok: "Every time I watch the ladle-wielder stir-fry over high heat with smoke and oil billowing, I sometimes am reminded of the violent temperament of Taiwan's early immigrants." [^2]

The plate served to the customer consists of a mound of pre-fried egg noodles at the bottom, topped with eel segments stir-fried over high heat and drizzled with a sweet and sour brown starch glaze. For those who do not want the glaze, one can also order it "dry-fried" (without starch). [^3][^4][^5] Jiao Tong said that eel noodles are a signature snack of southern Taiwan, adding that Tainan might be the city with the highest density of eel noodles in all of Taiwan. [^2] Everything that must be accomplished within those twenty-seven seconds was recorded one by one by Wang Hao-yi.

> **30-Second Overview:** Tainan's eel noodles consist of a mound of pre-fried egg noodles topped with high-heat stir-fried eel and a sweet and sour starch glaze; dry-fried versions without glaze are also available. The Liao family's stir-fry technique has passed to the third generation: A-Yuan opened a shop on Chenggong Road, his nephew A-Jiang established himself on Minzu Road, and an A-Hui, who is not surnamed Liao, has also operated in Tainan for over fifty years. The old-brand shop in Sakaribara with "27 seconds" written on its wall has since closed. As eels in the ditches become scarcer, the old shops say they now rely mostly on imports. How high the fire burns, how much sauce is added, and how thick the starch glaze is remains in the hands of those at the wok, adjusting with the city and the era.

## Garlic Aromatics, Starch Glaze, and Vinegar: Every House Competes on Heat

In _Snack Research Institute_, Wang Hao-yi wrote about the sequence used by old-brand shops, noting that this shop claims the entire process cannot exceed twenty-seven seconds. Garlic is first sautéed for aroma, followed immediately by the eel segments being tossed into the wok; then onions, scallions, chili, and seasonings are quickly stir-fried. A starch glaze is added along with a bit of Wu-yin vinegar (five-spice vinegar), and it is then plated onto the pre-served noodles. [^5] He categorized the four essential conditions for delicious stir-fried eel as: freshness, heat control, seasoning, and noodles. The crispness upon the first bite is the most vital characteristic of stir-fried eel, so a large wok and high heat are required, with both temperature and timing needing to be precisely mastered. [^5]

The egg noodles at the bottom of the plate are different from the fresh noodles used in meat sauce noodles; they are mounds of dough that have been pre-fried in a large ladle of hot oil until puffy and expanded. In typical eel noodle shops, bowl-sized, hard-dried fried noodle blocks are piled in front of the cooking station like a small mountain. [^5][^6] Jiao Tong wrote that stir-fried eel noodles often use a type of noodle called _yifu mian_ (伊府麵). This noodle is made by mixing egg liquid into the dough, cutting it into fine strips, boiling it to seventy percent cooked, then frying and drying it. He described its appearance when entering the wok: "The _yifu mian_ looks like instant noodles, appearing parched and hungry; once it enters the pan, it greedily absorbs the starch glaze and becomes soft in an instant." [^2] When Mao Da Ye saw the pile of pre-fried egg noodles on the counter at A-Yuan's on Chenggong Road, he also remarked that they looked somewhat like instant noodles. [^7]

A-Yuan's master at the Chenggong Road shop told Mao Da Ye that the ingredients used in this dish are starch powder, sugar, soy sauce, and lard, seasoned with onion, garlic, chili, and pepper; the key to deliciousness is heat control. [^10] As for vinegar, Jiao Tong noted that this dish primarily uses black vinegar, rice wine, and sugar to achieve a sweet and sour balance, with a flavor similar to _wuliu geng_ (five-stream soup). The most critical element is the vinegar; some sophisticated establishments use several types of vinegar to blend, which they call a "secret passed down through generations." [^2] Stir-fried eel can be glazed into a sweet-and-sour soup or dry-fried for a savory flavor; both styles are available in Tainan. [^4]

Fu Sheng Xiaoshi (福昇小食) on Guohua Street has been open for over fifty years, and the owner's wife became an internet sensation on Instagram for her stir-frying skills. Reporter Jiang Ting-yi of _Super Taste_ (_Shi Shang Wan Jia_) wrote that when this shop stir-fries eel, flames burst from the wok, and the owner's wife must cook while dodging the fire, making her "movements look like she is dancing the cha-cha." [^14]

Writing about Sakaribara, Yu Fu said that in Tainan, an eel can be stir-fried into a sizzling plate in just twenty-seven seconds by the "Eel Liao" (鳝魚廖) in the market, who "particularly enjoys performing the so-called 27-second stir-fry." Twenty-seven seconds is the signature written on that shop's wall and performed for its customers. [^4][^1] In 2022, a CTV reporter in Tainan watched an owner stir-fry over high heat before adding a secret sauce, with the narration stating the dish was plated in just over twenty seconds. [^8] The "twenty-seven seconds" on the wall and the "twenty-some seconds" in the narration measure the same thing: keeping the eel in the wok for a short enough time to preserve that crispness Wang Hao-yi spoke of. Yu Fu said the Liao brothers learned this technique from a Fuzhou master.

## The "Knife" Taught by a Fuzhou Master

Having lived in the capital of Tainan for ten years, Yu Fu wrote about the architecture and food in the alleys of the city in _Tainan Alleys_ (_Tai Nan Xiang Zi Nei_). In the chapter on stir-fried eel, he wrote: "In Tainan, those who stir-fry eel are mostly surnamed Liao; legendary figures like the brothers Liao Bing-nan and Liao Huo-tu represent two generations of my age group." These two brothers learned the art of the "knife" from a Fuzhou master. Yu Fu explained that this refers to the way of cooking, adding that stir-frying eel is actually not that difficult: "The technique for thickening a soup is similar; once you can integrate the two, you have graduated." Liao Bing-nan later earned the nickname "Eel Nan" (鳝魚南), and the Liao brothers passed the technique down through generations. [^4]

A-Hui, who stir-fries eel on Ximen Road, is not surnamed Liao. _Super Taste_ wrote that the first-generation owner, Hsu Yin, was a _zongpu shi_ (總鋪師)—a master chef for banquet catering (_banzhuo_)—who learned to stir-fry eel from a Fuzhou master; this shop has been open in Tainan for over fifty years. [^9]

According to local lore, Liao Bing-nan originally worked in a gold shop. It was his third brother who first started selling stir-fried eel, prompting him to change careers and set up a small stall in the You'ai Street Market. When the third brother eventually changed professions, Liao Bing-nan made a name for himself through his skill with high-heat stir-frying and a "low profit, high volume" business model. [^10]

The market where Liao Bing-nan had his stall was Sakaribara. [^11] This name comes from the Japanese word _moriba_ (盛り場), meaning a bustling place where crowds gather. In 1932, to complete the Ximen Ring Road project, the Tainan City Hall moved over one hundred night market stalls from a small park in Ximen-cho to a reclaimed fishpond area behind the West Market; this bustling "Tenmachi Night Market" was referred to in contemporary reports as a _moriba_. A few years later, the vendors moved to the current location of Kangle Market, retaining the Japanese pronunciation and being written in Chinese characters as Sakaribara. [^12]

## A-Yuan Started at Fourteen with His Father; A-Jiang at Seventeen with His Third Uncle

Liao Bing-nan's youngest son took his mother's surname; his nickname "A-Yuan" is more famous than his real name, Song Song-wei. Writing about him in 2015, Mao Da Ye noted that he began selling stir-fried eel with his father at age fourteen. The shop was originally on Zhengyi Street (later renamed Xinmei Street) before moving to Chenggong Road. [^10]

Writing about A-Jiang on Minzu Road in 2022, Mao Da Ye said he learned the trade from A-Yuan: "A-Yuan's nephew is A-Jiang; at age 17, A-Jiang began apprenticing under his third uncle A-Yuan and opened his own shop shortly after finishing military service." [^13] He recounted how A-Jiang's master proudly said that selling eel is a long-term endeavor requiring speed and precision in seasoning—true skill earned through practice. [^13] Yu Fu also noted that A-Jiang's brother recently started his own business, "Second Brother's Stir-fried Eel." Looking at the Liao family's stir-fry businesses in Sakaribara—A-Yuan, A-Jiang, and Second Brother—Yu Fu suggested they are largely from the same family. [^4]

Regarding the Sakaribara lineage, Wang Hao-yi recorded the succession as the first generation "Eel Nan," the second generation "Eel Lu," and the third generation Liao Kuo-hsiung. [^5] In 2013, a blogger chatted with the owner after eating and noted a ladle in the shop that had been used since the 49th year of the Republic (1960). [^1] Counting from the generation of the Liao brothers, the family's woks have been located in Sakaribara, Chenggong Road, and Minzu Road.

Writing about A-Jiang, Mao Da Ye also mentioned that stir-frying eel is physically demanding work. [^13] The address on Ximen Road Section 2 was originally A-Tie Stir-fried Eel; after Master A-Tie retired, the shop was taken over by A-Hui. [^10] The craft at the wok is passed down generation by generation; as for the eels in the pan, it depends on how they change in the fields and ditches.

## Eels in Rice Paddy Ditches are Becoming Scarcer

In his 2007 article, Jiao Tong wrote that the period from the fifth to the seventh lunar month is the breeding season for eels and also when fat eels draw gourmets to their doors. [^2] The formal name of the eel is the ricefield eel, scientific name _Monopterus albus_, also known colloquially as "field eel" or "field manatee." [^15] According to the Academia Sinica Taiwan Fish Database, they live in muddy-bottomed waters such as rice paddies, ponds, rivers, and ditches; they can also be found in marshes and flooded fields. They like to burrow and are active at night, using skin folds in their mouths to breathe air directly. During winter and the dry season, they burrow one to two meters underground, often living in small groups; they are distributed throughout the freshwater waters of western Taiwan. [^16] This burrowing fish that can breathe air lives in the mud at the bottom of rice paddy ditches.

In May 1930, the Chianan Irrigation System was completed. After it was finished and water began flowing, cultivated paddy fields increased while dry fields decreased. [^17] Senior travel and food reporter Chen Chih-tung described the ditches of that time to _Mirror Media_: "With the completion of Wusantou and the Chianan Irrigation System in 1930, the irrigation channels along the line were all earthen ditches, where eels lived happy, soft lives burrowing in the ditches and paddy edges. When farmers worked the fields, they would also catch eels and sea bass to bring home for nourishment." [^18] The same report continued that as earthen ditches turned into concrete channels, Taiwan's eels became scarce, and most are now imported. [^18] Yu Fu wrote that wild eels, which could once be caught with a bamboo pole, hemp line, and hook, are now "nowhere to be fished." [^4]

In 2015, A-Yuan's master told Mao Da Ye that there used to be many eels in the southern rice paddies, but now they are gone and must be imported, which naturally makes them more expensive. [^10] In 2021, _ETtoday_ introduced Fu Sheng Xiaoshi on Guohua Street, noting its use of imported wild eel. [^19] The Academia Sinica Taiwan Fish Database lists the ricefield eel as an economically important edible fish; most eels on the market are farmed, with more production in the south, and they are mostly sliced and stir-fried in Taiwan. [^16] The international database _FishBase_ also lists it as a species with commercial fishing and aquaculture, even traded in the aquarium market. [^20]

Wang Hao-yi places "freshness" as the first of the four requirements for stir-fried eel, seeking tender meat and a sweet flavor. He recorded the process: caught eels are first "rested" in fresh water for a few days to remove the muddy smell; when slaughtering, one uses "a short drill to pierce through the head to fix the eel to a wooden board," then "uses a sharp blade to cut off the head and spine in one stroke," leaving the bloody flesh spread out ready for frying. [^5] Jiao Tong wrote in 2007 that eels must be kept in fresh water for one or two days to remove the mud smell; only freshly slaughtered ones are sweet. Because eels are active and slippery, the knife must be fast and the depth precise, slicing through the belly and spine in one motion—a common scene at eel stalls. He also noted that many shops do not process the eels themselves, leaving the meat with a muddy, gamey smell. [^2]

Yu Fu's book leaves a comparison of past and present: "In the early days, shop owners had to handle the slaughtering themselves; I heard A-Yuan can still perform this specialty, but nowadays, freshness is hard to come by." [^4] The Taiwan Fish Database notes that ricefield eels are generally sold live. [^16] Writing in 2015, Mao Da Ye noted that because eels are difficult to handle and prone to smelling fishy, most famous eel shops do not sell eel soup; only A-Yuan, who has a unique way of handling them, does. [^10] Before an eel goes from being live to entering the pan, it must undergo several stages of preparation.

Jiao Tong, who insists on freshness, left a strong remark in the same article: "Most eels nowadays are frozen imports, hardly worth mentioning." [^2] He cited an exception from 2007: at that time, Qingxiang Eel Noodles in Xinying, Tainan County, still used wild eel. Xinying has many ponds and streams, so farmers would catch them at night and bring them to the market the next day. That shop even used rice bran to fuel its stove; he noted that rice bran has a low ignition point and catches fire quickly, making it perfect for high-heat stir-frying. Jiao Tong incorporates the origin of the fish into the standard for measuring a plate of stir-fried eel.

The origin of the fish is mostly something the person at the wok cannot decide alone. The few dozen seconds after the eel enters the pan, and the small amount of sugar and starch glaze added before plating, are what they master every day. In that CTV interview, a Tainan owner said that besides adding a bit more granulated sugar to Tainan's eel noodles, the secret sauce and the level of starch thickening are also part of the craft. [^8]

## For Friends Who Find the Soup Too Sweet, They Are Taken for Dry-Fried Versions

When Yu Fu took friends out for stir-fried eel in Tainan, he often encountered the same reaction. He wrote in his book: "Whenever I treat people to stir-fried eel, most complain the soup is too sweet! So I take them to A-Jiang's for the savory dry-fried version, which finally shuts them up." [^4] A 2019 report by _Mirror Media_ also listed dry-fried eel noodles as A-Jiang's signature dish. [^21] Yu Fu also mentioned that besides egg noodles, one can specifically request _you mian_ (油麵, oil noodles), which offers a different flavor profile. [^4]

Shi Rui-bin grew up in the West Central District of Tainan; his father, Shi Hung-yao, founded Dongfu Electric in 1973 to manufacture small appliances. In 2019, speaking to _Mirror Media_ about his favorite, A-Jiang on Minzu Road, he spoke of this shop's sweetness: "This place is actually quite famous now; their egg noodles tend to be sweet, which fits our Tainan preference for sweet food. It is the eel noodle dish I must eat every time I return to Taiwan from Shenzhen." [^21]

Director Wei Te-sheng, a Tainan native, said on a program in 2022 that his favorites are _tu-yu geng_ (土魠魚羹, Spanish mackerel soup) and eel noodles. Although they can be found in Taipei, he said, "I've eaten them in Taipei, but it's just not right; no matter how I eat it, it's not right." When asked if it was because Tainan people prefer sweetness, he replied: "That is a prejudice—it's not right. So every time I hear that Tainan people eat very sweet things, I have to debate and defend them." [^8][^22]

Two Tainan natives: one says hometown people love sweets; the other feels the need to defend against such claims. Regarding the same A-Jiang, Yu Fu takes friends for the savory dry-fried version, while Shi Rui-bin says it is sweet and suits Tainan tastes. When CTV reporters asked vendors in both the north and south, Tainan vendors explained the difference: "The methods are just one is dry-fried and the other is glazed; the glazed version is the more traditional taste. People in the north don't eat it as sweet." Taipei vendors, meanwhile, said: "Taipei people aren't used to it being this sweet; they prefer it a bit more savory." [^8]

In 2021, _ETtoday_ introduced Fu Sheng Xiaoshi on Guohua Street, noting its soup has a stronger sourness, is stir-fried with plenty of onion over high heat, and does not have too much "Tainan sweetness." _Super Taste_ wrote that its broth is sweet and sour with a certain thickness from the starch, and besides dry-fried, there is also a _lu mian_ (魯麵, braised noodle) version. [^19][^14] Cheng Bian Zhen Wei (城邊真味), established in 1970 near the Dongmen Ring Road, is a simple shop included in the Michelin Guide; the guide states its stir-fried eel is sweet and sour, and like the stir-fried eel dish itself, one can choose whether or not to have it glazed. [^23][^24]

Heading north to Tuku, Yunlin, _Mirror Media_ wrote about a sixty-year-old shop where the starch glaze is thick and the flavor is more savory than sweet, using _you mian_ (oil noodles). The owner, Yao Li-chiu, said that farmers coming from the fields find the thick soup more filling and flavorful. When eating there, one also adds Wu-yin vinegar and chili powder; the reporter called this "Plains Style," noting its flavor is completely different from Tainan's eel noodles. [^18]

In 2015, A-Yuan's master told Mao Da Ye that people used to prefer heavier flavors, but the dry-fried versions have been slightly adjusted now to be lighter. [^10] Whether it is Taipei vendors reducing the sweetness or A-Yuan's master making the dry-fried version lighter, the adjustments are all made by the person standing at the wok.

## A-Yuan's Master and Son Stir-fry Together; The Son Wields the Ladle

In July 2015, Mao Da Ye posted an article titled "A-Yuan's Stir-fried Eel: The History of Tainan Stir-fried Eel and a Unique Eel Soup," describing his visit to A-Yuan on Chenggong Road. The small shop has limited seating, and A-Yuan has always insisted on specializing in stir-fried eel, only later adding rice noodles; the eel is available both glazed and dry-fried, along with a bowl of eel soup. On the cooking station sits a basin of bright red, processed eel, alongside pre-fried egg noodles. [^10]

While Jiao Tong observed the high heat and smoke from the ladle-wielder, Mao Da Ye recorded on that day at Chenggong Road how A-Yuan's master cooked alongside his son, with the son primarily wielding the ladle. [^10]

In 2024, Mao Da Ye once again summarized Tainan's eel noodles: the old-brand "Eel Liao" in Sakaribara with "27 seconds" on its wall has closed; at the shop on Chenggong Road, A-Yuan—who began selling eel with his father at age fourteen—has handed the ladle over to his son. [^11]
