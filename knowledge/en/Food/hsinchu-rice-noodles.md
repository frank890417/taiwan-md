> **30-Second Overview:** Hsinchu Rice Noodles are not merely a regional specialty made from one type of rice. It grew in the area of Nan-shi and Keya-xi in Hsinchu, completed by paddy rice, stream water, sunlight, and the Jiuhang wind for drying; according to the Rice Noodle Guild, there were over a hundred factories in Da-Nan-shi in the early 1960s. Later, corn starch, mechanization, and packaging labels changed the product, also presenting consumers with a question: when we say "Hsinchu Rice Noodles," are we referring to rice, wind, place, or a set of processes?

## A Rice Noodle Hub is First Land, Then a Brand

In 1999, _Taiwan Guanghua_ magazine documented the rice noodle industry in the Nan-shi area of Hsinchu. The report quoted entrepreneur Kuo Yuan-yuan, who said: about a hundred years ago, Master Kuo, a rice noodle artisan from Hui'an, Fujian, moved from Tamsui to Hsinchu because the strong winds and sunlight here were suitable for drying noodles; his brothers and family followed along. The focus of the story was not an "inventor," but a place where the method found sustenance. [^1]

Historical records from the Hsinchu Rice Noodle Guild provide finer details on the location: early producers were concentrated in Nan-shi Village, downstream of Keya-xi, and around Wantan. The stream meandered, the river plain was flat, the sand and stones dried easily when heated, and there was wind by the stream. The finished rice noodles were carried one strand at a time to the riverside fields, and over time, the place where rice noodles were dried came to be called "Mifenpu" (Rice Noodle Field). [^2]

The "rice noodle hub" was not initially equivalent to all of Hsinchu. Guild records cite local placename studies, stating that Da-Nan-shi became a rice noodle manufacturing area due to the conditions of paddy rice production, water drawn from Keya-xi, and the unique winds of Hsinchu. The early 1960s were the peak period of production, with over one hundred factories, leading to the designation of "rice noodle hub" for Da-Nan-shi. [^2]

The wind here is not background music in tourism copy. It is an unstable piece of equipment incorporated into the process by artisans: if it dries too slowly, the rice noodles are prone to moisture; if it dries too quickly, the texture and shape may be affected. In a 2022 interview with Xinhua Rice Noodles, the entrepreneur described the role of natural air drying in the process as "3 parts sun-drying, 7 parts wind-drying, creating 100 points of rice noodles." This is the interviewee's metaphor, not a scientific ratio applicable to all factories. [^3]

> 📝 Curator's Note: Hsinchu Rice Noodles are most easily written as a "Wind City specialty," but what is truly interesting is that the wind must appear alongside the river plain, rice fields, rooftops, and the time of the workers; if any one element is missing, the place is left with only a label.

## Coarse Rice Noodles and Fine Cooked Noodles Are Not the Same Texture

Image page: [Rice vermicelli.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rice_vermicelli.jpg), original image URL: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6f/Rice_vermicelli.jpg._

"Rice noodles" in daily Taiwanese language often encompasses different products. _Taiwan Guanghua_ magazine referred to the traditionally coarser product as _shuifen_ (water noodles) because after being shaped, it was boiled in hot water, then cooled, scooped out, and dried. Later, molding technology allowed rice noodles to be made finer, and fine strands were steamed to become cooked noodles (_chuifen_). [^1]

Local exploration articles from iSee Taiwan also distinguished them by process: coarse rice noodles are pressed into strips, boiled, cooled, and sun-dried. Fine rice noodles are pressed into thin strands, steamed, and then sun-dried. The article places Hsinchu Rice Noodles in the latter path and points out that glutinous rice is a key raw material for traditional rice noodles. [^4]

Therefore, "Hsinchu Rice Noodles" is not just an issue of coarseness or fineness. The rice content, starch type, shaping method, steaming sequence, and drying conditions all affect the elasticity, soup-absorbing ability, and tendency to break when eaten. A 2013 interview with Cai Shen-xing, the general secretary of the Hsinchu Rice Noodle Guild by upstream/downstream reporters, recorded producers transitioning from pure rice production to incorporating corn starch, partly because the market desired noodles that were more Q (chewy), longer, and more durable; this is the interviewee's account of industry history and cannot replace government standards or the common history of all producers. [^5]

The "tradition" of rice noodles is therefore not a straight line. Some adhere to pure rice methods, some use starch to adjust texture and output, and others view mechanization as a condition that factories can retain. The 1999 _Guanghua_ report mentioned that there were over one hundred rice noodle factories in the Nan-shi area, with only about twenty remaining at the time. The same report also documented how automation increased the output of some factories. [^1]

Academic research views this change within local society: Hsinchu Rice Noodles have a history spanning over a century, but the consumer society and industrial transformation have reduced the connection between production and the local environment; on the other hand, the technology accumulated by producers, the milling machinery, and the factory system still form a set of local industrial culture. [^6]

## In 2013, the Name Was Dissected for Scrutiny

Around 2013, rice noodle content and packaging labels became a food controversy across Taiwan. Upstream/downstream reports cited consumer groups exposing that commercially sold rice noodles had low rice content, and interviewed the Hsinchu Rice Noodle Guild; Cai Shen-xing described the issue in interviews as a collision of false labeling, industrial costs, taste preferences, and brand protection. The report also recorded his original words: "Rice noodles are not just rice noodles," but this sentence represents the industry's position from the guild, not a disclaimer on food labeling. [^5]

The Ministry of Health and Welfare explained that it announced packaging standards for packaged rice noodle products on November 29, 2013, effective July 1, 2014. According to the official statement, only if the rice content was 100% could the product name use "Pure Rice Noodles (Strands)" or "Rice Noodles (Strands)"; if the rice content exceeded 50%, it could be labeled "Blended Rice Noodles (Strands)"; and if it did not reach 50%, these names could not be claimed. [^7]

The Food and Drug Administration further clarified on June 30, 2014, that "Hsinchu Rice Noodles" had not been eliminated, nor were all products forced to be renamed "Hsinchu Cooked Noodles." If a product used the special process of Hsinchu Rice Noodles or was produced locally in Hsinchu and met the 100% rice content standard, there was no legal issue with labeling it "Hsinchu Rice Noodles." [^8]

A difference often omitted here is: **the place name can refer to the origin or process, but the product name must be accountable for its contents.** Upstream/downstream reports of the discussion at the time mentioned a claim using 25% rice content as a reference ratio for a local brand; the same article also clearly stated that this was only a direction of discussion, not an established current standard, and recorded concerns from scholars and consumer groups about potential confusion caused by low-threshold naming. [^9]

Therefore, the controversy surrounding "Hsinchu Rice Noodles" cannot be simplified to pure rice versus fake noodles. It involves at least three issues simultaneously: what consumers expect from a name, how producers balance taste and cost in production, and how the government clarifies expectations through verifiable labeling. The local brand must remain, not by stuffing all variations into one nostalgic story, but by allowing the rice content, process, and origin of different products to be seen.

> 📝 Curator's Note: A name is not just a shell for flavor. When a local specialty enters a supermarket, it simultaneously becomes memory, trademark, and an ingredient label that must be understood.

## The Factory Rooftop Catches Weather and Succession Issues

The industry predicament of the rice noodle hub is not only about recipes. In a 2023 report on the Hsinchu Rice Noodles Festival (which was renamed Zhuxian Specialty Festival), the Rice Noodle Guild did not participate because the event moved away from the Nan-shi Rice Noodle Hub, which had weaker cultural connections; the report cited local accounts that during the peak period, there were over a hundred factories, but later, due to large-scale industrialization, aging labor in family industries, young people failing to take over, and land/factory management issues, only more than ten remained. This is an interviewee's statement within a controversial event, and the number should not be taken as an official city census without context from the report. [^10]

The same industry is also seeking another form of export. In a 2022 report, Cai Shen-xing, in charge of Xinhua Rice Noodles, viewed foreign sales as the main operating goal for Hsinchu Rice Noodles factories; facing low-price competition from other countries, producers must differentiate in quality, brand, and sales; the report also mentioned that experience activities allowed visitors to practically engage with steaming, cutting, kneading, dispersing, shaping, and drying noodles. [^3]

These actions do not look like "cultural preservation," but they are closer to industrial reality than a mere statement of preserving tradition. For rice noodles to survive, someone must be willing to start working before dawn, a factory must be willing to invest in machinery, consumers must be willing to read labels, and there must be local events that treat the rice noodle hub as an origin, not just background decoration.

Local industry research places the rice noodles back into the landscape, bringing issues of brand, technology, and market. The research points out that the technology accumulated by producers, the tools provided by machinery factories, and the local institutional power intertwine with consumer purchasing and culinary memory to form the industrial culture of Hsinchu Rice Noodles. Rice noodles are not a completed relic, but a production relationship that is still adjusting. [^6]

## After the Fried Rice Noodles Are Served

In the past, rice noodles were foods mainly consumed during celebratory banquets and festivals; today, they have become everyday items in noodle soups, fried rice noodles, and household dry goods cabinets. The iSee Taiwan article described this change as a process from an expensive festive food to a daily comfort food; the important thing is not the word "popularization," but the fact that rice noodles moved from special occasions into the schedules of more people. [^4]

Hsinchu Rice Noodles are not only eaten in Hsinchu. _Guanghua_ reported how vendors around Hsinchu City God Temple gather rice noodles, meatballs, and other snacks at a single city entrance; here, rice noodles are not an isolated local specialty but food distributed by the market, temple entrance, tourism, and urban reputation combined. [^1]

In one bowl of fried rice noodles, there may be a mixture that does not belong to the same era: fine strands waiting to dry on the rice noodle hub's roof, machinery controlling steam in the factory, the rice content on the packaging bag, and the familiar texture that requires no explanation at the dining table. This mixture does not need to be beautified as "unchanging ancient methods." It is more like the truth of many foods in Taiwan: tradition is not rejecting change, but answering what one still wants to preserve with every change.

The truly important aspect of Hsinchu Rice Noodles may not be whether it can be preserved as a pure version, but how it pulls together a city's natural conditions, immigrant technology, family industry, food regulations, and consumer memory into a single fine thread. The wind dries the rice noodles, and it also blows the name further away; the farther the name drifts, the more someone needs to look back and clarify where it comes from, how it is made, and what it has become now.
