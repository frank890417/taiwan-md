Around the Winter Solstice, processors in the Taiwan Strait's mouth area bleed the mullet while it is still in the pond. Mullet typically swim near Taiwan's coast from late November through late January of the following year, with the main fishing season falling within about ten days centered on the Winter Solstice. Western coastal fishermen therefore call it the "letter fish": when the season arrives, it is expected to show up. [^1]

> **30-second overview:** Mullet bottarga is a pair of ovaries from a mature female mullet, preserved through bleeding, salting, pressing, and drying to retain oil, saltiness, and chewiness. Wild fish runs once marked it as a seasonal sign; today, two-to-three-year aquaculture cycles in places like the Taiwan Strait, cold-chain processing, and the northward shift of wild fisheries make "good bottarga" the result of both sea conditions and human hands. [^1] [^3] [^4]

## A Letter Arrives from the Sea Before the Winter Solstice

The formal Chinese scientific name for mullet is _Mugil_ (grey mullet), commonly known in folk terms as "black fish." The National Cultural Memory Bank cites the _Taiwan Gazette_ noting that mullet caught before the Winter Solstice ("head-on black fish") tend to be plumper, while those caught after ("returning black fish") are leaner. This fish has long carried the maritime calendar onto coastal tables and livelihoods. [^2]

There are records of mullet fishing dating back to the Dutch colonial period; during the Dutch era, boats engaged in mullet fishing were even required to pay a mullet tax. Mullet bottarga was not merely packaged into gift boxes later—it was a method of extending the brief fish run into the festival season. [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** "Letter fish" is more than a romantic nickname. Fish runs are roughly predictable, which allows fishermen to schedule their boats, wind, and family New Year plans on the same calendar.

## Salt Keeps the Migration on Shore

According to data from the Ministry of Agriculture, the production process is broken down into selecting mature female fish, removing the ovaries, washing, draining blood, salting, desalting, shaping, and drying. If blood is not fully removed, it oxidizes and turns black; too much salt makes the ovaries overly hard; uneven drying affects color and texture. [^3]

| Process                       | Key Handling Point                                    | Result Left on the Product                     |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Bleeding & Cleaning           | Remove blood from microvessels and surface impurities | Reduce oxidation discoloration and off-flavors |
| Salting & Desalting           | Control salt penetration and rinse residual salt      | Balance saltiness and texture                  |
| Pressing & Shaping            | Apply even pressure to both egg sacs                  | Form flat, evenly thick appearance             |
| Sun-drying or Cold-air Drying | Remove moisture and continue aging                    | Retain oil aroma and elastic chewiness         |

Salt content is roughly 10–15% of the ovary weight, with salting lasting about four to five hours. Traditional sun-drying and air-drying take about three to five days. These figures are starting points for judgment, not mechanical formulas, because ripeness, ovary size, and weather all change the craftsman's scale. [^3]

Speaking of his early days working with salted mullet roe, Tsai Deng-jinin from Annan, Tainan said: "One piece of oyster pancake pairs perfectly with a bowl of rice porridge." Later, mullet bottarga moved closer to banquet tables and gift-giving, but the underlying logic remains the same: using salt to hand over perishable seafood to longer time. [^5]

## The Ponds of the Taiwan Strait, Waiting Two to Three Years Before One Winter

At the processing site of brothers Chuang Guo-xian and Chuang Guo-sheng, the technology behind mullet bottarga becomes tangible: the mullet is bled in the pond first, then transported at low temperatures. The extracted fish roe must be scraped clean of blood and mucus before entering the salting, pressing, and drying stages. [^5]

They adjust their methods through post-sun-drying with cold air and reduced salt content. Individual cases cannot represent all producers, but they are enough to reveal the differences in a single piece of mullet bottarga—coming from the pond, transportation, roe extraction, salting, and drying environment as a whole chain. [^5] [^6]

Research from the Taiwan Strait area shows that the life cycle of farmed mullet typically takes two to three years, with seed selection, pond environment, processing, grading, and marketing all interconnected. Wild or farmed are not two mutually exclusive report cards. The former points to the rarity of fish runs; the latter reminds us that consistent flavor must also be patiently cultivated. [^4]

## One Mullet, More Than Just One Piece of Bottarga

The Ministry of Agriculture classifies mullet bottarga, mullet roe, and mullet stomach as the "Three Treasures of Mullet": mullet bottarga comes from a pair of ovaries of a female mullet; mullet roe or mullet fat is the milt of a male mullet; mullet stomach, also called mullet tripe or mullet stomach sac, refers to the gastric sac. The fish body can be made into noodle soup or cooked dishes, making mullet bottarga a highlight of whole-fish utilization rather than its sole value. [^7]

In 1909, the Japanese hired a mullet bottarga manufacturing expert from Nagasaki and established processing plants in Kaohsiung and Lukang. This marks a chapter in the systematization of modern processing industries. Even earlier, coastal communities had already accumulated their own sense of time through mullet fishing and preserving seafood. [^8]

In the "Mullet Pays Homage to Mazu" legend passed down in Jiucuo, Kaohsiung, fishermen and mullet agree to stop fishing on the 15th day after the Winter Solstice, holding a sea ceremony after the fishing season ends. This is not an accurate historical record, but local memory: people place their dependence on fish runs and anxiety over overfishing into a story that can be retold. [^8]

## Fisheries Moving North, Making the "Letter Fish" Harder to Wait For

Data compiled by the Fisheries Research Institute from 1966 to 2019 found that the main catch locations of wild mullet have been shifting northward year by year. In 2018 and 2019, areas north of Taichung accounted for over 95% of the total catch nationwide. Traditional fishing grounds in southern Yunlin and Chiayi have thus had to wait longer for the fish to arrive. [^1]

The study does not simplify the changes into a single climate narrative. Fish stock abundance, sea conditions, marine meteorology, long-term climate change, and fishing pressure may all affect the habitat, migration, and aggregation of fish populations, and at present, the subsequent changes in fishing grounds remain difficult to fully predict. [^1]

> **Data on Mullet:** In 2019, Taiwan's total mullet catch was 3,597 tons, with a value of approximately NT$51.3 million; wild nearshore fishing accounted for about 59% of the value, while land-based aquaculture accounted for about 41%. This represents the industrial profile of that year, not a forecast of today's market or future fishing conditions. [^1]

## Origin and Processing Locations Are Two Different Coordinates

The flavor of mullet bottarga depends on processing, but "where it is processed" and "where the mullet roe comes from" are not the same thing. Customs authorities determine the origin of goods involving multiple countries' materials or processing based on the principle of the last substantial transformation location; this is the standard for trade management. [^9]

Therefore, when facing "Taiwan mullet bottarga," it is worth asking another layer of questions: does the packaging indicate the processing location, the source of the main ingredients, or both? The publication _Upstream and Downstream_ discusses possible information gaps between the two using mullet roe as an example. This is an issue of institutional frameworks and informed rights, not equivalent to making any unlawful determination about any product. It reminds consumers they can inquire about ingredient origins, whether wild or farmed, processing location, and storage conditions. [^10]

## Before Being Sliced, the Cold Chain Is Still in Handover

Data from the Ministry of Agriculture indicates that mullet bottarga should be stored by freezing or refrigeration. If stored for more than four months, fat oxidation may cause the color to turn dark red. If not consumed immediately after cutting, it should still be sealed and kept at a low temperature. [^3]

| What to Look At        | What You Can Read                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Appearance & Texture   | Two kidney-shaped egg sacs, evenly thick, slightly translucent, with uniform egg grains; pressing lightly leaves no fingerprint, usually indicating appropriate dryness. |
| Ingredient Information | The origin of the fish roe, whether wild or farmed, and the processing location—all different but equally important information.                                         |
| Serving Method         | Can be seared and paired with garlic scapes, pears, or apples; also suitable for fried rice and Italian pasta. [^5] [^12]                                                |

Food researcher Xu Zhong said: "Perfection has no end; one must always try to find interesting observation points." Wild or farmed, sun-dried or cold-air dried, garlic scapes or fruit—none of these need to be compressed into a single orthodoxy. They are different entry points for understanding the same seafood flavor. [^5]

Mullet bottarga is not only valuable when wild, nor is it merely an expensive souvenir. It compresses the migration of the sea, the waiting in the pond, and the repeated turning of the craftsman's hands into a slice of time that can be cut open. The fish may not arrive according to the old calendar, but people still use salt, wind, and gestures to try to keep one migration on the tongue.
