# Nutritious Lunches: From Skim Milk Powder to Fluorescent Curry, a Century of Transformation on Taiwan's Students' Tables

> **30-Second Overview:** In 1951, UN skim milk powder kicked off the era of nutritious lunches in Taiwan, changing students' meals from mere home cooking into something more. From the dominance of flour-based foods during the US aid era to the localized transition promoting rice consumption, and finally to today's widespread food education and free meal policies, this century of transformation has not only fed generations of students but also reflects the profound evolution of Taiwan's socio-economics, educational philosophies, and culinary culture.

In 1951, the United Nations International Save the Children Fund donated 5,000 tons of skim milk powder, marking the beginning of school meal programs in Taiwan; for the first time, powdered milk began appearing on students' tables[^4]. This was not only the starting point for nutritious lunches in Taiwan but also symbolized a shift in children's diets from a purely domestic sphere toward social care. From the flour and skim milk powder of the US aid era to today's diverse development emphasizing food education and local ingredients, the century-long evolution of Taiwan's nutritious lunches has not only fed generations of students but also reflects the profound progress of Taiwan's socio-economics, educational philosophies, and culinary culture. This article will explore the historical evolution of nutritious lunches in Taiwan, analyze their development under different administrations, touch upon the highly discussed free meal policies in recent years, and revisit those classic dishes that students both love and hate—such as _sansese dou_ (three-color peas), "telephone wires," fluorescent curry, and traditional lunch soups and steamed bento boxes.

## Historical Evolution: Imprints of an Era on the Dining Table

The development of nutritious lunches in Taiwan did not happen overnight; its roots can be traced back hundreds of years, presenting different facets across various periods of governance.

### Japanese Colonial Period: The Introduction of School Feeding

The Japanese colonial period (1895–1945) was a foundational era for Taiwan's modern education system and laid the groundwork for the development of nutritious lunches. Japan's experience implementing _gakko kyushoku_ (school feeding) in its home country was partially brought to Taiwan. Although initially targeting Japanese students or specific schools, models of schools providing meals began to emerge, such as simple meals consisting of _onigiri_ (rice balls), grilled fish, and pickled vegetables to improve students' nutritional status [^3]. For example, the "Taiwan Governor-General's Office National Language School Third Attached Higher Girls' School" (the predecessor of today's Taipei First Girls' High School), established in 1904, primarily enrolled Japanese female students; such schools may have already had relatively sophisticated meal arrangements at the time [^9] [^10] [^11]. During this period, the concept of school feeding began to sprout, laying the foundation for the post-war nutritious lunch system.

📝 Curator's Note: Although the scale of school feeding during the Japanese colonial period was limited, it was the origin of the collective dining experience for Taiwanese students and foreshadowed the social role that nutritious lunches would play in the future.

### Early Post-War and US Aid Era: Skim Milk Powder and Lunch Soup

After Taiwan's retrocession, severe malnutrition among schoolchildren occurred due to the scarcity of goods following the war. In 1951, UNICEF donated 5,000 tons of skim milk powder, marking the beginning of school feeding in Taiwan, with milk stations providing prepared milk for students to drink [^4]. In 1954, the US Food for Peace program also began providing free lunches to primary school students across Taiwan [^4].

In 1957, the Taiwan Provincial Government Department of Education and the China Rural Reconstruction Commission selected mountain primary schools in areas such as Pingtung and Taoyuan to pilot nutritious lunches. The menu included vegetables grown by the students themselves, skim milk powder donated by the UN, yeast provided by the government, and seaweed, dried small fish, and soy products purchased with student meal fees [^4].

In 1964, China and the United States signed a National School Lunch Program agreement. The US government provided commodities such as wheat, flour, durum wheat, skim milk powder, and cooking oil to serve as staples for students' nutritious lunches; the government bore the cost of purchasing side dishes like vegetables, fish, and beans [^5] [^12] [^13]. According to statistics, the US provided 13,060 tons of wheat, 5,760 tons of durum wheat, 2,880 tons of skim milk powder, and 1,152 tons of cooking oil in installments [^14]. During this period, the "lunch soup" frequently consumed by students was a representative nutritious soup of the US aid era, a concept derived from the 19th-century European relief food known as "Lundford porridge" [^5].

📝 Curator's Note: US aid commodities not only solved the post-war food shortage in Taiwan but also indirectly shaped the dietary habits of Taiwanese students; the popularity of wheat-based foods stands as a testament to this history.

### Modernization and Popularization: From Steamed Bento to Central Kitchens

With economic development, Taiwan's nutritious lunches gradually transitioned from the US aid model toward self-reliance. In 1973, after the Republic of China withdrew from the United Nations, the "Student Lunch Self-Reliance Plan" was formulated, borrowing commodities from the United States while continuing the "one wheat product, one vegetable, and one soup" menu pattern from the US aid era [^4].

📝 Curator's Note: The transition from US aid to self-reliance was not just a change in the source of supplies, but also a microcosm of the changes in Taiwan's international status and a demonstration of national autonomous development.

In the era before nutritious lunches became widespread, many students would bring _zheng bento_ (steamed bento) from home to school. Schools were equipped with "rice steamers" so that students could eat hot meals at noon [^6]. This culture of _zheng bento_ is not only a shared memory for many Taiwanese people but also reflects the importance families placed on children's diets at the time. However, with the popularization of school nutritious lunches and the rise of central kitchens, the _zheng bento_ culture gradually declined [^6].

📝 Curator's Note: The disappearance of the steamed bento symbolizes Taiwan's social shift from family-provided meals in the agricultural era to the industrialized, collectivized school meal model—a micro-manifestation of social change.

In the late 1960s, "Wheat Supply Centers" were successively established in Keelung, Taoyuan, Hsinchu, Yunlin, Chiayi, Tainan, Kaohsiung, Penghu, and other areas to provide wheat products to schools. In the 1970s, nutritious lunches began to be promoted to junior high schools, and some schools began experimenting with lunch supplies provided by private catering enterprises [^4].

#### Staple Food Transformation: The Shift from Wheat to Rice

During the US aid era, because the United States provided large quantities of flour, school lunches mostly consisted of wheat products, such as _mantou_ (steamed buns), bread, or noodles [^25]. However, with Taiwan's economic development and adjustments in agricultural policy, the government launched the "Promotion of Rice Consumption" campaign in 1981 to address the domestic surplus of rice production. Consequently, the staple food for student lunches gradually shifted from wheat products to rice [^26] [^27]. This change not only aligned with traditional Taiwanese dietary habits but also promoted local agricultural development, marking an important milestone in the transition of nutritious lunches from reliance on foreign aid to localization.

📝 Curator's Note: The shift from wheat to rice was more than just an adjustment in dietary habits; it was a concrete practice of national agricultural policy and local cultural identity, demonstrating Taiwan's autonomy during its developmental process.

## Classic Dishes and Cultural Phenomena: Memories on the Dining Table

Beyond providing nutrition, the dishes served in Taiwan's nutritious school lunches have created many unique culinary memories, including several "classic" dishes that left a deep impression on schoolchildren.

### Three-Color Peas, Telephone Wires, and Fluorescent Curry: The Love-Hate Relationship with "Hell Food"

In the memories of many former students, several dishes are often jokingly referred to as "hell food," yet they frequently appear on nutritious lunch menus, becoming a unique cultural phenomenon:

📝 Curator's Note: These dishes, mockingly called "hell food," are actually products of the trade-offs between cost, nutrition, and supply efficiency during the development of Taiwan's nutritious school lunches. They have unexpectedly become a shared memory for generations of students.

- **Three-color peas** (_sansè dòu_): Composed of corn kernels, diced carrots, and green peas, they are often picked out by picky eaters due to their monotonous texture and ubiquity. However, because they are easy to prepare and nutritionally balanced, three-color peas remain a common side dish in mass catering [^7].
- **Telephone wires** (_diànhuà xiàn_): This refers to strips of seaweed or squid found in dishes, named for their curly shape which resembles old-fashioned telephone cords. Its formal name is actually _hǎiróng_ (sea tangle), scientifically known as _Cyanea capillata_ (or more accurately, _Himanthalia elongata_ / sea tangle), and is also called "Antarctic bull kelp." It is an imported ingredient from places like Chile and New Zealand [^15] [^16] [^17]. While the seaweed itself is straight, it curls when soaked after being cut into strips and dried. Due to its unique texture and rich dietary fiber, it is often used in cold salads or stir-fries [^17].
- **Fluorescent curry**: This refers specifically to curry with a yellowish-green hue and a mild flavor. This unique color primarily comes from the high proportion of turmeric powder used in Taiwanese-style curry, often thickened with potato starch (_tàibáifěn_) to create a translucent, viscous consistency [^8] [^18] [^19] [^20]. Unlike Japanese or Indian curries, which use curry roux or various spices to achieve a deep brown color, Taiwanese fluorescent curry is a flavor unique to Taiwan. Although opinions are polarized, it remains a part of many people's childhood memories.

While these dishes are often teased, they have become a part of many people's childhood recollections and even spark heated discussions on social media, demonstrating their special status in Taiwan's food culture.

📝 Curator's Note: Whether it is three-color peas, telephone wires, or fluorescent curry, these seemingly ordinary dishes carry the unique collective memory of Taiwanese students, becoming a common topic across generations.

### Traditional Luncheon Soup and Steamed Bento: The Warmth of Tradition

In addition to the "hell food" mentioned above, traditional "luncheon soup" (_wǔshàn tāng_) and "steamed bento" (_zhēng biàndāng_) also represent different eras of nutritious school lunches. Luncheon soup was a common nutritional soup during the US aid era, typically using skimmed milk powder as a base to provide students with basic calories and nutrition [^5]. Meanwhile, steamed bento refers to the traditional method used before nutritious school lunches became widespread, where students brought meals from home to school and heated them using steam rice cookers. This method allowed students to eat the flavors of home, symbolizing familial warmth and care [^6].

📝 Curator's Note: Luncheon soup and steamed bento are two distinctly different meal service models, yet both carry the warmth of their respective eras and the care of the family, serving as indispensable chapters in the history of Taiwan's nutritious school lunches.

## Recent Policies and Challenges: Food Education, Free Meals, and Sustainability

In recent years, the focus of Taiwan's nutritious lunch development has shifted toward the promotion of food education, the implementation of free meal policies, and the challenges of sustainable development.

### Promotion of the 《Food and Agriculture Education Act》

In 2022, Taiwan officially implemented the 《Food and Agriculture Education Act》, which aims to help citizens understand the relationship between agriculture, food, and the environment through education, thereby fostering an identity with local agriculture and culinary culture [^28]. Article 14 of this Act explicitly stipulates that schools providing lunches should prioritize the use of locally produced, traceable ingredients and integrate food and agriculture education into their curricula [^29] [^30]. The implementation of this legislation provides a more solid legal foundation for nutritious lunches, emphasizing that they are not merely about providing meals but are an important educational component, encouraging schools to cooperate with local small-scale farmers to promote "local production for local consumption."

📝 Curator's Note: The promotion of the 《Food and Agriculture Education Act》 marks Taiwan's nutritious lunches moving from simple "filling stomachs" toward "nurturing and educating," treating the dining table as an important educational space to cultivate students' connection to the land and food.

### Food Safety Challenges and Oversight Mechanisms

Throughout the development of nutritious lunches in Taiwan, food safety has always been a focus of social concern. From early incidents of food poisoning to recent food safety storms, these issues have impacted the level of trust in school lunches. This has not only prompted the government to strengthen oversight mechanisms but also deepened the necessity of promoting food education.

#### Food Poisoning Incidents and Food Safety Storms

As early as the 1970s and 1980s, several large-scale food poisoning cases occurred in Taiwan's schools. For example, the 1980 Taoyuan Dacheng Elementary School food poisoning incident resulted in over a thousand teachers and students being hospitalized, prompting the Education Department to review the scale of nutritious lunch operations [^4]. Many of these early incidents were related to spoiled ingredients, improper cooking, or poor sanitary conditions [^5].

Entering the 2010s, major food safety storms erupted in Taiwan—such as those involving plasticizers, toxic starch, and gutter oil—which also affected school lunches, triggering a serious crisis of trust among parents regarding ingredient sources and safety [^食力-校園午餐沒有想像中安全][^LINE-饅頭到乳香雞][^食力-食安年年爆危機][^華人心理健康網-頂新餿水油][^主婦聯盟-校園午餐用油堪慮][^台北畫刊-捍衛食安]. These events prompted the government to strengthen supervision of catering providers and the food supply chain.

In recent years, food safety cases in school lunches have continued to occur frequently, including issues such as metal foreign objects in meals, expired ingredients, excessive pesticide residues, and even live insects crawling in food [^客新聞-宜蘭食安][^商周-供應商過期食材][^CTWANT-活蟲爆黑心][^Yahoo-肉品有蟲][^LINE-食材過期]. Some catering providers have also been involved in scandals involving kickbacks, non-transparent procurement, or fraudulent subsidy claims, seriously affecting the quality of nutritious lunches and student health [^商周-供應商過期食材][^LINE-食材過期].

#### Control Yuan Oversight and Mechanisms

Regarding nutritious lunches, the Control Yuan conducted an investigation into elementary and junior high school lunches in 2018, urging the Ministry of Education to study special legislation and establish a meal cost adjustment mechanism to ensure ingredient quality. In early 2024, Control Yuan members Tien Chiu-chin and Tsai Chung-yi raised the issue again through an automatic investigation, citing that issues such as a lack of nutritionist supervision and students not being full still persist [^監察院-學校午餐調查]. In recent years, the government's improvements have largely been on the institutional side: the school nutritionist system, ingredient inspection and sampling, and encouraging schools to adopt "3 Chapters & 1Q" domestic, traceable ingredients.

### School Nutritionist System

School nutritionists play a critical role in the planning and execution of nutritious lunches. They are responsible for menu design, nutritional analysis, ingredient inspection, kitchen hygiene management, and the promotion of food education, ensuring that students receive balanced and safe meals [^31]. According to the 《School Health Act》, schools should appoint nutritionists, but actual staffing levels still need improvement; particularly in remote areas, a shortage of nutritionist manpower remains a major challenge [^32].

📝 Curator's Note: Nutritionists are the unsung heroes ensuring the health of students' dining tables. Their expertise is reflected not only in menu design but also in transforming nutritional knowledge into delicious meals that students accept, while promoting food education concepts.

### Free Nutritious Lunch Policy: Reducing Burdens and Promoting Health

In recent years, various counties and cities in Taiwan have successively promoted "free nutritious lunch" policies, aiming to reduce the economic burden on parents and ensure all students can receive balanced nutrition. The promotion of this policy reflects society's emphasis on student health and educational equality.

#### Policy Background and Objectives

The promotion of the free nutritious lunch policy is mainly based on the following considerations:

1.  **Reducing Family Economic Burden**: Especially for economically disadvantaged families, free lunches can effectively reduce the pressure of meal costs for their children, ensuring students receive sufficient food while at school.
2.  **Promoting Student Health and Learning**: Research shows that nutritionally balanced lunches help with students' physical development and academic performance. Free lunch policies help improve students' nutritional status, reducing declines in learning efficiency caused by hunger or malnutrition.
3.  **Educational Equity**: Ensuring all students, regardless of family background, enjoy equal rights to education and opportunities for healthy growth.

### Food Waste Issues and Local Ingredients: Challenges for Sustainable Development

With the popularization of nutritious lunches, the issue of food waste has also received increasing attention. According to statistics, schools across Taiwan produce approximately 500 tons of kitchen waste daily, with the cumulative value of leftover food reaching NT$2.4 billion per year [^22] [^23]. This not only causes resource waste but also places a burden on the environment. An investigation by the Children's Food and Welfare Association pointed out that about 15% of each tray consists of "edible but wasted" food, rather than just bones or peels, with staple foods and vegetables being the most frequently left over [^剩食-新聞市場][^剩食-LINE TODAY][^剩食-兒福聯盟].

Beyond students' individual eating habits, several institutional factors contribute to the problem of food waste [^剩食-新聞市場][^剩食-udn]:

1.  **Fixed Meal Standards**: Current nutritious lunch standards are often fixed, making it difficult to flexibly adjust portions to match different students' appetites, leading to oversupply.
2.  **Menu Variety and Taste**: To prioritize efficiency and cost, catering providers offer limited menu variety, or the cooking methods result in poor texture, affecting students' appetites.
3.  **Short Meal Times**: Some schools have very short meal periods, preventing students from eating at a relaxed pace, which leads to leftovers.
4.  **Lack of Food Education**: Students lack an understanding of food sources and production processes, failing to cultivate the concept of valuing food.
5.  **Contract and Preparation Mechanisms**: Catering contracts often require fixed portions, and preparation tends to lean toward over-preparing, leading to a situation where "what might not be finished becomes inevitably unfinished" [^剩食-udn].

To solve this problem, the government is actively promoting the policy of "schools using domestic, traceable ingredients," known as the "3 Chapters & 1Q" label, encouraging schools to prioritize purchasing local, fresh ingredients with production histories [^24]. To date, the average coverage rate of labeled ingredients in school lunches nationwide has increased to 91.8% [^24]. This policy aims to improve food safety, support local agriculture, and reduce food miles, while also hoping that through food education, students will value food more and reduce waste.

It is worth noting that after the ban on feeding kitchen waste to pigs begins in 2026, the cost for catering providers to handle waste will rise significantly. This may further impact meal quality and the willingness to supply meals, becoming a new challenge for the sustainable development of nutritious lunches [^剩食-新聞市場].

#### Remote Area School Central Kitchen Project

To address the difficulties faced by small schools in remote areas due to low student numbers and insufficient kitchen staff, the Ministry of Education has promoted the "Remote Area School Central Kitchen Project" [^33]. Through this project, the government subsidizes schools to build or improve central kitchens. Meals are cooked centrally and then transported to nearby small schools. This not only ensures that children in remote areas can enjoy hot, nutritionally balanced lunches but also improves meal delivery efficiency and quality while effectively utilizing human resources [^34].

📝 Curator's Note: The remote area central kitchen project is not just a solution for meal delivery efficiency; it is a concrete action to practice educational equality, ensuring every child can learn and grow with full stomachs, regardless of their location.

## Conclusion

From the powdered milk and lunch soups of the US Aid era to modern central kitchens, free policies, and the promotion of local ingredients, Taiwan's nutritious lunch system has undergone a long and rich evolution. It is more than just a meal to fill students' stomachs; it is a microcosm of Taiwan's social development, changes in educational philosophy, and the inheritance of culinary culture. Whether it is the nostalgic steamed bento or the "love-hate" relationship with three-color peas and fluorescent curry, these memories at the dining table will continue to accompany generations of Taiwanese students as they grow. From the initial goal of "poverty relief" to today's "food education," the story of nutritious lunches serves as a perfect footnote to the continuous progress of Taiwan's society and its care for the next generation.
