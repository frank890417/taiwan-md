> **30-Second Overview:** Sweet potato balls are a fried snack found in Taiwan's night markets. The general method involves steaming peeled sweet potatoes, mixing them with _shushu_ (tapioca) starch while hot, and then continuously pressing them after they are dropped into the oil vat to let the oil out and air in, frying them until they are crispy on the outside and hollow inside[^1]. Some stalls use spatulas to press them repeatedly[^2], while others use a slotted spoon to crush them one by one into dense balls[^3]. In northern Taiwan, they are mostly called sweet potato balls (_di-gua qiu_), whereas in Kaohsiung and Pingtung, they are often called _QQ_ eggs[^2]. In 2023, a large bag at a stall in Neihu, Taipei, cost NT$50[^4], while a stall at the entrance of Shezi Night Market was still selling them for NT$2 per piece at the end of 2024[^5]. Regarding their origin, the most widespread story is this: under the Zhongzheng Bridge in New Taipei City, a retired elder's sweet potato field was destroyed by an excavator; he fried the potatoes, which "later underwent improvements" to become the sweet potato balls we know today. The story ends there, but today, whether each ball is hollow or dense, vendors must press them out vat by vat and prepare them batch by batch at their prep tables.

Around 2019, Liu Jai-hong (劉在泓) had only learned to fry sweet potato balls for one day before he started running his own stall[^6]. He originally thought it was a simple business: buy the raw materials, drop them in the oil, fry them, and sell. At that time, when frying sweet potato balls, he would remove them from the oil after only seven or eight presses.

Reflecting on those early days of opening, he told a reporter: "At that time, I really wanted to increase my income quickly, so I oversimplified the process. In the beginning, about one-third of the sweet potato balls I fried were ruined—they wouldn't puff up."[^6]

Holding a bag of sweet potato balls in your hand, the balls are round, the shells are crispy, and when you bite into them, they are hollow inside; it looks as if they would naturally grow this way once dropped into the oil. That one-third that failed to puff up was the price he paid for entering the fray after only one day of learning. The most widespread answer to how sweet potato balls first came to be is a story.

## The Story of That Sweet Potato Field Under Zhongzheng Bridge

An article by the food website _SuperTaste_ (食尚玩家) asking "Who invented sweet potato balls?" first states that "there are different versions regarding the origin of sweet potato balls," and then uses "it is said" to recount the most widespread one: a retired elder was growing sweet potatoes under the Zhongzheng Bridge in New Taipei City[^7].

When the government was renovating the riverbank, an excavator operator accidentally destroyed the elder's sweet potato field. Not wanting to let the crop go to waste, the elder threw the sweet potatoes into an oil vat to fry them. The fried sweet potatoes were fragrant and sweet, so he simply set up a stall at a night market to sell fried sweet potatoes[^7].

The story ends there, with the vendor still selling fried sweet potatoes. The final sentence brings it to the present: "Later, through improvements, they became the sweet potato balls we know today—the snack that is hollow in the middle, fluffy in texture, and has a _QQ_ (chewy) bite."[^7]

An elder, a bridge, an accidental excavator, and a refusal to let food go to waste—it is a perfect story to tell friends while waiting in line at a night market; even the stall the elder set up in the story is located in a night market. This version of sweet potato growing under a bridge has also been included in the English Wikipedia entry for sweet potato balls, alongside another version suggesting they were inspired by fried sesame balls[^8].

The _SuperTaste_ article that tells this story begins with a sentence stating that shops across Taiwan have each developed their own unique "golden ratio" recipes[^1]. From fried sweet potatoes to small hollow balls, this step is still being recreated vat by vat in front of night market oil vats today.

## Seven or Eight Presses, or Twenty or Thirty?

When Liu Jai-hong first started selling sweet potato balls, his heart bled as he looked at a pile of balls that wouldn't puff up. Meanwhile, the older brother and friends who had gone to learn with him were all making two or three times his revenue[^9]. Initially, he blamed the location: "My brother has a stall in Tamsui and my friend is at Ningxia Night Market; I originally thought their locations were better than mine, not that my skills were inferior."

![A sweet potato ball stall at Gongguan Night Market, with the vendor standing before an oil vat. A large stainless steel slotted spoon rests on the vat, and a line of customers queues along the street](/article-images/food/digua-qiu-gongguan-press-2019.webp)
_A sweet potato ball stall at Gongguan Night Market, the vendor stands before an oil vat with a large stainless steel slotted spoon resting on it, and a row of customers is queuing by the roadside, October 2019. Photo: Maypollyanna, CC BY-SA 4.0._

It wasn't until one day, when a friend needed extra hands at his Ningxia Night Market stall and asked him to cover a shift, that he found the answer[^9]. He later recounted that moment: "My friend saw it and immediately corrected me, saying I was pressing too little. If you press too few times, the sweet potato balls will be too soft and chewy; you have to continuously press them 20 or 30 times to increase the temperature and color before removing them from the oil."

His first reaction was skepticism: "I was suspicious at the time—aren't sweet potato balls supposed to be soft and _QQ_? But thinking back, I realized something was wrong because my sales were mediocre while my friend's business was excellent." He began frying using his friend's method and found that the repeatedly pressed sweet potato balls tasted crispy and fragrant, and the customer response was good[^9].

The logic behind continuous pressing is stated plainly in a caption from a _SuperTaste_ image: "When frying sweet potato balls, you need to continuously press them to let the oil out and encourage air to enter, so they become crispy on the outside and hollow in the middle."[^1] The method reported by SET News in 2018 follows the same rhythm: drop a whole tray of sweet potato balls into the oil vat to heat evenly for five minutes, then use a spatula to repeatedly press them until they expand, only scooping them out to drain once they have set[^2].

His eldest uncle, who helps run the stall, spoke of those days of pressing twenty or thirty times per batch: "Pressing like this for long periods makes muscle inflammation and tennis elbow common occurrences. There's no choice; to earn a living, you just have to endure it."[^9]

The _SuperTaste_ article also mentions that the frying temperature affects the degree of expansion and the surface crispiness of the sweet potato balls[^1]. Later, in addition to watching the changes in the sweet potato balls in the oil with his eyes, Liu Jai-hong also used a thermometer to measure the oil temperature, building his own standardized frying procedure[^10]. The temperature at which he removes them from the oil, as recorded by _Mirror Media_, is 170 degrees Celsius[^4].

In an alley on Roosevelt Road, Section 4, in Gongguan, Taipei, there is a vegetarian sweet potato ball stall where the balls are twice the usual size, averaging NT$2.5 each. An introduction compiled by _SuperTaste_ during the pandemic period noted that this stall does not use machines for the dough; instead, it relies on manual cutting to ensure even sizes, and after being dropped into the oil, they must be constantly stirred and pressed forcefully. A specially made pot allows an entire batch of sweet potato balls to be pressed at once, yet the fryer still meticulously selects them to press[^11].

## The Dense Style: NT$2 per Piece at Shezi Night Market

At "Zeng Haochi Sweet Potato Balls" (曾好吃地瓜球) at the entrance of Taipei's Shezi Night Market, the owner rolls the dough into small pieces, fries them until golden, and then uses a slotted spoon to crush them one by one. After crushing, he quickly scoops them up, unlike the common method of using large pots for convenient and rapid pressing. Crushing them one by one allows excess oil to drain away and presses out the elasticity of the dough[^3].

In December 2024, _Mirror Media_ reporter Liang Xiao-jie (梁孝傑) went to scout locations for a video on Shezi's nighttime delicacies; in his downtime, he ate at this stall and rated it as one of the best he had ever had. In his words, the crushed balls have no "airy" feeling; the skin is firm and crispy, while the inside is soft, _QQ_, and chewy, providing a dense bite with a distinct sweet potato aroma. Even as he was still chewing, he already had the next one on a skewer[^3].

At this stall, one piece costs NT$2, or ten pieces for NT$20. The owner sets up at 2:30 PM and closes around 6:00 PM; although located at the night market entrance, he primarily does afternoon business. The stall is open-air, and the owner's wife said there is a high chance of resting on rainy days, so if you want to eat them, check the weather first[^5].

![Twelve golden sweet potato balls spread on a white paper bag, sprinkled with fine black pepper, with a bamboo skewer beside them](/article-images/food/digua-qiu-muzha-2017.webp)
_Twelve sweet potato balls bought in Wuzhou, Taipei, spread on a paper bag, sprinkled with black pepper, with a bamboo skewer beside them, November 2017. Photo: Ianbu, CC BY-SA 4.0._

When _UDN News_ compiled netizens' tweets in 2021, they cited the experience of a gourmet eater: those called "sweet potato balls" have a heavy airy feeling and a crispy shell, while those called "_QQ_ eggs" are a modified version with a dense, chewy texture that is somewhat like mochi[^12]. This stall in Shezi, located in Taipei, is named Zeng Haochi Sweet Potato Balls, and the ones you bite into are of the dense variety.

In Kaohsiung, Grandpa Zhu's _QQ_ eggs cost NT$1 each; the owner's hands almost never stop as the _QQ_ eggs roll and are pressed continuously in the oil vat. The version eaten by _SuperTaste_ bloggers is the one with a crispy outer layer and an airy interior[^11].

At Ali Sweet Potato Balls in Fengshan, Kaohsiung, even the shape has changed. The stall is under a corrugated iron shed; the female owner steams fresh sweet potatoes every day and mashes them by hand to make long, strip-shaped _QQ_ eggs that are thicker than a finger, which bloggers found to be quite dense. At the stall, one can see the owner using tools to press the _QQ_ eggs to let the air out, then using a fan to cool them down after removing them from the oil to maintain surface crispiness. There is a 5-minute wait for fresh ones, and one batch makes about eight bags, which sell out quickly[^13].

At Fan Shu Pan Sweet Potato Balls in the Zhudong Night Market in Hsinchu, bloggers visiting on weekend evenings found the stall crowded with people; the owner fries and packs them incessantly. The small sweet potato balls roll and are kneaded in the oil vat, growing larger and larger until they are twice the size of a NT$50 coin; they come in two varieties: purple sweet potato and golden sweet potato. Bloggers heard the owner say that the balls must be kneaded just right so they will expand into large pieces; without proper expansion, the filling becomes compressed, significantly worsening the texture[^14].

The story of Zhongzheng Bridge describes "hollow in the middle" as the improved version. The slotted spoon at Shezi Night Market crushes the balls into a dense form; the Zhudong stall decides how much they will expand during the kneading process; and the Fengshan stall has even changed the shape. How many times to press, how to press, and how large to roll the balls—each stall makes its own decision, and being hollow is just one of those choices. Some choices are made before the dough ever hits the oil.

## The Sweet Potato Washing Vat in the Garage

Every morning at 10:00 AM, Liu Jai-hong begins preparing ingredients in the garage in front of his father-in-law's house for the sweet potato balls he will sell in the evening. He has converted a car-washing faucet into a sweet potato washing vat; the steamer, refrigerator, mixer, and prep table are all squeezed into a space of about 15 to 20 square meters[^15].

The steam from the sweet potatoes fills the garage and dampens his clothes; he takes a quick gulp of tea and begins processing the bags of sweet potatoes he carried home from the market earlier. He steams the sweet potatoes with the skin on and peels them after steaming, as steaming with the skin helps reduce oxidation once exposed to air. Every two days, he must ride his scooter early in the morning to various markets across the Taipei-New Taipei area to select sweet potatoes. Normally, his wife is responsible for washing and steaming; however, on the day _Mirror Media_ visited, his wife was home caring for a sick child, leaving him to work busily by himself[^15].

Sweet potatoes steamed in garages have been grown in Taiwan for over 400 years. According to data from the Ministry of Agriculture, sweet potatoes were introduced from Fujian during the late Ming Dynasty and the Dutch occupation of Taiwan in the early 17th century[^16]. In the early 20th century, when many could not afford white rice, sweet potatoes were shredded and cooked with rice, sometimes in greater quantities than the rice itself[^17]. Between 1946 and 1973, rural areas primarily used sweet potatoes as major pig feed; in 1964, the cultivation area reached 246,000 hectares across Taiwan. Later, feed gradually shifted to imported corn, and by 1990, only 12,000 hectares remained; in the last decade, it has been maintained at around 9,000 hectares[^16]. Following the heavy rains and Typhoon Danas in 2025, the wholesale average price of sweet potatoes rose by over 70% compared to the same period last year, and sweet potato balls were also listed among the affected items[^18].

The steamed sweet potatoes are mixed with flour and mashed into a paste, which serves as the base for the sweet potato balls; this is brought to the stall and then cut into individual pieces using a manual portioning machine[^15]. The starch used in the mixture is typically tapioca starch, though some recipes add glutinous rice flour or sweet potato starch. Every shop has its own unique formula regarding the ratio of sweet potato to starch and the time spent in the oil; if the ratio changes, the resulting texture changes accordingly[^1]. Tapioca is also known as cassava, and the starch made from it is called tapioca starch or white starch (_taibai fen_). In 1974, Taiwan grew 26,782 hectares of cassava, but by 2015, only 35 hectares remained. A 2016 article noted that Taiwan imports approximately 330,000 metric tons of tapioca flour or starch annually, primarily from Thailand, followed by Vietnam and Indonesia[^19].

When Liu Jai-hong first mastered the crispy texture, one thing still puzzled him: "Why do the sweet potato balls fried by my supplier always have a 'crunchy' sound?" When he asked for advice, the supplier would only give vague answers. Since then, every time he goes to pick up ingredients, he steals glances at his supplier's stall to observe the details of their process. He noticed that while most vendors sieve their prepared dough very thoroughly, his supplier only sieves it slightly before dropping it into the oil. After repeated trials, he caught the secret to that "crunchy" sound, and the texture at his own stall has remained consistent ever since[^15].

In July 2022, he began developing his own sweet potato ball ingredients, eventually finding a recipe that eliminated the "lumpy" sensation in the balls. Making the ingredients himself increased his working hours; he starts purchasing at 8:00 AM and works until the night market closes around 1:00 AM[^15]. He put it bluntly: "The quality of the wholesale ingredients was unstable. I have to support a family of five and repay debts, so I couldn't afford the high cost caused by a poor ingredient failure rate; that's why I developed my own ingredients."

Speaking about his homemade ingredients, his fatigue vanishes, replaced by a hint of pride: "For my stall alone, the amount of ingredients used has gone from twenty-odd _jin_ (approx. 12kg) at the start to 45 _jin_ now." By the time _Mirror Media_'s report was published in November 2023, one or two other sweet potato ball vendors had also become his customers[^15], and he is preparing to open branch stores[^20].

## Tainan Calls Them Sweet Potato Balls, Kaohsiung Calls Them _QQ_ Eggs

The eaters are also judging the frying technique: In August 2024, _UDN News_ reported on a Threads post where a netizen asked friends what kind of powder they sprinkle on their sweet potato balls. Most replied "None!", which shocked him into asking the public. The "original flavor" camp commented, "Truly well-fried sweet potato balls don't need powder!" and "Sweet potato balls are naturally sweet and delicious." The "plum powder" camp replied, "Plum powder is number one," and "I am the sole supporter of plum powder; I'm joining the plum powder army." Others prefer pepper, condensed milk, or chocolate[^21].

In May 2018, SET News asked passersby on the street what these balls were called. A resident in Kaohsiung named them based on texture: "_QQ_ eggs, because they should feel _QQ_ when you eat them." Residents in Taoyuan and Taipei answered "sweet potato balls," while those in Pingtung also said "_QQ_ eggs." When the reporter pressed a Taoyuan resident if they had heard any other names, they simply replied, "No"[^2].

In the same report, a sweet potato ball vendor named Mr. Weng added that in central Taiwan: "In parts of Yunlin, Nantou, and Chiayi, they are called _jiu-jiu qiu_ or _QQ_ eggs; the names differ, likely due to regionality."[^]

In 2021, _UDN News_ compiled a series of netizens' tweets where people from different regions reported their local names: some said "Central Taiwan sweet potato balls," others said "Hualien _QQ_ eggs." From these tweets, it is roughly apparent that the north mostly calls them sweet potato balls, while the south, especially Kaohsiung, is accustomed to calling them _QQ_ eggs. Some found it novel: "Tainan calls them sweet potato balls and Kaohsiung calls them _QQ_ eggs, even though they are right next to each other." A netizen from Yunlin said, "Only people from Douliu understand _jiu-jiu qiu_," while a student in Taichung encountered "_QQ_ sweet potato balls" when walking through Yizhong Street. That report counted hundreds of tweets: "sweet potato balls" appeared 131 times, and "_QQ_ eggs" appeared 53 times[^12].

The monthly magazine _Taiwan Business TOPICS_, published by the American Chamber of Commerce in Taipei, summarized the origin of _QQ_ in 2020: _Q_ is the sound of a specific word in Taiwanese (Hokkien) that has no familiar Chinese character to write it out, so the English letter Q appeared on restaurant menus and snack stall signs. A culinary historian cited in the article added that this word originally described something winding or curvy; when grilled squid is rolled up, it is called _QQ_[^22]. English Wikipedia writes this term as _khiū_, and doubling it to _QQ_ is a more intense way of expressing it[^23].

On Ali Sweet Potato Balls' counter in Fengshan, there are six types of seasoning powders, with plum powder remaining the most popular[^13]. At Liu Jai-hong's stall, besides the original flavor, customers can also choose plum powder or chocolate powder to change the taste[^4].

## Entering the Trade After Only One Day of Learning

The reason this stall offers plum and chocolate powder started when Liu's brother mentioned a sweet potato ball shop that was delicious and wanted to learn from them; Liu was also interested. However, he couldn't afford the NT$88,000 franchise fee, which included the stall itself. His brother negotiated a deal where they could learn how to fry the balls if they simply purchased the ingredients from them, thereby avoiding the franchise fee[^20].

He borrowed NT$50,000 from relatives to buy a food cart and started working after learning to fry sweet potato balls for only one day[^20]. A _Mirror Media_ supplement noted that he initially viewed frying sweet potato balls merely as a job; he felt he was just a vendor and didn't think about the details, so his business was mediocre. Once he realized this, he began to seriously study every step. He said: "Being a boss is very different from being an employee; I have to be invested and constantly find ways to break through to achieve what are called 'results'."[^10]

He originally had a stall in the Chang Gung commercial area in Linkou, New Taipei City, but later moved to the 737 Night Market in Neihu, Taipei, to expand his business[^10]. His stall is named "Taipei's 6th Best Sweet Potato Balls." According to _Mirror Media_, his sweet potato balls remain crispy even when cold, making his stall a popular destination at the 737 Night Market[^20].

![A vegetarian sweet potato ball cart at Gongguan Night Market, with a yellow light box showing three prices (large, medium, small), and a sign next to it warning 'Danger: Oil Vat, Do Not Approach'](/article-images/food/digua-qiu-gongguan-stall-2019.webp)
_A vegetarian sweet potato ball cart at Gongguan Night Market, featuring a yellow light box and a sign that reads "Danger: Oil Vat, Do Not Approach," October 2019. Photo: Giantmudcrab, CC BY-SA 4.0._

In November 2025, _United Daily News_ reported on a judgment from the Kaohsiung District Court regarding a franchise dispute involving sweet potato balls: a franchisee's goods came from the headquarters, which stopped supplying them citing the expiration of the contract; the franchisee sued for compensation for business losses during the shutdown. The judge ruled that the contract had automatically renewed and ordered the headquarters to pay compensation, though an appeal is possible[^24].

## A Pressing Handle Re-welded Twice

_Mirror Media_ reported that the method of pressing twenty or thirty times immediately after dropping them into the oil is quite taxing on the tools. Liu Jai-hong pointed at his oil vat and said: "Look, this pressing handle has been broken before; I've already had it re-welded twice."[^9]

The man who only pressed seven or eight times when he first opened his stall, by 2023, had a pressing handle that had been re-welded twice. The story under the Zhongzheng Bridge gave sweet potato balls a beginning; their subsequent forms are still being pressed out, vat by vat, in front of every vendor's oil vat.

_Mirror Media_ noted at his stall that the Tainong No. 5 sweet potato enters its peak season in November; from then until March or April of the following year, the sweet potato balls are at their most fragrant, chewy, and sweet[^4]. During those few minutes waiting for a bag of sweet potato balls, one can count how many times the owner presses them.
