At five in the morning in the Douliuzi tribal village of Yujing, a thin mist still clung to the sun-facing slopes. A fruit farmer tore an Irwin mango from its paper bag, and his fingertips were instantly coated with sticky juice. The great-great-grandfather of this mango was one of five mango varieties that Taiwan Agricultural Research Institute researcher Lu Chih-lin brought back from Florida, USA in 1954[^1]. The Irwin variety itself was born only in 1939, from a natural cross between Lippens and Haden seedlings in the Miami home of F.D. Irwin[^2].

In 1962, Yujing farmer Cheng Han-chih planted the nation's first batch of Irwin mangoes — one hundred trees — in fields that had previously grown sugarcane and sweet potatoes. That year brought the worst frost damage in Taiwan's history; only four trees survived[^3]. They first bore fruit in 1965. The Yujing Farmers' Association's 1967 annual report recorded Irwin mango revenue of NT$300 million that year, equivalent to roughly NT$2 billion today[^3]. In 1973, the government established Taiwan's first mango specialty zone in Douliuzi Village.

The journey of a single Irwin mango from tree to hand, and what this island treats each fruit as when cultivating it, is a story far more three-dimensional than the four characters "Fruit Kingdom."

## From Florida to Yujing: The Lineage History of an Irwin Mango

Taiwan is called the Fruit Kingdom, and many assume this refers to natural geographic blessings. The Tropic of Cancer cuts across the center; the south is subtropical, the north temperate, the Central Mountain Range alpine temperate, and the Kuroshio Current makes the south warmer than other regions at the same latitude. This sentence about geographic endowment is correct. But that is only the skeleton, not the flesh.

What truly turned this soil into a tree of varieties were generations of named breeders. In 1912, Japanese agricultural scientist **Iso Eikichi** arrived at the Taiwan Governor-General's Agricultural Experiment Station as a technician, and in 1915 was promoted to director of the Taichung Agricultural Experiment Station[^4]. He and **Suenaga Jin** began crossing the Japanese japonica rice varieties "Kameji" and "Jinriki" in 1921, and in 1929 bred **Taichung No. 65**, which could adapt to Taiwan's plains. On April 24, 1926, Governor-General **Izawa Takio** officially named it "Ponlai rice" at the 19th All-Japan Rice Grain Conference held at the Taipei Railway Hotel[^4]. The success of the rice cultivation system incidentally laid the infrastructure for tropical fruit tree research.

In April 1939, the **Fengshan Tropical Horticulture Experiment Branch** was established in Torimatsu Village, Fengshan District, Kaohsiung Prefecture; the land — 50 hectares — was donated by the "Taiwan Consolidated Pineapple Co., Ltd."[^5]. After the war it was taken over in October 1945, and renamed a "Branch" in August 1949. Today it remains the only national institution whose primary research subject is tropical fruit trees[^5]; papaya, mango, wax apple, lychee, passion fruit, carambola, jujube, guava, sugar apple, and red dragon fruit are all bred here.

The mango story is especially interesting: **Irwin** came from Florida; **Atemoya** was bred in 1908 in Florida, USA, by **P.J. Wester** and **Edward Simmonds** through interspecific crossing of sugar apple and cherimoya[^6]. **Tainong No. 17 Gold Diamond Pineapple** was named in April 1998 by the Agricultural Experiment Station's Chiayi Branch; its parents were a cross between "Normal Smooth Cayenne" and "Rough leaf variety"[^7]. Single fruit weighs about 1.4 kg with a sugar content of 14.1°Brix; today it occupies over 80% of Taiwan's pineapple cultivation area.

> [!NOTE]
> The lineage of "native varieties" is in fact a history of global hybridization. The Irwin mother tree came from Florida; the Atemoya crossing pattern was already completed in the US in 1908; the Gold Diamond's parent Rough variety was also introduced from abroad. The "purely native" framework does not hold; "introduction, local acclimatization, reverse export" is the true narrative of Taiwan's variety tree.

Some stories are closer to the grassroots: **Huang Chin-huang**, born 1932, from Jhugang, Chiayi, later moved to Liugui, Kaohsiung. Elementary school graduate, no formal breeding training[^8]. During his military service in 1958, he observed researchers at the nearby Tainan District Agricultural Improvement Station performing crossing pollination on crops, and "just like that, understood the principle." After discharge he returned home and crossed "Keitt" as pollen parent with Indian "White" as seed parent, taking eight years.

Mangoes flower at night, and the flowers are extremely small. Village chief **Wang Ming-yang** remembers Huang Chin-huang in those years: "Back then he would always take a flashlight into the orchard; no one knew what he was doing"[^8]. In 1966, **Jinhuang** was successfully bred. It only gradually spread in the 1980s. It won the Shennong Award twice, in 1984 and 1991. Later, because Jinhuang was too easy to propagate, supply exceeded demand, and the price fell from **NT$100 per catty** to **NT$10**. In his later years Huang Chin-huang suffered from cancer, yet still worried about production and marketing regulation for farmers. He passed away on March 6, 2016, at age 85.

In 1995, Yujing farmer **Kuo Wen-chung** used Jinhuang as seed parent and Irwin as pollen parent to breed "**Yuwen**," taking the "Yu" from the place name Yujing and the "Wen" from his own name[^9]. On the Taitung side, **Taitung No. 1 (Dulan variety)** was named in 2001; **Taitung No. 2** was selected in 2008 from the orchard of **Huang Chen-hsieh** in the city outskirts; **Taitung No. 3 Green Treasure** was bred in 2018, an interspecific cross between sugar apple and Atemoya, both heat- and cold-tolerant[^10]. The Wendan (pomelo) line is even older: introduced from Zhangzhou, Fujian in the 40th year of the Kangxi reign (1701); around 1850 **Kuo Yao** and **Kuo Chiu**, father and son, cultivated superior strains in Madou, laying the foundation for the Madou Wendan brand[^11].

## Flowers Under a Flashlight, and a 64-Year-Old Orchard

The image of Huang Chin-huang holding a flashlight in an orchard in 1958 connects forward to Iso Eikichi under a microscope at the Taichung Experiment Station in 1912, and backward to the orchard of Wu Ching-chin, a 60-something production and marketing class leader in Yujing today. Taiwan's breeding has never been a laboratory-only affair; it is something farmers throw their bodies into, accumulated generation after generation.

The problem is that this generation of farmers is disappearing rapidly: the 2020 Agriculture, Forestry, Fishery and Husbandry Census put the average age of agricultural operators at 64.4, up 0.9 years from 2015[^12]. The share of young farmers under 45 was 39.61% in 1980 but only 5.64% in 2015. Farmers over 65 rose from 15.29% to 45.96% over the same period[^12]. This is not an abstract narrative about "farmers having it hard" — it is the physical age structure of orchards flipping upside down entirely.

Named young-farmer cases are not few. Kuo Tai-cheng of Qishan, Kaohsiung, a second-generation farmer, returned home and launched a co-branded "Banana Rice Guai Guai" with Guai Guai, selling 60,000 packs in 10 days[^13]. Hung Ming-wei of Daliao, Kaohsiung, a second-generation pineapple farmer, put it bluntly: "The government spent hundreds of millions encouraging people from other industries to return to farming, but agriculture majors keep leaving"[^14]. Chen Chao-yuan, president of the Pingtung County Young Farmers Association, said the survival rate for young farmers returning home is "only a little over 30%"[^14].

Liu Yu-chang, a graduate of NTU's Graduate Institute of Horticulture and Landscape Architecture, chose organic farming. He said: "Without family backing, it really comes down to luck and probability"[^14]. An anonymous agriculture graduate called "A-hua" started with NT$30,000; a typhoon and torrential rain wiped out his crops, leaving him with zero income for nine consecutive months, surviving on odd jobs at NT$100 a day before finally leaving farming[^14]. These names rarely make the media; most never enter the lens of "model farmer stars."

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The "model farmer star" narrative has selection bias. Only the 30% who survive get written into reports; the 70% who leave farming are seldom tracked. When young-farmer stories read like nothing but "return home, innovate, succeed," one must remember to ask: what about the ones who left?

Farmland itself is fragmenting. About 76% of agricultural operations are under one hectare, with an average farm size of 1.1 hectares, and plots are scattered[^15]. Article 16 of the Agricultural Development Act stipulates that after subdivision, if each person's holding is under 0.25 hectares, further subdivision is prohibited — this is to prevent excessive fragmentation, but it also makes it harder for young farmers to acquire land. The "Small Landlord, Large Professional Farmer" policy launched in 2009 encouraged elderly farmers to lease land to professional farmers to expand scale; however, a Legislative Yuan review in 2019 found that professional farmers' annual income "not only failed to rise but fell"[^15].

The fields grow quieter, yet more foreign accents are heard. In 2019, Taiwan's first legal agricultural migrant workers arrived — 7 Thai nationals, 2 in dairy farming and 5 in extensive agriculture[^16]. The Council of Agriculture estimated a chronic shortage of about 10,000 workers and a seasonal shortage of about 250,000 worker-shifts. In June 2023, policy was relaxed: fruit-tree operations of 2 hectares or more could apply, and the domestic-to-migrant worker ratio for small farmers with 10 or fewer employees was raised from 35% to 1:1[^16]. The total migrant worker quota doubled from 6,000 to 12,000, mainly from Vietnam and Indonesia.

But a gulf still separates system design from the field: take the figures from October 15, 2022 — 271 extensive-agriculture entities applied for 4,680 migrant agricultural workers, but the Council of Agriculture approved only 162 entities and 1,218 workers, a 60% entity approval rate but only a 26% quota approval rate[^17]. With legal channels blocked, farmers turned to missing migrant workers to fill the gap. The missing-worker rate in agriculture, forestry, fishery, and animal husbandry reached 17.94%, with over 85,000 missing migrant workers nationwide[^17]. The Control Yuan's language was severe: this has "deformed into Taiwan's invisible agricultural labor force, propping up a whole sky of our nation's agriculture"[^17].

## At 3:20 AM, the Binjiang Auctioneer's Chant

After an Irwin mango is picked from the slopes of Yujing, how does it reach a Taipei dining table? This is a question of the production-and-marketing system, and the answer lies in the pre-dawn fruit and vegetable markets and the mountain vapor-heat treatment facilities.

The Taipei Agricultural Products Marketing Corporation (TAPMC, commonly known as "Beinong") was established in 1974. Its First Fruit and Vegetable Wholesale Market is in Wanhua, and the Second Fruit and Vegetable Wholesale Market (Binjiang Market) was established in 1985 next to Neihu[^18]. With 500 employees, it handles 2,000 metric tons of fruit and vegetables daily, supplying the 3.5 million people of the Greater Taipei area. About 70% of the fruit and vegetables for Taipei, New Taipei, and Keelung pass through the First Market; price fluctuations here go straight into consumers' wallets.

At 1:30 AM, freight handlers start work, verifying the unloading sequence. At 2:00 AM, auctioneers inspect quality grades and set floor prices. At 3:20 AM, the auction begins. Buyers bid through gestures or verbal cues. Binjiang auctioneers process 130,000 transactions and 2,300 metric tons of auction volume per day, closing a deal every 7 to 10 seconds[^18]. It ends at 7:00 AM.

> [!TIP]
> Auctioneers face evaluation every three years; those who fail are demoted back to cargo handlers. Without a university degree and three to five years as a cargo handler in a wholesale market, one isn't even eligible to take the exam. "This is a business of conscience," says 31-year-old cabbage auctioneer Lin Wei-chih. "In a year, there might be only 100 days of good market conditions; the remaining 200 days are a struggle." He has seen prices "at NT$15 per kilogram in the morning, dropping to NT$2 by the afternoon market"[^19].

Farmers' associations at various levels take 4 to 4.5%, TAPMC takes 1.5%, and the rest goes to the farmers[^20]. The system's designed commission is low, but allegations at the rumor level — auctioneers' subjective pricing, off-market price negotiations, "sandwich fraud" — have never ceased. The political storm triggered by Wu Yin-ning's appointment as TAPMC general manager in 2018 thrust this pre-dawn auction hall into the national headlines[^20].

Leaving the auction hall, we head to the Yujing Vapor Heat Treatment Plant. This is the technical checkpoint for exports to Japan and South Korea. The principle uses high temperature to kill fruit fly eggs; the export-to-Japan specification is 46.5°C for 30 minutes, then cooling back to room temperature[^21]. Japanese and Korean quarantine officers are stationed here. From each basket they select the largest mango, insert a temperature probe, then place that mango back in the position where temperature rises slowest in the batch, ensuring the core of every fruit in the lot meets the standard.

For the past 20 years, Yujing has used two old-model vapor heat units commissioned in 2005, with a combined capacity of 8 metric tons. The old equipment caused heat damage to 25% of the fruit. Only in 2024–2025 did the Council of Agriculture (now Ministry of Agriculture) contribute 85% and the Tainan City Government 15%, investing NT$54.051 million to purchase two Japanese-made container-type vapor heat units, with a combined capacity of 10 metric tons[^21]. The new equipment achieves a yield rate near 100%, cuts processing time from 4.5 hours to 3 hours, and extends shelf life from 8 days to 14 days. Infrastructure investment lagged export demand by 20 years; this is another, less photogenic side of the Fruit Kingdom.

On the export front, Taiwan's agricultural exports reached US$4.89 billion in 2024, up 4.7% year-on-year[^22]. Japan is Taiwan's largest fresh fruit market; in 2023 exports to Japan totaled NT$22.2 billion, accounting for 47.4% of fresh fruit export value. Eleven fresh fruit varieties are approved for export to Japan: mango, grape, papaya, red dragon fruit, ponkan, white pomelo, wendan (pomelo), lychee, jujube, banana, and pineapple[^22].

But order diversion has had both successes and failures: when China banned pineapples in March 2021, Japan absorbed over 90%, with annual exports of 19,400 metric tons[^23]. In September 2021 China further banned sugar apples and wax apples; the Council of Agriculture set a target of diverting 5,000 metric tons of pineapples and sugar apples. Southeast Asia worked: Malaysian and Singaporean Taiwanese businessmen snapped up the fruit. Canada failed: air freight rose from NT$100 to NT$300 per kilogram, sea freight full container at US$20,000; operators calculated per-box cost at NT$3,500, but Brazilian-imported sugar apples in Canada sold at only 39.5 Canadian dollars for 4 kg (approx. NT$893)[^24]. Operators described that Canadian export situation as "distant water cannot put out a nearby fire."

## Night Market Glass Freezers, Mid-Autumn Pomelo Peel Hats, and Offering Tables

The same Irwin mango, if it doesn't go through the auction market or the vapor heat treatment facility, where does it end up? The answer is usually the family dining table. The relationship between Taiwanese people and fruit is largely built in family settings.

For the grandparents' generation, fruit was mostly picked from their own yards or bought by weight at the market; peeling and plating was a ritual of family togetherness. For the middle generation, there were night market fruit platters and post-meal fruit. The younger generation can buy imported substitutes at supermarkets year-round, but has instead lost the seasonal sense of "May lychee, June mango, July longan, August pomelo."

Night market fruit stalls are the joint of this generational line: in glass freezers, tomatoes dipped in soy sauce paste with minced ginger, guava dipped in plum powder, pineapple on bamboo skewers; a NT$100 combo platter of two or three kinds was the ending to countless 18-year-old nights.

On June 2, 2024, after a lecture at NTU, NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang went to a 43-year-old fruit stall at Tonghua Night Market (Linjiang Street Night Market), listed on Google as "Grandma's Premium Fresh-Cut Fruit." He bought 10+ bags at a time, favoring passion fruit and cherry tomatoes, once spending NT$2,300[^25]. This news brought up the night market youth of several generations at once.

The Mid-Autumn Festival table has long been tied to Madou pomelo. Introduced from Zhangzhou, Fujian in 1701 (the 40th year of Kangxi), during the Japanese colonial period its unique fragrance and sweetness made it an "imperial fruit" presented to the Japanese Emperor[^26]. "You" (pomelo) sounds like "you" (bless/protect), and it peaks in season right around Mid-Autumn. Children wearing pomelo peels as hats after peeling the fruit is a cross-generational childhood memory for many Taiwanese.

On the offering table for worship, there are rules for fruit types: "three sacrificial animals and four fruits" is the rhythm between family and deities; the three animals are chosen from five sacrificial animals, while "four fruits" refers to "seasonal fresh fruit," yet odd numbers (1, 3, 5 types) are often used for worship[^27]. For the Earth God, jujubes (early ripening); for deities, oranges (auspicious) and apples (peace); when visiting the sick, avoid bananas (attract), pears (separate), and plums (separate).

Taboos for the Ghost Festival (_Pudu_) in the seventh lunar month are even stricter. In folk custom, pineapple (sounds like "prosperity comes") is feared to attract more "good brothers" (wandering spirits); whole clusters of fruit like lychees and grapes imply good brothers gathering in groups; bananas plus plums plus pears together sound like "attracting you" in Taiwanese, feared to invite evil spirits[^28]. But the Agriculture and Food Agency has officially debunked this: in Taiwanese, banana is _kim-chio_ (golden banana), originally meaning attracting wealth; _Taiwan Folklore_ records the tradition of distributing bananas to friends and relatives during _Pudu_ feasts; "cannot worship with bananas in the seventh lunar month" is a later error passed down[^28]. Academician Li Feng-mo of Academia Sinica also emphasized that homophony is one factor forming ritual taboos, but taboos often vary by time and place; "compared to offerings, sincerity during worship should be valued more"[^28].

> [!NOTE]
> Folk taboos and official debunking are two voices on the same offering table. Which is right? Both are. Taboos are the rules of family elders; official debunking is the reckoning of documents. This island accommodates both layers coexisting, no need to force a single conclusion.

## 1967 Bananas, 2021 Pineapples, 2025 Chilean Cherries

The journey from the dining table to international shipping containers is long, and it has never been smooth. Taiwan's fruit is one of the few products from this island with a distinct "brand" on the international stage, and for that reason it was the first to be used as a bargaining chip.

Banana exports to Japan were already a well-established route during the Japanese colonial period. In the post-war 1960s, Taiwan entered the "Banana Kingdom" era. In 1963, Japan liberalized imports. At its peak in 1967, Taiwan exported 27 million boxes, earning US$62 million in foreign exchange — one-third of Taiwan's total foreign exchange that year[^29]. Taiwan held an 80% market share in Japan. In 1969, the "Banana Peeling Case" erupted; Wu Zhen-rui, chairman of the Green Fruit Association, was imprisoned, marking a turning point for the Banana Kingdom.

In 2010, the two sides signed ECFA; the early harvest list included zero tariffs on 18 agricultural and fishery products from China, including Phalaenopsis orchids, enoki mushrooms, bananas, oranges, lemons, melons, dragon fruit, tea, and wax apples[^30]. Pineapples were not on the list of 18. This omission became critical in 2021.

On February 26, 2021, China's General Administration of Customs suddenly announced a suspension of pineapple imports from Taiwan starting March 1, citing quarantine concerns. Before the ban, China accounted for 97% of Taiwan's pineapple exports[^31]. On September 20, sugar apples and wax apples were also banned, again citing scale insects. Annual production was 57,000 metric tons for sugar apples and 50,000 metric tons for wax apples; in 2020, exports to China accounted for 23% and 9.6% of total production respectively[^31]. In November 2021, Taiwan's WTO Permanent Representative Lo Chang-fa raised a "Specific Trade Concern," emphasizing that China's ban lacked scientific basis and exceeded the necessary level prescribed by WTO agreements[^31].

Historians interpret this differently: Tamkang University Cross-Strait Research Center Director Chang Wu-yueh views the early harvest list as a political commitment that China would continue to revoke as cross-strait relations deteriorated; the Council of Agriculture's official position emphasizes that the 18 items were selected through industry consultation, reflecting substantive industrial needs corresponding to farmers' concerns and export potential[^30]. The same list: one side sees a political tool, the other sees economic demand.

The import side has also been shifting recently: in 2025, imported fruit value reached NT$30.2 billion, breaking NT$30 billion for the first time, a 44.5% increase over five years[^32]. Top three: apples NT$9.02 billion, grapes NT$4.45 billion, cherries NT$4.32 billion (up 23.8% year-on-year, a new high). Taiwan's climate cannot support large-scale temperate apple cultivation; reliance on apple imports is a structural fact. Shine Muscat grapes spread from Japan to cultivation in South Korea and China, entering Taiwan's supermarkets through various channels; Japan loses over 10 billion yen annually because it failed to apply for variety rights protection[^33].

Chilean cherries are another easily misread example. Media often write "abandoning China for Taiwan," but in the 2025/26 season China still accounted for 87% of Chilean cherry exports (down from 92% the previous year); Taiwan's 45.1% year-on-year growth is merely marginal; in January 2026, Taiwan imported US$27 million of Chilean cherries, ranking third globally, far below China's US$1.075 billion in the same period[^34]. Order diversion exists, but the scale is far smaller than the narrative suggests.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The NT$30.2 billion in imports and the 20% decline in domestic production are not a simple story of "imports crowding out domestic produce." Taiwan Importers Association Chairman Tseng Wen-tso points out that importers mostly adopt strategies avoiding overlap with Taiwan's agricultural products[^32]. At the same time, young fruit farmers are shifting toward high-unit-price premium markets like pineapples, sugar apples, and Crisp Pears, so the decline in domestic output does not equal industrial decline. Both trends are real.

## Lychees on the Front Line of the Extreme Climate Tsunami

Exports and imports are only the surface tremors of the fruit kingdom. The real change lies underground, in the climate. And among all fruits, lychees have taken the hardest beating from climate change.

Associate Researcher Fang Hsin-hsiu of the Fengshan Tropical Horticulture Experiment Branch gave lychees a name: "the front line of the extreme climate tsunami"[^35]. The reason: lychee flower bud differentiation requires sufficiently low winter temperatures — below 20 °C and dry from November through January — for normal flowering. Taiwan's winters are getting warmer, and this hurdle is becoming harder to clear.

The numbers are brutal. Early on, lychees could produce 100,000 metric tons a year. In 2019, the warmest winter in 70 years hit, with average winter temperatures reaching 23.1 °C in Kaohsiung and 20.2 °C in Taichung. Total losses across Taiwan exceeded NT$2 billion, and annual output plummeted to a historic low of 25,000 metric tons[^35]. The 2024 estimate of 50,000-plus metric tons still only reaches 60% of the 2001–2010 average. Cultivated area has shrunk from about 12,000 hectares in 2000 to 9,000 hectares today. The average wholesale price broke NT$100 for the first time in 2019, and hit a record high of NT$127 in 2024[^35].

Ou Hsien-lung, head of the Dashe District lychee production and marketing group in Kaohsiung, remembers the old days: "Back then, even though wholesale prices weren't high, yields were stable, and with high volume and thin margins, overall returns were still decent; in recent years, even when prices are good, it's hard to break even." "Growing lychees now isn't as easy as it used to be"[^35].

Besides climate, there are pests: the lychee stink bug (_Tessaratoma papillosa_) was first discovered in Kinmen in 1999, invaded Taiwan proper in 2008, and agricultural authorities didn't issue an epidemic warning until 2012[^36]. It spread from Kaohsiung all the way across northern, central, and southern Taiwan. Its damage range includes longan and lychee, as well as street trees like _Koelreuteria henryi_ and _Sapindus mukorossi_ — in summer you'll see red dots covering tree trunks in parks. The lychee stink bug pierces and sucks sap from tender shoots, flower spikes, and young fruit, causing flower and fruit drop, fruit skin blackening, and it also vectors lychee leaf curl disease. Preliminary estimates put agricultural losses at 20–30%[^36].

The oriental fruit fly (_Bactrocera dorsalis_) is another constant visitor: guava, mango, lychee, jujube, wax apple, and dragon fruit are all its prey. Control relies on three things: sex pheromone trapping (methyl eugenol traps collected and monitored every 10 days), egg parasitoid wasps as natural enemies (Taiwan surveys show parasitism rates above 74% on guava), and bagging — best suited for guava, wax apple, and mango[^37]. Walk into a wax apple orchard and you'll see black nets covering the canopy, each fruit meticulously wrapped in a paper bag — the daily war between fruit farmers and fruit flies.

![Pingtung's Black Pearl wax apple orchard, canopy covered with black anti-insect nets, fruit already bagged](/article-images/food/fruit-kingdom-waxapple-orchard.webp)

_Pingtung's Black Pearl wax apple orchard. Bagging and black nets are the daily tools fruit farmers use against the oriental fruit fly, Photo: Su Yu-jen (Taiwan7007), CC BY 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons._

Three typhoons making landfall in succession in 2024 also caught farmers off guard. Kemi landed in Nanao, Yilan on July 25 — the first typhoon to make landfall in Taiwan at severe typhoon strength in eight years — causing agricultural losses exceeding NT$2.4 billion, with Yunlin hardest hit and bananas suffering the greatest losses[^38]. Krathon made a slow landfall in Kaohsiung in October, causing NT$500–600 million in agricultural damage; guava, jujube, wax apple, and papaya were devastated; farmers believe the government underestimated losses because it only calculated fruit about to be harvested, excluding small fruit and flower buds three to four months out[^38]. Kong-rey took another NT$1.317 billion. The same orchards hit by three typhoons in one year could only claim disaster relief once.

Under all this pressure, a group of farmers has turned to organic. Taiwan's certified organic area grew from 7,569 hectares in 2017 to 20,304 hectares in 2024, a 2.68-fold increase; friendly farming area grew from 529 hectares to 6,708 hectares, a 12.68-fold increase[^39]. Together they account for 3.47% of cultivated land. The passage of the Organic Agriculture Promotion Act in 2018 was the key accelerator.

Hsieh Mei-li entered organic farming with zero experience in 1991, relying on self-study through courses and years of trial and error, winning the Shennong Award in 1999[^40]. Her core philosophy: "Organic farming's pests and diseases can't be handled with control, they must be handled with management" — letting the farm ecosystem find its own balance, with pests kept in check by natural enemies. Weng Chin-hsing, originally in garment processing, switched to organic in 2012, mainly growing dragon fruit and guava; for weeding, he raises chickens in his guava orchard[^40]. These stories won't appear in memories of youthful night markets, but they are another kind of quiet experiment deep in the orchards.

## Hands That Peel Fruit

By the time we reach this point, the journey of a single Irwin mango has come full circle. From the four seedlings that survived out of the hundred Cheng Han-chi planted in 1962 in the Douliuzi community of Yujing, it traveled past the breeding station Iso Eikichi built beside the rice paddies, past the nights Huang Chin-huang spent with his flashlight, past the seven-second hammer-fall of the Binjiang auctioneer, past the thirty minutes at 46.5 °C in the Yujing Steam Heat Treatment Plant, past the temperature probes of Japanese quarantine inspectors, past the Mid-Autumn tables piled high with pomelos and the NT$100 platters in night-market glass freezers.

Finally it comes to rest on a dinner table one afternoon, lifted by a pair of hands that never stop peeling fruit.

You know whose hands they are: you might remember your grandmother peeling apples until the juice stained her fingernails, the weight of a pomelo rind worn like a hat after Mid-Autumn peeling, the guava your mother sliced beside your lunchbox, the eighteen-year-old you standing before a night-market freezer case. Or perhaps you remember the ritual of taking a frozen mango cube from a storage container.

This island grows every fruit as both life and memory. The life part is the breeding trellises of the Agricultural Research Institute, the 5 a.m. harvest on Yujing's slopes, the Mandarin accents of Indonesian and Filipino migrant workers in the orchards, the relentless tug-of-war between lychee stink bugs, warm winters, and typhoons. The memory part is those peeling hands, passing down a rhythm across three generations of dinner tables.
