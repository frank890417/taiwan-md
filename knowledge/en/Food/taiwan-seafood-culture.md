# Taiwan's Seafood Culture

The geographical advantage of being surrounded by sea on all sides has fostered a rich seafood culture in Taiwan. From the first-hand freshness of fishing boats returning to port at dawn to the steaming oyster omelets in night markets, the ocean's bounty nourishes the people of the island and shapes a unique maritime culinary tradition.

## Fishing Ports: The Starting Point of Maritime Flavors

Taiwan's seafood culture begins with the fishing ports scattered across the island. From Keelung and Nanfang'ao in the north to Donggang and Fangliao in the south, and from Chenggong and Fuguang in the east, every port has its own characteristic species and fishing methods. The seafood snacks at Keelung Miaokou Night Market are famous throughout Taiwan; mackerel from Nanfang'ao is the pride of the northeast coast, and the bluefin tuna of Donggang is a prized delicacy sought after by gourmets.

Fishing port culture is more than just catching and selling fish; it is a way of life. At the 5:00 AM fish market auctions, fishermen use concise and powerful gestures to bid—this is the most primitive and direct form of transaction in the marine economy. Fresh catches are delivered directly from fishing boats to dining tables; this short-distance "port-to-plate" supply chain ensures the quality and freshness of Taiwan's seafood.

## Oysters: The Soul of Taiwan's Maritime Flavors

Oysters (_o-a_) hold a special place in Taiwan's seafood culture. The oyster farming industry along the western coast of Taiwan has a history spanning hundreds of years, with Budai in Chiayi, Anping in Tainan, and Taixi in Yunlin being important production areas. Traditional oyster rack farming methods are still in use today: during high tide, oysters filter plankton from the seawater, and during low tide, they are exposed to the air to enhance their disease resistance.

The oyster omelet (_o-a-chian_) is one of the most representative snacks of Taiwan's seafood culture. Fresh, plump oysters paired with eggs and sweet potato starch batter are fried until fragrant on a hot iron plate, then drizzled with a special sweet and spicy sauce; every bite offers the fresh sweetness of the ocean. This seemingly simple snack perfectly interprets the Taiwanese approach to seafood: maintaining original flavors, using simple seasoning, and highlighting freshness.

Various oyster dishes, such as crispy fried oysters, oyster vermicelli (_o-a mian-xian_), and oyster soup, demonstrate the deep development of this ingredient by the Taiwanese people. Each cooking method showcases different flavor profiles—from crispy to silky, from refreshing to rich—allowing oysters to take on a wide range of forms in Taiwanese cuisine.

## Deep-Sea Delicacies: Bluefin Tuna Culture

The bluefin tuna of Donggang is a top-tier representative of Taiwan's seafood culture. During the bluefin tuna season from May to July each year, Donggang Fishing Port is always crowded with gourmets from all over. These giant fish, which migrate through the deep waters of the Pacific Ocean, possess exquisite meat and rich fat, making them the ultimate choice for sashimi.

The auction scenes for Donggang bluefin tuna are spectacular. A single bluefin tuna weighing several hundred kilograms can often sell for high prices reaching hundreds of thousands of New Taiwan dollars. This is not just a reflection of economic value, but also represents the standard of Taiwan's fishing technology and seafood culture. From harvesting techniques to preservation and transport, and from auction systems to culinary craftsmanship, every link in the bluefin tuna industry chain embodies professional excellence.

## Seafood Restaurant Culture

Taiwan's seafood restaurant culture has a strong familial atmosphere. From small eateries by the fishing ports to large seafood restaurants in cities, gathering to enjoy seafood is an important social activity for Taiwanese people. Steamed grouper on a rotating round table, steamed scallops with garlic, salt and pepper crab, or lobster with ginger and scallions—every dish embodies a culinary philosophy where "freshness" comes first.

A characteristic of Taiwan's seafood restaurants is the display of live seafood and the "order-and-cook" system. Various fish, shrimp, crabs, and shellfish swim in transparent aquariums, allowing customers to select their own ingredients, which chefs then prepare using the most suitable cooking methods. This interactive dining experience turns tasting seafood into a ritualistic activity.

## Commoner's Delicacies: Baby Squid and Cuttlefish

Compared to high-priced bluefin tuna, baby squid (_xiao-juan_) and cuttlefish (_tou-tsau_) are seafood much closer to the daily lives of ordinary people. The waters north of Taiwan are rich in these cephalopod mollusks; fresh baby squid requires only simple blanching with a bit of ginger and soy sauce to be delicious. Cuttlefish is well-suited for quick stir-frying or being made into cuttlefish thick soup, and its chewy texture is beloved by Taiwanese people.

Crab soup and cuttlefish soup in Keelung night markets, as well as fried cuttlefish balls and grilled cuttlefish from night market stalls, allow ordinary citizens to enjoy the flavors of the ocean at affordable prices. This democratization of seafood is an important feature of Taiwan's seafood culture.

## Innovation in Aquaculture Technology

Taiwan's seafood culture does not rely solely on wild catching; the development of aquaculture technology is also a vital support. From milkfish farming in the south to the eel industry in central Taiwan, and from freshwater catfish to coastal grouper, Taiwan's aquaculture technology holds a leading position in Asia.

The grouper aquaculture technology in Taiwan is particularly noteworthy. From fingerling cultivation to adult growth, Taiwan has established a complete industry chain. High-quality grouper not only supplies the domestic market but is also exported in large quantities to places like Hong Kong and mainland China, becoming an important item in Taiwan's agricultural exports.

## Seafood Preservation and Cold Chain

The development of modern seafood culture in Taiwan is inseparable from cold chain technology. From rapid freezing on fishing boats to low-temperature distribution in markets, a sophisticated cold chain system ensures the quality of seafood. This allows consumers in inland areas to enjoy fresh maritime flavors, greatly expanding the reach of seafood culture.

Sashimi counters in supermarkets and seafood bento boxes in convenience stores have made seafood consumption more convenient through modernized sales methods. The establishment of systems such as quality labeling and origin traceability has also increased consumer confidence in seafood safety.

## Seafood and Healthy Eating

With rising health awareness, the status of seafood in Taiwan's dietary culture has become even more important. Rich in protein, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals, seafood has become an essential component of a healthy diet. Low-oil and low-salt cooking methods, such as sashimi, steamed fish, and grilled fish, are becoming increasingly popular.

At the same time, seafood preparation methods are becoming more diverse. Japanese, Korean, and Western culinary techniques have integrated into Taiwan's seafood culture, creating a richer sensory experience. This cultural fusion reflects the openness and inclusivity of Taiwan's food culture.

## Reflections on Sustainable Development

Facing challenges regarding marine resources, Taiwan's seafood culture is also contemplating sustainable development. Efforts to reduce overfishing, develop responsible fisheries, and promote eco-friendly aquaculture are all aimed at ensuring the long-term continuity of seafood culture.

Consumer education is also a vital link. Promoting consumption concepts such as choosing seasonal seafood, supporting local fisheries, and reducing waste helps protect marine resources. Some restaurants have begun offering sustainable seafood menus, labeling information such as the origin and fishing method of the fish, allowing consumers to make more responsible choices.

Taiwan's seafood culture embodies the deep connection between an island people and the ocean. From traditional fishing techniques to modern aquaculture technology, and from simple cooking methods to exquisite culinary arts, seafood culture bears witness to the developmental changes in Taiwanese society. In today's globalized world, this food culture, rooted in the local environment, is one of Taiwan's most precious cultural assets.
