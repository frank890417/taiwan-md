> **30-second overview:** The Alishan Forest Railway is not merely a tourist train carrying passengers to watch the sunrise. It was built in 1906 to haul high-mountain timber down the slopes, reached Erwanping in 1912, and only later shifted toward passenger service and tourism. Typhoon Morakot in 2009 brought it to a standstill for 15 years. Today this 71.6-kilometer route reconnects the Chiayi plain, forestry settlements, and high-mountain forests. When a railway born to develop the forest becomes a cultural asset, what are we really preserving — the tracks, or the entire history of how people and the mountain have coexisted?[^1] [^2] [^3]

## What the Train Crosses First Is Not Scenery, but an Elevation Gap

In December 1912, the Alishan Railway from Chiayi to Erwanping officially opened. The starting point sits at roughly 30 meters above sea level; Alishan Station stands at 2,216 meters.[^1] [^2] Heading from the plain into the mountains, the train passes through farmland, then bamboo groves, tea plantations, and forests at successive elevations. Passengers see the scenery change layer by layer; engineers faced the challenge of keeping the track continuous across steep slopes, fault lines, and torrential rain.

The operating length of this route is approximately 71.6 kilometers, while its cultural landscape potential points span Chiayi City, Chiayi County, and Nantou County, encompassing the railway, settlements, forestry ruins, and the natural environment.[^2] It is therefore not an isolated piece of transport infrastructure, but a linear cultural landscape that strings together a lowland city, mountain settlements, and ecological zones.

![Alishan Forest Railway DL 36 diesel locomotive at Beimen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fd/Taiwan%2C_Alishan_Forest_Railway_%27DL_36%27_at_Beimen.jpg)

_Figure: DL 36 diesel locomotive at Beimen. Photographer Industrial Wales. CC BY-SA 2.0. Image page and license information at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan,%5FAlishan%5FForest%5FRailway%5F%27DL%5F36%27%5Fat%5FBeimen.jpg).[^10]_

Precisely because of this, the Alishan Forest Railway has several "termini." For passengers, the terminus is Alishan Station. For the early forestry industry, the terminus was where timber reached Chiayi and entered factories and markets. For the cultural landscape, the terminus cannot stop at the station platform; it must extend to how the settlements along the line continue to live. This multiplicity of termini makes a train journey not just a movement from low to high, but a passage between different eras of Taiwan.

The Alishan Forest Railway's distinctiveness lies not only in "climbing the mountain." It simultaneously possesses the characteristics of a forest railway, a mountain-climbing railway, and a high-mountain railway, using 762 mm narrow gauge and leaving behind spirals, switchbacks, and wide curves born of the terrain.[^2] The Duli Shan spiral lets the train circle the mountain to gain elevation. Where space is insufficient, Z-shaped switchbacks let the train gain height by alternating forward and reverse movements.

| Place you pass    | Historical traces it leaves                                                                               |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Beimen Station    | Wooden station at the plain end, connected to Chiayi's timber-city texture.[^2]                           |
| Duli Shan         | Spiral alignment handling steep terrain, letting the train gain elevation.[^2]                            |
| Fenchihu          | Midway station where forestry work and mountain settlement intersect.[^2]                                 |
| Zhaoping, Zhushan | From forestry lines and high-mountain railway, gradually turning toward tourism and sunrise journeys.[^2] |

This table is not a travel itinerary, but a method for reading the railway: every station name simultaneously points to a terrain type, a segment of industrial history, and a way in which people have paused in the mountains.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** What is most worth remembering about the Alishan Railway is not that it is "beautiful," but that it turned an elevation gap into a visible engineering language. Outside the window the forest belts change; beneath the wheels the alignment answers the same question: the mountain will not yield to the railway; the railway can only learn to go around.

The train is slow, and that slowness makes this relationship easier to see. It does not compress the mountain scenery into a snapshot from a freeway; instead it lets passengers pass through different forest formations, stations, and tunnels. This speed also preserves the original sense of scale of an industrial railway. This is not romanticizing slow travel, but acknowledging that in the mountains, safety, maintenance, and observation all require time.

## It Was Originally a Road That Turned Forests into Commodities

In 1903, the Government-General of Taiwan began planning the development of the Alishan forests. Construction started in 1906 by the Fujita-gumi, but financial problems led to a switch to government operation. The Chiayi–Erwanping section opened in 1912, spanning 66.6 kilometers. As forest development extended, the line later reached Alishan, forming today's operating length of approximately 71.6 kilometers.[^1]

This history cannot be whitewashed by the four characters "century-old railway." The Alishan Forest Railway was built to transport timber. After the extension to Zhaoping in 1914, logging operations formally commenced. From the Japanese colonial period until the war's end in 1945, the Alishan forestry operations continued for about 30 years, and the old-growth cypress forests were heavily logged as a result.[^2]

![View of Beimen Station platform from Alishan Forest Railway carriage](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0f/Alishan_Forest_Railway%2C_Beimen_Station%2C_Chiayi_CIty_%28Taiwan%29.jpg)

_Figure: View of Beimen Station platform from Alishan Forest Railway carriage. Photographer Malcolm Koo. CC BY-SA 4.0. Image page and license information at [Wikimedia Commons](<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Alishan_Forest_Railway,_Beimen_Station,_Chiayi_CIty_(Taiwan).jpg>).[^11]\_

The changes brought by the railway were not confined to the mountains. Lumber mills, timber wholesale and processing, employee dormitories, shops, and schools clustered along the transport system, and Chiayi gradually formed an urban fabric centered on the timber industry, earning the name "Timber Capital."[^2] A single industrial line thus did two things at once: it moved forest resources out of the mountains, and brought urban life into them.

This is the Alishan Forest Railway's first irony. Today it is treated as slow travel for getting close to the forest, but its original mission was to make the forest more efficiently exploitable. If it is only written up as a romantic high-mountain train, readers will miss the Taiwan history this railway truly carries: modernization, forestry, urban growth, and the process by which natural resources were recalculated.

Academic research discusses the Alishan Forest Railway at the intersection of "railway cultural asset preservation" and "cultural tourism," rather than treating it as a single attraction.[^7] This angle matters because it reveals that preservation work faces two kinds of time simultaneously: one is the material age of the railway, stations, and locomotives; the other is the social age of industrial decline, tourism's rise, and the transformation of local life. Restore only the former, and the latter vanishes from the story.

## From Logging Line to Cultural Landscape — Transition Is Not Erasing the Past

After the logging industry withdrew, the railway did not disappear with it. Its function gradually shifted toward passenger transport, tourism, and cultural education, and the stations, settlements, tunnels, bridges, and forestry ruins along the line turned from production facilities into historical sites to be preserved, interpreted, and used. The Forestry and Nature Conservation Agency summarizes this change as a settlement fabric and human-land interaction transformed from logging industry to tourism.[^1]

Beimen Station is a good entry point to this transformation. Formed roughly between 1910 and 1912, it is a Japanese-style wooden station built with Alishan red cypress, and the first space at the plain end for understanding the forest railway.[^2] Walking from downtown Chiayi into Beimen Station, visitors see not the summit scenery, but how timber was received, processed, and distributed in the city. Here the "forest" and the "city" are placed on two sides of the same platform.

In 2018, the government established the Alishan Forest Railway and Cultural Heritage Management Office to more centrally promote research, education, and cultural asset management. In 2019, "Alishan Forestry and Railway" was registered as Taiwan's first national-level important cultural landscape, its value organized into five dimensions: natural ecology, forestry development, railway technology, settlement life, and cultural memory.[^1]

The significance of this registration lies in its refusal to protect only a single station or a single stretch of track. The object of the cultural landscape is the railway together with the work, residence, transport, and natural environment around it. In other words, preservation is not locking an old carriage in a glass case; it is keeping a once-operational set of relationships still understandable.

Alishan has thereby entered the discussion of World Heritage potential sites. Official materials describe it as a 32,900-hectare area encompassing settlements and ecological environments around the railway, running from Chiayi, Beimen, Zhuqi, Zhangnaoliao, Fenchihu all the way to Zhaoping.[^2] This scale reminds us that Alishan is not a "scenic spot," but a system spanning city, mountains, and industrial memory.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** The hardest part of cultural assets to preserve is often not the wood and steel rails, but how they were once used by people. If the Alishan Forest Railway leaves only pretty stations, only the shell remains. What it truly needs to preserve is how a road changed the destinies of Chiayi, mountain villages, and the forest.

## 2009: The Mountain Laid Bare the Railway's Fragility

After Typhoon Morakot in 2009, 421 instances of damage — soil loss, road collapse, bridge breaks — appeared along the Alishan Forest Railway. Official retrospectives note the railway was therefore suspended for 15 years. Chiayi to Fenchihu resumed in 2014, but Typhoon Dujuan in 2015 delayed the remaining sections' restoration plans once again.[^3] [^4]

This was not a "fix it and return to original condition" project. Mountain roadbeds are carried away by water, tunnels encounter rockfall, and the original alignment may lie in geological environments no longer suitable for carrying trains. Interviews recorded by National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University mention that the restoration team assessed a new tunnel between Pingshena and Erwanping, and addressed the collapse of Tunnel No. 42 between Shizilu and Pingshena.[^5]

In 2021, the management unit built a new tunnel of approximately 1.1 kilometers between Shizilu and Zhaoping, at a project cost of about NT$420 million. Construction was simultaneously affected by groundwater seepage, rockfall, labor shortages, and blasting material supply.[^4] These figures are not just engineering scale; they also illustrate that "full-line reopening" is not simply reconnecting old tracks, but re-judging which places can be retained and which must be realigned.

On July 6, 2024, the Chiayi–Alishan full line resumed service.[^3] The Ministry of Agriculture's English travel feature describes the main line as a 71.6-kilometer route from Chiayi Station to Alishan Station, with Chiayi Station at roughly 30 meters and Alishan Station at 2,216 meters. Full-line resumption is therefore not just tourism news; it also represents the reconnection of a mountain public transport link long severed by disaster.[^6]

This reopening date also changes the meaning of "Alishan little train." _The New York Times'_ English photo essay places this journey at the intersection of Taiwan's cultural assets and international visitors.[^8] But what truly gives it substance is not whether it can become a pretty postcard, but whether passengers can know why this line was once severed, who restored it, and what it must bear after restoration.

## The End of Restoration Is Not Reopening, but the Next Rain

After the 2024 return to service, the railway's safety work no longer relies solely on personnel patrols. The restoration project added track and slope anomaly detection systems. Between Tunnels No. 29 and 30, the system can issue alerts when rockfall or fallen trees obstruct the track, allowing station staff to notify the driver.[^4]

Interviews by National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University also record the restoration team's emphasis on long-term monitoring, including using LiDAR to track terrain changes and identify potential risks early. The interviews mention that after Typhoon Kemi in 2024, the forest railway briefly suspended service to clear debris and fallen trees, then resumed operations.[^5] For a mountain railway, "restoration" does not mean it will never be interrupted again; it means that after each interruption, they know how to judge the next step more quickly and safely.

The 2025 reopening anniversary review by the authorities divides the restoration content into track, road, motive power, digitalization of line-side communications, and station development.[^3] This list looks engineering-oriented, but it precisely explains why cultural preservation cannot rely on nostalgia alone: for an old railway to stay alive, it must simultaneously possess historical credibility, modern safety standards, and a system capable of sustained maintenance.

When the railway becomes a tourist route again, new pressures emerge. Official World Heritage potential site materials have long noted that Alishan bears human impact from visitors and vehicles, and that disaster reconstruction and environmental maintenance require long-term effort.[^2] Tourism can let more people understand the cultural landscape, but it may also burden the mountains with more traffic, waste, noise, and maintenance loads. "Letting the world see Alishan" and "not consuming Alishan away" must both hold true.

## The Questions a Railway Leaves Behind

The most moving thing about the Alishan Forest Railway is not that it finally defeated the mountain. It never defeated the mountain: the 1906 engineering had to wind around the mountain's form; the 2009 deluge could wash away the roadbed; the 2015 collapses could delay a near-complete reopening; every typhoon after 2024 will test it anew.[^3] [^4] [^5]

What it truly achieved was letting human engineering gradually acknowledge the mountain's conditions. The early railway treated the forest as a resource to be hauled out; the later cultural landscape forces us to place the railway, settlements, ecology, and disaster on the same map. This is not rewriting the past logging history into an environmental story, but acknowledging that Taiwan once used this road to develop the mountains, and must now use new governance to face the consequences that development left behind.

So, when boarding the Alishan Forest Railway, you can remember two directions. The train goes up, showing you the transition from tropical to warm-temperate to subalpine forest. History goes back, showing you how timber, workers, the city, Indigenous traditional territories, wind disasters, and restoration engineering are layered on the same road.[^2]

You can remember one more direction: look to the side. Alishan's story is not only on the main line ahead of the carriage, but also in the preserved branch lines, the old corridors turned into trails, the shops in the settlements, the forests around the stations, and the spaces where the Tsou (鄒族) have long lived. Official English materials cite the Shuishan Line's old corridor converted into a trail, illustrating that suspension does not equal disappearance. A line can continue to be used in a different way.[^6]

The Alishan Forest Railway did not take Taiwan away from nature. It is more like a long reminder: we once entered the forest on steel rails, and only after learning to respect the forest's speed, gradient, and uncertainty can we possibly continue to stay there.
