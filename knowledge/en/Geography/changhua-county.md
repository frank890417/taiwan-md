# Changhua County: The Agricultural Giant That Defeated DuPont But Cannot Retain Its Youth

> **30-Second Summary:** In 1709, Shi Shih-bang constructed the Baobaozhen to divert water from the Luochuan River to irrigate over 19,000 hectares, finishing 211 years earlier than the Jianan Dazhen built by Badian and Yi. Established in 1723 (Yongzheng Year 1) after being carved out of Zhulu County, with the intent "to establish learning and teachers to promote refinement," the history of Changhua began here. In 1786, during the Lin Shuangwen incident, the county seat was attacked but Lukang was spared; in 1788, Emperor Qianlong decreed the construction of the Mazu temple (Xinzu Temple) in Lukang. The Fanxing Roundhouse opened in 1922, and the anti-DuPont movement by Lukang residents in 1986 marked Taiwan's first environmental movement. As of April 2026, Changhua County has a population of 1.206 million people, less than 50,000 short of the 1.25 million threshold required to upgrade to a direct-level municipality. The magnetic pull of Taipei has always been strong, yet this county had its own established city dating back to 1723.

## A Chrysanthemum Night City with Forty Million Blooms Annually

If you ask a person from Changhua when the county is least like what you imagine, they will not tell you about daytime in Lukang Old Street (which is for tourists). They will tell you about night in Tianwei.

Tianwei Garden is located on both sides of Provincial Highway 145, with over 140 businesses spread across a commercial area spanning 3.6 kilometers. In 2021, the Ministry of Agriculture designated it as a leisure agricultural zone, covering nearly 297 hectares[^1]. Taiwan Provincial Highway 1 is the main artery of Western Taiwan; the garden is a field of chrysanthemums that grew from this artery.

But if you visit at night, you see something strange. The entire chrysanthemum field lights up. Chrysanthemum fields, one hectare after another, are illuminated, looking like a galaxy fallen into the fields from afar.

These are not decorative lights. Chrysanthemums are short-day plants; excessive sunlight exceeding 12 hours in summer inhibits flowering. Taiwanese farmers use artificial lighting to extend the "day," making the chrysanthemums think it is still winter, thereby avoiding supply and demand imbalance caused by clustered blooming seasons. Consequently, the entire Tianwei chrysanthemum field stays awake all night, and locals call this scenery "Chrysanthemum Night City." The chrysanthemum fields cover an area of 120 hectares, yielding 40 million blooms annually[^2].

Forty million chrysanthemums. In just one township, Tianwei, within Changhua County's 26 townships, it shoulders half the glory of Taiwan's flower industry through the method of "not letting the chrysanthemums sleep." At the same time, a Dutch flower auction center 120 kilometers away is capturing the global market using the same strategy. **This is the contemporary structure of Changhua: an agricultural county with an established city since 1723, surviving by finding loopholes in global agricultural competition every day.**

## "South Intercepts Huwei, North Reaches Dajia"

In the first year of Yongzheng (1723), the imperial court made a decision.

The original text of the "Grand Construction Records" on the Changhua County government website states: "The court divided over a hundred li of land between Zhulu, intercepting Huwei to the south and reaching Dajia to the north, establishing Changhua County, setting up the county office at the midpoint; this is where Changhua was established."[^3] Wikipedia also records: "In view of the vastness of Zhulu County being insufficient, Emperor Yongzheng carved out the land north of the old Huwei Stream and south of the Dajia Stream in 1723 (Yongzheng Year 1) to establish Changhua County."[^4].

The origin of the county name is as follows. Emperor Yongzheng intended "to establish learning and teachers to promote refinement" and "to showcase the prosperity of the Holy Son of Heaven in the sea region," naming this land Changhua[^3]. "Midpoint" refers to the phonetic translation used by Han Chinese for the Banshen community, a group that, along with parts of the Hongya tribe, were the original inhabitants of this plain for thousands of years[^5]. After Changhua was established, Banshen Town served as the county seat before being renamed Changhua.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The establishment dates of Taiwanese administrative divisions are often confused. Changhua County was separated from Zhulu County in 1723 (Yongzheng Year 1), nearly 90 years earlier than Kamalang Prefecture in 1812, and 152 years earlier than Taibei Prefecture established by Shen Baozhen in 1875. In other words, Changhua is one of Taiwan's earliest established counties. However, the claim "Changhua Plain is the Nth largest plain in Taiwan" is often misreported. The Jianan Plain (4,550 km²) is the largest plain in Taiwan, and the Pingtung Plain (1,210 km²) is the second largest[^6]. The Changhua Plain is about 900 km², ranking third or smaller. Changhua County itself is the county with the smallest area on the island of Taiwan (1,074.39 km²; according to [Changhua Tourism Information Network](https://tour.chcg.gov.tw/), it boasts the reputation of being a "Taiwanese granary and agricultural giant" despite its small size). The plain accounts for 87.68% of the county's area[^7], with an agricultural density far exceeding its size.

The boundaries set in 1723 are still relevant today. Changhua County’s 26 townships are divided into three topographical zones: the Plain (Changhua, Yinlin, Hemei, Beidou), the Coast (Lukang, Fuxing, Erlin, Fangyuan), and the Plateau (the western side of Baguashan). The Baguashan Plateau is about 210 km², with Hengshan peaking at 440 meters. It stretches from the south bank of the Dadu Stream to the north bank of the Luochuan River. The coast includes Wanggong Oyster Fields and Erlin Oyster Fields, while the plateau features the Baguashan Eagle Watching route.

## Mr. Lin: The Water Conservancy Legend of Baobaozhen in 1709

Before the county was established, this land had already been rewritten by one person.

In the 48th year of Kangxi (1709), Shi Shih-bang began constructing the Baobaozhen in the Banshen area, diverting water from the Luochuan River to build the canal. The project was completed in the 58th year of Kangxi (1719)[^8]. The Baobaozhen is a full **211 years** older than the Jianan Dazhen, which was completed by Badian and Yi in 1930.

But the construction stalled at the beginning. Wikipedia records the legend as follows: "It is said that during the initial construction of Baobaozhen, attempts to divert water into the canal were repeatedly unsuccessful. Legend says an old man came to see Shi Shih-bang and provided him with hydraulic diagrams; Shi Shih-bang proceeded with the construction using this method and employed 'earthwork techniques,' which was successful. After the canal was completed, the old man refused payment and did not reveal his name, only calling himself 'Mr. Lin'; later generations revered his kindness and built Mr. Lin's Temple at the head of the Ershui Canal to commemorate him."[^8]

![Water intake of Baobaozhen, located in Yuanquan Village, Ershui Township, constructed by Shi Shih-bang in 1709. This is where the irrigation backbone of the entire Changhua Plain diverts water.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3c/%E5%85%AB%E5%A0%A1%E5%9C%B3.jpg/1280px-%E5%85%AB%E5%A0%A1%E5%9C%B3.jpg)
_Baobaozhen. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%85%AB%E5%A0%A1%E5%9C%B3.jpg)._

The true identity of Mr. Lin remains unknown. But every November, the Baobaozhen waterway in Yuanquan Village, Ershui Township, hosts the "Water Running Festival," a ceremony revived after the 1995 event by the Cultural Construction Committee of the Executive Yuan, titled "Baobaozhen Legend." The water capturers—"**head wrapped with red cloth, body clad in straw rain gear, feet wearing straw sandals**"—run barefoot into the newly released water according to ancient rites, welcoming the year's irrigation water[^9].

The Baobaozhen is considered one of the "Three Great Water Conservancy Projects of the Qing Dynasty," alongside Taipei's Lucongzhen and Kaohsiung's Caogongzhen, and it was the earliest of the three. The current combined irrigated area of Baobao One and Two Canals is approximately 21,400 hectares[^10]. The rice harvested three times a year in the Changhua Plain, along with Yinlin peaches, Ershui pomelos, Tianwei chrysanthemums, and Erlin grapes—all this agricultural density rests on the 21,400-hectare waterway from 1709. **A project whose legend involves an old man refusing payment sustained the most densely cultivated land among the 22 counties and cities.**

The Baobaozhen crosses the border between Nantou and Changhua, making it a shared heritage of two counties. The water source for Shi Shih-bang came from the Luochuan River diversion weir located between Jiji Township and Ershui Township in Nantou County, which then diverts into the Changhua Plain.

## After "One Prefecture, Two Lucang, Three Wanhua" Ended

The year after the county was established (1724), a port emerged on the map of Zhulu County: Lukang.

The _Zhulu County Gazetteer_, completed in 1717 (Kangxi Year 56), is the earliest record showing Lukang on a map. The official website of Lukang Township recorded in 1728 (Yongzheng Year 6): "In Yongzheng Year 6, Changhua County established a granary at the west side of Mishi Street in Luzai Port, with a plaque reading: Tianyu Zheng Gong (this is mentioned in the Daoguang edition of the Changhua County Gazetteer, totaling sixteen granaries)"[^11]. The 16 granaries of Lukang formed the basis of this port's Mishi Street.

The real turning point was in 1784. Wikipedia states: "Its prosperity began because the Qing government established special officials to manage trade, imitating the models of Lu'er and Xiamen, opening up trade between 'Jianjiang Port belonging to Quanzhou Prefecture, Fujian' and 'Luzai Port, Changhua County, Taiwan.' Subsequently, suburban commerce flourished, rapidly becoming the largest port in Central Taiwan."[^12] During the peak period of Qianlong through Daoguang, the "Eight Suburbs" trading companies and 300 merchant ships traveled back and forth from Lukang. Lin Zhensong was the wealthiest merchant in the entire port. The phrase "One Prefecture, Two Lucang, Three Wanhua" refers to the commercial map of Taiwan from the late 18th to the early 19th century.

![Longshan Temple in Lukang, relocated to its current site in 1786, featuring a three-bay, two-courtyard, seven-opening structure; the ceiling painting above the stage is one of the oldest and largest preserved works in Taiwan.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/49/Lukang_Longshan_Temple_in_2017.jpg/1280px-Lukang_Longshan_Temple_in_2017.jpg)
_Longshan Temple in Lukang. Photo: Outlookxp, [CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lukang_Longshan_Temple_in_2017.jpg)._

Two events occurred simultaneously in 1786 (Qianlong Year 51). One was the relocation of Longshan Temple in Lukang to its current site by Grand Secretary Lin Zhensong and Governor Chen Bangguang. Wikipedia records the architectural features: "The ceiling painting structure above the stage of Longshan Temple in Lukang is one of the oldest and largest preserved works in Taiwan. The Bagua ceiling was established above the stage, providing resonance during performances."[^13] The three-bay, two-courtyard, seven-opening structure is one of the oldest and most complete Qing Dynasty temples remaining in Taiwan. The other event was the outbreak of the Lin Shuangwen incident; while Changhua County seat was attacked, Lukang remained uncaptured due to its geographical location and commercial strength. The Qing court sent Fu Kang'an from Lukang to quell the rebellion, and this campaign became an opportunity for the Qing court to re-evaluate the strategic position of Lukang.

In 1788 (Qianlong Year 53), after Fu Kang'an quelled the rebellion, Emperor Qianlong decreed the construction of a Mazu temple in Lukang with official funds to showcase the miraculous deeds of Mazu who assisted in quelling the unrest. This officially funded temple was called "Xinzu Temple," and its full name is "Imperial Edicted Tianhou Temple." It is the only Mazu temple in Taiwan built with an imperial edict and paid for by state funds, with Lin Zhensong of the Dimao Company contributing the remaining construction costs[^14].

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Lukang has two famous Mazu temples that are often confused. One is "Lukang Tianhou Temple," whose predecessor dates back to the late Ming Dynasty and was expanded in 1725 (Yongzheng Year 3), making it the "only temple dedicated to the ancestral Mazu from Meizhou" in Taiwan, which tourists are most familiar with today. The other is "Xinzu Temple (Imperial Edicted Tianhou Temple)," built by state funds in 1788. The former represents the accumulation of folk religion; the latter is a monument to national power. These two temples represent Lukang possessing both a "folk maritime guardian deity" and an "imperial rebellion merit" type of Mazu culture. Stage 0 would flatten these two completely different historical contexts by treating the '1788 Lukang Tianhou Temple' as just one temple.

Then, Lukang began to decline. Three blows came sequentially.

The first was port siltation. Wikipedia states: "Lukang's port advantage gradually declined due to mud and sand accumulation. After the Jiaqing period, merchant ships increasingly shifted to Wanggong Port in the south."[^12] The second was the longitudinal railway line. When the longitudinal railway was completed in 1908, it passed through Changhua City but bypassed Lukang. The official website of Lukang Children wrote: "During the Japanese colonial period, Lukang had a low administrative level (it was merely a 'town' in the administrative classification), and major public transportation systems such as Taiwan Provincial Highway 1 and the longitudinal railway system did not pass through it."[^15] The third was the Japanese port reduction strategy in 1934: the Japanese concentrated port functions in Keelung and Kaohsiung, effectively canceling Lukang's port functions.

> **✦** "Later, the main reason for the prosperity and decline of Lukang was port siltation, making docking difficult; coupled with the longitudinal highway and railway not passing through this area, leading to transportation inconvenience." (Taiwan Guanghua Magazine, _Lukang_[^16])

The era of "Two Lucang" in "One Prefecture, Two Lucang, Three Wanhua" ended by the mid-19th century. Lukang's most vibrant period lasted only 70 years.

## In 1986, the People of Lukang Said "No"

But Lukang was not finished. It had another chance to speak.

In 1985, the American company DuPont planned to set up a factory in the Changhua-Hsinchu Industrial Zone to produce titanium dioxide. This was a high-polluting chemical process, using mineral sand as raw material and producing sulfuric acid and heavy metal wastewater as byproducts. The coast of Lukang is home to Wanggong Oyster Fields and Fuxing Wetlands, and residents feared that marine pollution would directly destroy the fishery.

The struggle ignited in 1986. Lukang residents launched petitions, street protests, and direct confrontations with the DuPont company. At the time, Taiwan was under martial law (which ended on July 15, 1987), making it difficult for residents to protest foreign investment. In March 1987, DuPont announced its withdrawal from Lukang. The Executive Yuan established the Environmental Protection Administration that same year.

The Chemical Knowledge Map of the EPA records: "Taiwan's first street environmental movement—the anti-DuPont movement."[^17] The same article continues: "After the success of the anti-DuPont movement in Lukang, anti-chemical wars were ignited sequentially across various areas, including Houjin against Five Chemicals, Linyuan against Three Chemicals, Yilan against Six Chemicals, and Qixu against Seven Chemicals."

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The historical positioning of the anti-DuPont movement is often underestimated. Placing it solely within the framework of "Changhua local issues" is insufficient: this was the beginning of Taiwan's environmental movement. Before 1986, social movements in Taiwan mainly revolved around political systems (extra-party movements, the美麗島 Incident); the anti-DuPont movement first brought "environmental rights" onto the streets. The subsequent Houjin against Five Chemicals (1987), Linyuan against Three Chemicals (1988), Yilan against Six Chemicals (1990), and Qixu against Seven Chemicals (1998) were extensions of this path. In 1986, the people of Lukang opened another dimension of Taiwanese civil society with a single "No": not just democracy, but also environment. The significance of Lukang in 1986 rivals that of Cheng Nanrong and the extra-party movements nationally, but what is remembered nationwide is political activism; very few remember the people of Lukang.

Forty years later, in 2026, Lukang still speaks, but it says something different.

Changhua Children (founded in 2012) stated in an interview: "Many people move to cities for work, but we chose our current jobs so that we can live in Lukang."[^18] The Changhua Children website further writes: "In addition to nostalgia and a relatively solid cultural foundation, the most important key to local revitalization for Lukang is its 'industrial' base."[^19] Starting in 2015, they co-organized the "Autumn Art Festival" with the Preserve Lukang Movement Association, centering the curation on "local participation first, then talk about local art festivals." In 2020, the "Lukang Future Center" was established to guide youth entrepreneurship and retention.

This small town that defeated the foreign company DuPont now retains its youth using the same industrial thinking. In 1986, they drove away pollution; in 2020, they seek to retain jobs. But these two tasks are completely different in difficulty. The latter has no external enemy to focus on; it must contend with the magnetic pull of Taipei, salary gaps in the north, and the shifting industrial center of Taiwan as a whole.

## Twelve Railroad Tracks in Half a Circle

If you want to see how a city handles its industrial heritage, go to the Changhua Fanxing Roundhouse.

Another in-depth report from _Keelung Port 130 Years_ recorded: "The Changhua Fanxing Roundhouse, commissioned in October 1922, officially entered its centennial year."[^20]. During the Japanese colonial period, the Fanxing Roundhouse was a steam locomotive turning center: 12 tracks radiated in a semi-circular arrangement around an 18-meter diameter turntable. After the train arrived, it turned on the turntable before being routed to various tracks for maintenance or resupply. Each track had a chimney stack on its roof because the smoke from the steam locomotives had to vent upwards.

After WWII, Taiwan had six Fanxing Roundhouses (Keelung, Taipei, Hsinchu, Chiayi, Changhua, Kaohsiung). The other five were dismantled and converted into modern roundhouses in the 1990s. Changhua is the only one that survived.

The reason for its survival was resistance. A reporter recorded: "27 years ago, seeing the Chiayi Fanxing Roundhouse being demolished with a group of partners concerned about railway cultural heritage... they who were reluctant to see the roundhouse and turntable simply dismantled and converted into a modern roundhouse started a preservation movement for the fanxing roundhouse, and finally, the Changhua Fanxing Roundhouse was designated as a county-level historic site in 2000."[^20] On August 1, 2022, the Ministry of Culture announced its upgrade to a **National Historic Site**.

![Changhua Fanxing Roundhouse, commissioned in 1922, with 12 tracks radiating in a semi-circle. The only surviving fanxing roundhouse in Taiwan.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Changhua_Roundhouse_07.jpg/1280px-Changhua_Roundhouse_07.jpg)
_Changhua Fanxing Roundhouse. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Changhua_Roundhouse_07.jpg)._

But the struggle is not over. In 2021, the feasibility study for the Changhua urban railway elevated project was passed, with a total budget of NT$39.95 billion, and the central government's share being NT$29.63 billion. This project will "exclude three witness facilities—the 'coal water platform,' the 'oil depot,' and the 'maintenance depot'—from the scope of the Changhua railway elevation construction area."[^20]. While the main body of the National Historic Site is preserved, the three surrounding key facilities lack space under the elevated rail project.

This is the contemporary state of the Changhua Fanxing Roundhouse: a semi-circular building built by the Japanese 100 years ago to turn steam locomotives, which in 2026 is negotiating its boundaries with an NT$40 billion elevated railway project. The water on the turntable still spins daily for active diesel locomotives.

## Erlin in 1925: The Price of Sugarcane Collected by the Japanese

Changhua has another underestimated story. It was the starting point of Taiwan's farmers' movement.

In June 1925, Dr. Li Yingzhang (a director of a cultural association) established the "Erlin Sugar Farmers' Cooperative" in Erlin, demanding that Lin Benyuan Sugar Company raise the sugarcane purchase price and abolish the "raw material collection zone" system (where specific areas could only sell sugarcane to designated sugar mills). On October 21, representatives from Lin Benyuan Sugar Factory forcibly harvested sugarcane fields belonging to non-members. On October 23, a large patrol surrounded Dr. Li Yingzhang's clinic and arrested him.

Wikipedia's entry on the "Erlin Incident" records: "A total of 93 people were arrested," and "subsequent searches led to more than 400 arrests."[^21]. After the incident, local farmers' cooperatives were established in Fengshan Street and Madou Street; in June 1926, the island-wide "Taiwan Farmers' Cooperative" was established, which was Taiwan's first island-wide farmers' organization.

The Erlin Incident is often glossed over in Taiwanese history textbooks, but its significance resonates with the anti-DuPont movement. **Both movements occurred in Changhua and were starting points for certain social movements in Taiwan: Erlin in 1925 was the beginning of the farmers' movement; Lukang in 1986 was the beginning of the environmental movement. Changhua County is a place where "firsts" repeatedly appear in the history of Taiwanese social movements.** How could an agricultural county that was digging canals in 1709 become the starting point for two social movements? Perhaps because the people of this land's dependence on water, land, and crops was so tangible that their reaction was the quickest when these things were threatened by outsiders.

36 years after the Erlin Incident, Taiwan experienced post-war periods, the 228 Incident, the White Terror, and the Ten Major Construction Projects. Then, another event occurred within Changhua.

## Baguashan Great Buddha: The 22-Meter Concrete Bodhisattva

Changhua did something special after WWII. It built a great Buddha on top of Mount Baguashan.

The time was 1956. The first County Magistrate of Changhua, Chen Xiqing, initiated the construction of the "Largest Buddha in East Asia." Construction was interrupted by the August 7th flood in 1958 and completed in 1961[^22]. The statue is 22 meters high (seven zhang two chi), with a lotus base diameter of 14 meters, made of reinforced concrete, designed by Lin Qingyao. The interior of the statue has 6 levels that can be visited.

A report from _China Times_ recorded: "After the Great Buddha was completed in 1961," local residents formed a belief: "Changhua 'has wind but no typhoons, rain but no disasters,' all believing it is due to the blessings of the Great Buddha."[^22].

![Baguashan Great Buddha, completed in 1961, standing 22 meters tall, made of reinforced concrete, designed by Lin Qingyao. The interior has 6 levels that can be visited.](/article-images/geography/changhua-04.webp)
_Baguashan Great Buddha. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%BD%B0%E5%8C%96%E5%85%AB%E5%8D%A6%E5%B1%B1%E5%A4%A7%E4%BD%9B31.jpg)._

The religious objects within Changhua County—from the wooden Bodhisattva at Lukang Longshan Temple in 1786 and Xinzu Temple in 1788 to the Earth God temple near Tianwei chrysanthemum fields—do not share a continuous timeline from the Qing era to after WWII. Each one is a specific answer by the people of that time to the question: "What kind of power do we need protection from?"

> **✦** "Many people move to cities for work, but we chose our current jobs so that we can live in Lukang."[^18]

## 1.2 Million People Fall Below Upgrade Threshold

In April 2026, the total population of Changhua County was **1,206,458**, having fallen below the 1.25 million threshold required to upgrade to a direct-level municipality[^23].

⚠️ Note that this number will continue to decline. Changhua County has experienced continuous population outflow from 2024–2026, with projections showing it falling below 1.2 million by the end of 2026. Changhua still holds the status of "the county with the largest population outside the Six Metropolises (the seventh largest administrative region in Taiwan)," but the distance between this framing and that of a "direct-level municipality" is widening.

Population distribution (as of late October 2024):

- Changhua City: 223,933 people (most populous county seat)
- Yinlin City: 122,733 people (second largest)
- Hemei Township: 87,509 people
- Lukang Township: approx. 80,000

There is a common misunderstanding regarding the relationship between the county seat, Changhua City, and Yinlin City, which has the second-largest population. Changhua City is the administrative center; Yinlin City is the commercial center along the central corridor railway line. The station culture of Yinlin chicken leg lunch boxes is completely different from the northern pork chop lunch boxes; Yinlin peaches and bananas are food cultures born from agriculture. These two cities have an asymmetrical function, yet they have been in a population tug-of-war for 30 years.

The reasons for population outflow are clear but complex: **the "magnetic pull" of Taipei** (young people moving to Taipei for work), **low birth rates**, **few job opportunities**, and **insufficient medical resources**. Changhua County lacks a single driving industry like Hsinchu Science Park; it relies on three legs—agriculture, traditional industries (Hemei textiles, Yinlin footwear), and tourism (Lukang, Tianwei)—but each leg is constrained by global competition.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The common narrative is "Changhua is an agricultural county." This narrative is correct but incomplete. Another side of Changhua is the "**central corridor hub that failed to upgrade a direct-level municipality**." Its location (sandwiched between Taipei and Yunlin), its population size (the largest outside the Six Metropolises), and its history (established in 1723, earlier than Taibei Prefecture) all suggest it should have upgraded. But it never did. During the May 5 Great South Construction Reform (where Taipei retained direct status / New Taipei / Taoyuan were not yet promoted / Taichung merged counties / Tainan merged counties / Kaohsiung merged counties), Changhua was a "close call" candidate; when Taoyuan upgraded in 2014, Changhua was skipped. The political economy of upgrading involves three dimensions: population, tax base, and administrative resources. The core question is whether the "hat" of a direct-level municipality can bring resources. And Changhua, caught in Taipei's shadow, has young people working in Taipei but living in Changhua; taxes go to Taipei, but the population is registered as Changhua. **This is a county that is always "just short."**

## The Gray-faced Buzzard of Baguashan

If you want to know how a declining agricultural giant coexists with nature, go to Mount Baguashan.

Around Qingming Festival (March or April) every year, the Gray-faced Buzzards (colloquially known as "Nanlu Hawks," "Tomb Sweeping Birds," or "Qingming Birds") migrate through Baguashan. The migratory numbers can reach tens of thousands, creating a magnificent spectacle in flight. On March 22–23, 2025, Changhua County held the "Eagle Soaring Baguashan" bird-watching event[^24].

These raptors fly from the Philippines and Indonesia back to Northeast Asia for breeding; Baguashan is a resting stop on their western corridor. Beneath the 22-meter Buddha, tens of thousands of Gray-faced Buzzards draw arcs in the sky every spring. The statue was completed in 1961, while the buzzard migration route predates the statue by tens of thousands of years. Yet, the image of these two—the concrete Bodhisattva and the raptor—is a unique spring landscape of Changhua County.

Returning to the opening scene.

The chrysanthemum fields of Tianwei light up again. The artificial lighting of one hectare after another turns the entire plain into a night city. In the industrial zone of Taichung, 150 kilometers away, people are stuck in traffic after work; chicken leg lunch boxes at Yinlin Station are still being sold; Lukang Old Street closes at 9:30 PM; and the turntable of the Fanxing Roundhouse must turn diesel locomotives tomorrow.

Xie Bingying wrote about the significance of rain to the people of Keelung in _Rain Port Keelung_. For the people of Keelung, rain is part of their body. The relationship that the people of Changhua have with "water" is different; water is the lifeblood of agriculture, the legend of Baobaozhen from 1709, the 21,400 hectares irrigated by the Luochuan River, and the drip irrigation system in Tianwei chrysanthemum fields at night. Without water, there is no identity as an agricultural giant for Changhua.

The relationship this county has with the nation depends on where you look. From Taipei's perspective, Changhua is a collection of three tourist keywords: "Lukang Old Street," "Baguashan Great Buddha," and "Yinlin Chicken Leg Lunch Box." From Taichung's perspective, Changhua is a "suburban residential area" and a "source of cheap labor." From the perspective of the people of Lukang in 1986, Changhua was a place where they could say "No." Looking at this number—1.206 million, which is about to fall below 1.2 million—from 2026, Changhua is a "**county that had an established city in 1723 and is still struggling with how to retain people in 2026**."

Next time you visit Changhua, do not just tour Lukang Old Street. Go to Tianwei, preferably after 8 PM. Watch the chrysanthemum fields light up one hectare after another. Then you will remember one thing: Changhua is the physical structure of the Western Taiwan Corridor; Lukang is merely a label from a specific era within it.
