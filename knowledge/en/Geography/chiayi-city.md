# Chiayi City: Bestowed Its Name by an Emperor, Yet the Most Easily Overlooked Provincial City

> **30-second overview:** In 1787, the Qing Qianlong Emperor issued an edict changing "Zhuluo" to "Chiayi," taking the meaning "praising their loyal righteousness in defending the city to the death" — this is the only city in Taiwan personally named by an emperor. In 1908, the Japanese erected the world's first Tropic of Cancer marker tower in a rice paddy 3.3 km southwest of this city. In 1931, the Chiayi Agriculture and Forestry team — a ragtag squad of Han, Indigenous, and Japanese players — advanced to the runner-up position at Koshien. On March 25, 1947, painter Chen Cheng-po was bound and executed in front of the train station; his body was left in the street for three days. Today Chiayi City has 260,000 people, 60 square kilometers, no HSR station — it is the archetype of a Taiwan mid-sized city. Four national-level stories all unfolded within these 60 square kilometers.

## Four Hundred Years of the Central Fountain

If you take the train to Chiayi, exit the station and walk east along Zhongshan Road for about ten minutes, you'll see it. The Central Seven-Color Fountain sits where four roads—Wenhua Road, Zhongshan Road, Gongming Road, and Guanghua Road—intersect to form a traffic circle. Built during the mayoralty of Hsu Shih-hsien in the 1970s, the fountain changes its lights every few minutes, with its highest water jet reaching 20 meters[^1].

Its liveliest moment comes on the night before an election. Each candidate brings their supporters to circle the traffic circle in campaign motorcades, the sound of gong-and-drum troupes, campaign trucks, and microphones drowning each other out. Other cities hold their election rallies in stadiums or plazas, but Chiayi residents choose a traffic circle—because this circle has been the center of their city for three hundred years.

During the Qing era, this spot was called _Tau-á-bué_ (Peach Tail), the end of the Chiayi city wall. In 1704 (the 43rd year of the Kangxi reign), Magistrate Sung Yung-ching built a wooden stockade here to establish Tirosen (Zhuqian) City, with gates in the four cardinal directions—"the earliest city stockade built among the one prefecture and three counties at the time"[^2]. During the Japanese colonial period, the 1906 earthquake nearly destroyed the old city. The Japanese carried out urban replotting the following year, straightening streets, creating right-angled intersections, and planning a circular plaza. Today's Central Fountain stands at the center of that former circular plaza[^3].

Beneath the fountain's water jets lie three layers of history: the city walls of Han Chinese settlers, the Japanese urban replotting, and the post-war Taiwanese civic plaza. Chiayi residents rarely speak of these layers explicitly, but every time they walk around this traffic circle, the ground beneath their feet spans those three hundred years.

## Two Months of Defending the City Earned a Name

What truly etched this city into history was the 1786 siege.

That year (the 51st year of the Qianlong reign), in the eleventh month, Lin Shuang-wen rose in rebellion, and rebel forces from north and south converged to besiege Zhuqiao City. The city's population — a mixed settlement of Zhangzhou and Quanzhou immigrants — transformed into a fortress defended by several thousand people working as one. During the siege, food supplies were cut off; Lien Heng's _General History of Taiwan_ records the hardship: "**The siege of Zhuqiao grew ever tighter; with no food to be had, they dug up tree roots and boiled bean pulp to stave off hunger, yet their resolve to hold only hardened**"[^4].

Two months later, Qing imperial reinforcements lifted the siege. Qianlong deemed the city's defense extraordinarily tenacious and, in an unprecedented move, issued an edict bestowing a new name. The Chiayi City Government's official history website quotes the edict verbatim: "**The Qing dynasty, taking as its meaning 'commending their loyal righteousness in defending the city to the death,' on the third day of the eleventh month of the following year issued an edict changing 'Zhuqiao' to 'Chiayi'**"[^5].

This is the only county seat in all of Taiwan personally named by an emperor. The four characters "嘉其忠義" (commending their loyal righteousness) define a city. Most other county and city names derive from Indigenous transliterations, geographic features, or administrative designations (Keelung from "foundation prosperity," Changhua from "manifest imperial transformation"); Chiayi alone bears a name that is itself a condensation of political history.

From that moment, Chiayi acquired a dual identity. It was a city the Qing court thought worthy of an imperial edict, yet its historical zenith also halted here.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Open any county or city history, and the name behind it is usually a geographic or political label. But "Chiayi" is an edict — the Qing court's posthumous commendation of a group of city-defending people. The problem: a city singled out for imperial praise does not necessarily remain remembered by history. More than two centuries after Qianlong's renaming, when Taiwanese hear "Chiayi," the first associations are often turkey rice, the Alishan gateway, or "my brother brings back two boxes of pineapple cakes when he returns from Taipei." The four characters "嘉其忠義" have long been worn smooth by time, but the city has never taken them down. The emperor's bestowed name still hangs above the gate; only the people inside have changed, batch after batch.

## The Tropic of Cancer Grows Out of the Rice Paddies

In April 1908 (Meiji 41), to celebrate the completion of Taiwan's north-south trunk railway, the Japanese erected a large stone tower in a rice paddy 3.3 kilometers southwest of Chiayi City. The National Cultural Memory Bank entry states it directly: "This is the first-generation Tropic of Cancer landmark, and also the world's first erected Tropic of Cancer marker tower."[^6]

This fact carries two layers of significance. First: the Tropic of Cancer is a physical coordinate produced by Earth's 23.5-degree axial tilt; it exists at every longitude, but before 1908 no country considered this invisible line worth commemorating with a monument. By choosing to do this as part of Taiwan's railway completion celebration, the Japanese treated "modern geographical science" as a symbol of colonial modernization. Second: Chiayi thereby became "the starting point of southern Taiwan." South of the Tropic of Cancer is tropical; north is subtropical. Chiayi is the nearest city to this dividing line.

This marker tower has spent over a hundred years in the hands of Chiayi people. The first generation was blown down by a typhoon in 1912; the second was hastily rebuilt with bamboo and wood in 1915; the third was completed in 1926, prompted by Crown Prince Hirohito's 1923 visit to Taiwan when he passed through Shuishang, saw the crude bamboo-and-wood tower, and ordered its reconstruction on the spot[^7]. The fourth was rebuilt circa mid-1930s, later destroyed by the 1941 Caoling earthquake; the fifth was built in 1942, renovated into a small park by the Chiayi Air Force in 1968; the sixth was completed in 1995, later renovated into today's Tropic of Cancer Solar Pavilion[^8].

Chiayi people's attitude toward this marker tower is quite special. It lies outside the city, strictly speaking belonging to Shuishang Township in Chiayi County, yet every generation of Chiayi people takes out-of-town friends to see it. The tower's form has kept changing (from stone tower to steel frame to modern architecture), but its location hasn't moved in a hundred years. Typhoons can knock it down and it can be rebuilt; earthquakes can destroy it and it can rise again. The point is that this line at 23 degrees 27 minutes north latitude continues to pass through Taiwan, and the world's first city to commemorate this fact is right here.

## Alishan Turned This City into a Timber Capital

What truly made Chiayi prosper in the 20th century was Alishan's timber.

In 1899, the Japanese discovered Alishan's rich virgin cypress forests and began railway planning. In 1906, the private Fujita-gumi undertook construction, and in 1910 it was nationalized. **In December 1912**, the forest railway from Chiayi to Erwanping opened, spanning 66.6 kilometers, and was extended to Alishan in 1914[^9]. That same year (1914), the Chiayi Lumber Mill began operations; it was the largest state-run timber industrial park by land area during the Japanese colonial period. **"Employing the most advanced equipment from Europe and America, with an almost fully automated process, it earned the reputation of the 'Number One Lumber Mill in the Orient.'"**[^10]

The quality of Alishan cypress was so high its impact extended beyond Taiwan: "These premium building materials were used to construct the great torii of Meiji Shrine and repair the Buddha Hall of the national treasure Horyu-ji."[^11] Every red cypress log brought down from Alishan was first sent to the Chiayi Lumber Mill for cutting, then shipped to Japan. Chiayi became the distribution hub for Alishan's timber. In 1935, this city of 70,000 people **"had one-tenth of its population engaged in the timber industry, and was the fifth most populous bustling city in Taiwan at the time."**[^11]

Beimen Station, the starting point of the Alishan Railway, **"was once the largest timber trading market in all of Taiwan."**[^12] Today, walking into Beimen Station, the wooden station's exterior still looks as it did in 1912. Although a fire in 1998 burned down half the building, the Chiayi Forest Management Office restored it that same year. The station structure was built using Alishan's own red cypress; the wood may be over a thousand years old. Using thousand-year-old trees to build a station, then using that station to transport even more thousand-year-old trees down the mountain — this was the cycle of the Alishan forestry industry during the Japanese colonial period.

The timber capital era, counted from the 1914 opening of the Alishan Lumber Mill to the 1963 government halt on large-scale logging at Alishan, lasted exactly 50 years[^11]. In the late 1960s, the Alishan forestry industry gradually declined; loggers, mill workers, and transport workers around the forest farms changed careers one after another. But timber never completely left Chiayi — it became the city's material heritage. Smile Taiwan records that Chiayi City today **"still retains over 6,000 wooden houses, the highest density in the country."**[^11] Each one is a material relic of the "timber capital era." The city government's "Timber Capital Revival" brand in recent years is built upon these 6,000 old wooden houses.

> **✦** **"These premium building materials were used to construct the great torii of Meiji Shrine and repair the Buddha Hall of the national treasure Horyu-ji."** (Smile Taiwan "Chiayi Timber Capital" series)[^11]

Alishan's timber built Tokyo's great torii and repaired Nara's Horyu-ji. Chiayi people don't talk much about this, but if you walk past the "Alishan Forest Railway and Japan's Kurobe Gorge Railway Sister Railway Commemorative Stone" beside Beimen Station, a line of text will remind you: this city was once a key node in the Japanese Empire's timber supply chain.

## Koshien's Ragtag Team

In the summer of 1931, Chiayi produced a baseball team.

Chiayi Agricultural and Forestry School (Chiayi Nōrin, KANO) participated in the Japanese National High School Baseball Championship (Summer Koshien) for the first time as **Taiwan's representative**. Most unusual was the team's composition: **a mix of three ethnic groups—Japanese, Han Chinese, and Taiwan Indigenous peoples**—which was unprecedented at a time when Taiwan's baseball teams were dominated by Japanese from the north. Coach Kondo Hyotaro came from Matsuyama Commercial School in Ehime Prefecture, a Koshien powerhouse. The Taiwan Baseball Wiki notes that he only **"began to show his prominence"** after arriving at Chiayi Nōrin[^13].

On the day of the final, August 21, Chiayi Nōrin's pitcher Wu Ming-chieh, who had pitched four consecutive games, was already exhausted. The Taiwan Baseball Wiki preserves a verbatim record of this game: **"In the final on August 21, Chiayi Nōrin's pitcher Wu Ming-chieh, who had pitched four consecutive games... the team ultimately lost 0-4 to Chukyo Commercial School from Aichi Prefecture, finishing as runners-up"**[^13].

Their opponents, Chukyo Commercial, later became the only school in Koshien history to win three consecutive championships (1931-1933), and to this day remains the high school with the most victories at Koshien. That summer, Chiayi Nōrin lost by a margin of four runs to what would become Koshien's strongest school.

The historical significance of this game lay not in victory or defeat. It was the earliest concrete example of three-ethnic cooperation under the framework of "colonial modernity." **A single baseball game** showed the world that Han Chinese, Indigenous peoples, and Japanese could fight side by side on the same field—something that official propaganda at the time could never have scripted. Wei Te-sheng's 2014 film _KANO_ brought this legend back into 21st-century Taiwan's collective memory, but the story of Chiayi Nōrin has always been passed down by word of mouth among Chiayi locals. The Chiayi Nōrin school still exists today (now upgraded to National Chiayi University), and the baseball field next to the original campus site is now the KANO Park.

## Three Days of Corpses on Chiayi's Streets

In March 1947, another story unfolded in Chiayi.

After the 228 Incident erupted across Taiwan, on March 2 dozens of young men who had come south from Changhua and Taichung "rallied citizens between the train station and the fountain"[^14] — the very central fountain mentioned at the start of this article. The crowd besieged the official residence of Mayor Sun Chih-chun and the police station. On March 5, Chiayi City Councilor and Youth Corps Secretary Lu Ho-ch'in went up to Alishan to ask Tsou youths to come down and help maintain order; Tsou Township Chief Kao I-sheng (indigenous name Uyongu Yatauyungana) dispatched Tang Shou-jen to lead indigenous youths down the mountain to garrison at Chiayi's Falung Temple. That same day, militia besieged Shuishang Airport and the Hongmaopi Arsenal, resulting in approximately 300 casualties[^14].

During the airport siege, both sides negotiated multiple times. On March 8–9, the Chiayi side sent negotiation representatives to the airport; the representatives were detained, and only three female committee members were released. On March 11, "a battalion of the 430th Regiment, 21st Army Division arrived at the airport; southern reinforcements reached Chiayi"[^14]. On the 18th of the same month, Chen Fu-chih, chair of the Chiayi Branch of the 228 Settlement Committee, was "paraded through the streets and then executed by firing squad in front of Chiayi Train Station"[^14].

Seven days later, on March 25, four Chiayi city councilors were taken to the station forecourt: Chen Cheng-po (painter, the first Taiwanese to have a Western-style painting accepted into the Japan Imperial Art Exhibition in 1926), Pan Mu-chih (physician educated in Japan), Ko Lin (owner of Ching-sheng Theater), and Lu Ho-ch'in (dentist). The _Chiayi 228 Photo Special_ records: "On March 25, four people — Chen Cheng-po, Pan Mu-chih, Ko Lin, and Lu Ho-ch'in — were executed by firing squad"[^14].

Chen Cheng-po was 52 when he died (born 1905/02/02, executed 1947/03/25, actual age; the Chen Cheng-po Cultural Foundation chronology lists "aged 53" using the traditional East Asian age reckoning[^15]). An English report in _Taiwan Gazette_ notes a detail: "The Kuomintang forbade families from collecting the corpses immediately, so Chen's remains were left to decompose on the street for three days"[^16]. The KMT prohibited families from retrieving the bodies immediately; Chen Cheng-po's remains lay on the streets of Chiayi for three days.

In front of the very same plaza where this city's "first steel-framed reinforced concrete station on the Western Trunk Line," touted as "the most modern reinforced concrete train station on the entire island," completed in 1933[^17], stood the corpses of Taiwanese elites for three days in 1947. A city's modernization and its political violence, compressed into a single square.

The fate of the Chiayi School of Painting is bound to Chen Cheng-po's death. Since the mid-Japanese era, Chiayi has been known as the "City of Painting"; in the first Taiwan Viceroy Art Exhibition (Futen) in 1938, "Chiayi painters accounted for twenty percent of those accepted," and the _Taiwan Nichinichi Shinpo_ ran the headline "Chiayi Is the City of Painting; Accepted Works Account for Two-Tenths"[^18]. Lin Yu-shan (1907–2004, original name Ying-kuei, born on Mei Street in Chiayi City) had _Water Buffalo_ and _Great South Gate_ accepted into the first Taiwan Art Exhibition (Taiten) in 1927, and together with Chen Chin and Kuo Hsueh-hu was known as the "Three Youths of the Taiten"[^18]. From the Chun-meng Painting Society (1928), the Chiayi Calligraphy and Painting Self-Improvement Society (1931), to the Mo-yang Society (1934), the Chiayi School grew into the city with the highest acceptance rate into the Imperial Art Exhibition (Teiten) across Taiwan.

After Chen Cheng-po's death, the story of the City of Painting continued. In **October 2020**, the Chiayi Municipal Art Museum opened on Guangning Street in the West District beside Chiayi Station. Its predecessor was the "Tobacco and Alcohol Monopoly Bureau Chiayi Branch," completed in 1936, a city-designated historic site designed by Japanese architect Umezawa Sukejiro[^19]. Architectural clusters from three eras — 1936, 1954, and 1980 — were fused into new life; the address number commemorates the generation of painters including Lin Yu-shan, Chen Cheng-po, and Chang Li-te-ho.

## U.S. Aid Chicken and the HSR Station "Not in Chiayi City"

Outsiders' quickest impression of Chiayi is turkey rice, but this dish actually only appeared after the war, and the first bite wasn't turkey.

The Chiayi Tourism Bureau's official website states clearly: "According to oral accounts from Chiayi elders, it originated as early as 1949 (the 38th year of the Republic), when Lin Tien-shou, a veteran shopkeeper at 'First Market,' had a sudden inspiration to slice the chicken meat (which was broiler chicken at the time) bought for worship, place it on white rice, pour braised sauce over it to make chicken rice, found the texture unique, and formally began selling chicken rice on Zhongshan Road"[^20].

When Lin Tien-shou opened his shop in 1949, the plate held broiler chicken, not turkey. Turkey had to wait for the U.S. aid era to arrive in Chiayi. The Chiayi Tourism Bureau also records: "Taiwan originally had no turkey farming; after WWII ended, many U.S. troops stationed in Taiwan brought large quantities of turkey into Chiayi City and Shuishang Township, Chiayi County, opening the source of the delicious 'turkey rice'"[^20]. During the 1951–1965 U.S. aid period, Taiwan imported white-feathered turkeys from the United States, replacing the original black-feathered turkeys, driving a dual increase in farming volume and quality. Turkeys were large, cheaper than domestic chickens, and highly nutritious; in the postwar era of material scarcity, they gradually replaced the broiler chicken in Lin Tien-shou's shop, becoming Chiayi's unique affordable protein.

Evolving from "broiler chicken shredded rice" to "turkey rice" took a curve of over a decade. StoryStudio has organized this history: "Turkey rice didn't appear out of thin air; its predecessor was 'chicken shredded rice.' In the early postwar period, due to inflation and soaring prices, material conditions in Taiwan society were generally poor; chicken was an ingredient many families only had a chance to eat during festivals"[^21]. Lin Tien-shou's "Fountain Chicken Rice," started with broiler chicken in 1949, was located beside the central fountain roundabout on Zhongshan Road, named for its geographic location, and later became the representative old shop of Chiayi chicken rice, the earliest corporatized chain brand of chicken rice in Taiwan.

Outsiders coming to Chiayi for turkey rice have two main routes: Liu Lichang Chicken Rice (West District), Ah Hong Shi (East District), Simple Chicken Rice (East District) — these are the tourist queue shops; locals each have their own stalls. The area around the Seven-Color Fountain is often where tourist density is highest; locals detour to small shops on Xinyi Road and Wenhua Road near Beimen Station. The ratio of four elements — turkey, rice, braised sauce, and fried shallots — is the criterion for judgment. The sauce recipe (ratio of minced meat in the braised sauce, frying method for the shallots) forms each shop's fingerprint[^21].

After eating, outsiders wanting to take the HSR back north discover another thing: Chiayi City has no HSR station.

The HSR Chiayi Station is located in Taibao City, Chiayi County, not in Chiayi City proper. This is a rare structure in Taiwan's administrative divisions: Chiayi City is completely surrounded by Chiayi County. After Chiayi City separated from Chiayi County and was upgraded to a provincial city in 1982, the Chiayi County Council voted in 1982 to move the county seat to the Dongshiliao Farm in Taibao Township. "Coastal-line legislators temporarily proposed the Taibao Township addition proposal," passing with 27 votes, exceeding a majority[^22]. In 1991, the Chiayi County Government officially moved to the "Xianghe New Village" county administrative special zone in Taibao; Taibao Township was upgraded to Taibao City[^22].

Then-Chiayi County Magistrate Tu Te-chi had a comment on the outcome of the county-city separation: "Originally a modestly well-off household, now become two poor households"[^22]. Chiayi City's population was 250,000 at the time of upgrade, and 260,000 in 2026; "its population and industrial scale can be said to have remained unchanged for decades"[^22]. Over the intervening 30 years, the city's peak population reached 274,212 in April 2009, falling to 261,626 in April 2026, a decline of about 12,600 people over 17 years[^22].

When the HSR opened in 2007, Chiayi Station was built in Taibao. About 10 km in a straight line from Chiayi City center, with no rail system (TRA or MRT) connecting them, passengers must transfer to BRT or taxi to reach Chiayi City, with the BRT ride taking about 20 to 40 minutes[^33]. Seventeen years later, as of 2026 there is still no improvement. This is a case of the "nearest provincial capital" absent from the most important transportation hub within its own county boundaries. A city named by an emperor, and the 21st century's most advanced transportation system, are separated by a single BRT line.

## The Roundabout of Peach City, Three Hundred Years in the Same Place

Return to the central fountain from the opening.

In the Qing era it was the end of the city wall of Thô-á-bué (Taoziwei). In the Japanese colonial period it became a circular plaza from urban replotting. In the 1970s it turned into Mayor Hsu Shih-hsien's seven-color fountain. Every decade or so it gets a new look, but the roundabout's position has never moved.

Walk west along Zhongshan Road and you reach the Chiayi Railway Station designed by Ufu Takeo in 1933, the square where Chen Cheng-po was executed by firing squad in 1947; a little farther on Guangning Street stands the Tobacco and Alcohol Monopoly Bureau built in 1936, today's Chiayi Municipal Art Museum.

To the south lies the Wenhua Road Night Market, stretching from the roundabout all the way to Wenhua Park on Chuiyang Road, five hundred meters.

Head toward Gonghe Road in the East District and you find Beimen Station, the starting point of the Alishan Forest Railway, and the urban fabric that grew along the line after it opened in 1912.

Continue east to the end of Zhongshan Road and you arrive at Chiayi Park and the Municipal Baseball Stadium.

A few roads, a few segments of history, all taking this roundabout as their origin.

People of Chiayi don't particularly emphasize this. They know Taipei can't see Chiayi (every time Chiayi is mentioned, Taipei people think of a transfer station, the HSR stop where they never get off), but they don't really need Taipei to see them.

They have the moment the Tropic of Cancer marker rose from the rice paddies, the thousand-year-old red cypress from Alishan that became the grand torii of Tokyo's Meiji Shrine, the ragtag team of players of three ethnicities that reached Koshien in 1931, the square where no one could collect the bodies for three days in 1947, that bowl of rice that evolved from chicken to turkey in 1949, six thousand historic wooden houses still standing.

These don't need the "most easily overlooked provincial city" frame as a foil. They don't need Taipei to validate them.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Chiayi's location is special. It sits south of center on the island of Taiwan, at 23°27′N; the world's first Tropic of Cancer marker tower stands just 3.3 km outside this city. Geographically, it is the physical coordinate of Taiwan's "north-south divide" — subtropical north of the Tropic of Cancer, tropical to the south. But the identity of Chiayi people has never been "south" or "north." They are "Peach City people." In the Qing era, Zhuqian City was shaped like a peach, and this nickname persists today. When outsiders argue over whether Chiayi counts as southern or central Taiwan, Chiayi people answer "Peach City." The emperor's bestowal of "Chiayi" is official memory; "Peach City" is the citizens' daily life. A city's name actually has two layers: the one for history is called Chiayi, the one for themselves is called Peach City.

Next time you go to Chiayi, don't rush to eat turkey rice and hop on the HSR. Walk out of the train station, first circle the central fountain once. Watch the fountain's water jets switch between colors, count how many exits this roundabout has and which segment of history each exit leads to (Station 1933, Art Museum 1936, Wenhua Road Night Market, Alishan Railway). Then you'll remember one thing: Taiwan isn't only north and south. In the middle of Taiwan there's a city personally named by an emperor, and it has stood beside this roundabout for three hundred years.

From the moment Qianlong gave her the name "Chiayi," she has never left her position.
