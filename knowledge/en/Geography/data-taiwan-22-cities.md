# Viewing Taiwan's 22 Counties and Cities Through Data: The Densest and Sparsest Differ by 151 Times, Elderly-to-Children Ratio Shifts from 1:1 to Nearly 3:1

Drive from Taipei's Xinyi District, head south, then east, until you reach Taitung City. Navigation shows 300-plus kilometers, less than a day's drive. But if you watch a different gauge — how many people live in each square kilometer — that journey feels like crossing two countries. Taipei City, where Xinyi sits, crams 8,975 people into every square kilometer. Taitung County, where Taitung City sits, has just 59 per square kilometer. Same island, same passport, density differs by a full 151 times.

This number isn't a cherry-picked extreme. It's Taiwan's real internal gap. We're used to talking about "Taiwan" as a monolith — its economy, its elections, its low fertility — but when you spread out the Ministry of the Interior's complete 2025 year-end county-level data, you realize there is no homogeneous Taiwan. Some people queue at red lights in concrete jungles; others drive ten minutes without seeing a second car. Some jurisdictions are still growing; most are shrinking. In the youngest corners, elderly and children are roughly equal. In the oldest, one child faces nearly three elders.


This article aims to do one thing: use official data to draw a scaled, verifiable portrait of the whole island. When finished, you'll see a face that is severely polarized and rapidly aging.[^1]

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's total population at 2025 year-end stood at 23,299,132 (approx. 23.3 million), declining for 23 consecutive months, with newborns falling below 110,000 for the first time. That same year Taiwan officially entered "super-aged society" status — one in five people over 65. But the national average masks massive internal gaps: population density between highest and lowest jurisdictions differs by 151 times, population scale by 297 times, elderly-to-children ratio stretches from 1:1 to nearly 3:1. People concentrate in the six special municipalities, yet aging doesn't follow population density: the highest elderly share is in the densest Taipei City, the highest elderly-to-children ratio is in Chiayi County, and the youngest places are Hsinchu County and City propped up by the Hsinchu Science Park. This is a Taiwan that is polarized, and aging together.[^2]

## Seven-Tenths of People, Three-Tenths of Land

First, an easily overlooked fact: Taiwan's people actually live extremely concentrated.

At 2025 year-end, the six special municipalities (Taipei, New Taipei, Taoyuan, Taichung, Tainan, Kaohsiung) together housed 16,278,931 people, 69.87% of the national total. In other words, nearly seven out of ten Taiwan residents live in the six special municipalities. Yet these six municipalities' combined land area accounts for only 30.12% of Taiwan's total. Seven-tenths of people, squeezed into three-tenths of land. The remaining three-tenths of people scatter across the other seven-tenths of land. This is the first structural feature of Taiwan's population distribution.

```tw-stat
23.3 million | Total national population | 2025 year-end, declining for 23 consecutive months
20.06% | Share of population aged 65+ | 2025 officially crossed super-aged threshold
69.87% | Six special municipalities' share of national population | Yet living on only 30% of land
Source: Ministry of the Interior Department of Household Registration population statistics (2025 year-end total population, 65+ share, six special municipalities population share) and special municipality reorganization data[^1][^4]
```

This 70-30 pattern didn't form naturally; it has a clear institutional starting point. On December 25, 2010, Taiwan rewrote its administrative map in one stroke: Taipei County upgraded to New Taipei City, Taichung County and City merged, Tainan County and City merged, Kaohsiung County and City merged, joining the existing Taipei City — five special municipalities born simultaneously. Four years later, on December 25, 2014, Taoyuan upgraded to become the sixth. In just four years, Taiwan went from two special municipalities to six, and the balance of resources, budgets, and construction tilted accordingly.[^4]

Upgrading was never just a name change. Special municipalities receive far higher general grants, personnel quotas, and autonomous financial resources than ordinary counties and cities. Where construction goes, job opportunities grow, and young people follow. The institution drew a line first; population flowed along it afterward. Today's six-municipality concentration is, to some extent, the fruit borne a dozen-plus years after that 2010 reorganization.

The chart below breaks down "where Taiwan's people live" even more clearly. New Taipei City alone holds 17.4% of the national population, Taichung 12.3%, Kaohsiung 11.7%, Taipei 10.5%, Taoyuan 10.1%, Tainan 7.9% — the six special municipalities sum to nearly seven-tenths. The other 16 counties and cities scattered across Taiwan, from north to the outlying islands, together account for just 30.1%.


Stare at this waffle chart a bit longer and a question emerges: what kind of life do those 30% scattered across 70% of the land live? The answer hides in density.

## From Most Crowded to Most Empty, a 151-Fold Gap

Population concentration speaks to "how many people"; density speaks to "how crowded." The latter lets you feel the distance between the two Taiwans more viscerally.

Taipei City has 8,975 people per km², the most crowded place in Taiwan. Taipei's crowding is daily life: MRT packed at rush hour, waiting through two light cycles at intersections, buildings tall and narrow. Next is Hsinchu City at 4,376 per km², not even half of Taipei. Then Keelung City at 2,710. Just among these three most crowded cities, density already drops off a cliff.

```tw-bars
Taipei City | 8,975 people/km²
Hsinchu City | 4,376 people/km²
Keelung City | 2,710 people/km²
Changhua County | 1,126 people/km²
Hualien County | 68 people/km²
Taitung County | 59 people/km² | Sparsest in Taiwan
Source: Ministry of the Interior Department of Household Registration, 2025 year-end
```

Shift your gaze to the other end of this bar chart, and the gap truly materializes. Changhua County at 1,126 per km² is already relatively dense for an agricultural county. By [Hualien County](/en/geography/hualien-county/), the number plunges to 68. At the very bottom, Taitung County has just 59 people per km². The people Taipei fits in one square kilometer would take 151 square kilometers to fill in Taitung. This is the true face of the 151-fold gap — you drive from Xinyi District all the way to Taitung, watching the population thin out outside your window.

Behind this lies a geographic fact. Taiwan's largest county by area is Hualien County, 4,628 km², covering nearly the entire eastern flank of the Central Mountain Range. Large area, few people, many mountains — Hualien and Taitung's densities are naturally diluted to the national lowest. The smallest, [Lienchiang County](/en/geography/lienchiang-county/) (Matsu), is only 28.8 km². Density is half human choice, half terrain destiny.

Conventional wisdom often equates "high population density" with progress and "low density" with backwardness, but this causality is actually reversed. Taitung's low density isn't because it failed to develop; it's because it backs onto great mountains and faces the Pacific — inherently a geographically vast, sparsely populated condition. Hualien's "emptiness" contains Taroko, the Coastal Mountain Range, Taiwan's most intact hinterland landscapes. Density numbers only tell you how crowded people are; they don't tell you the value of that land. Line up all 22 counties and cities from crowded to empty, and you see an island growing different ways of life on different terrains.

## Giants and Dust

If density compares "how crowded," population scale compares "how big." The gap on this dimension is even more staggering.

Taiwan's most populous is New Taipei City, 4,044,831 people — a single city rivaling many countries. The least populous is Lienchiang County, aka Matsu, just 13,621 people. New Taipei's population is 297 times Lienchiang's. Move all of Lienchiang's people into New Taipei and they wouldn't even register as a rounding error. Same Republic of China passport, but one county's "whole" is but a speck of dust in another's eye.

This scale gap directly becomes a governance challenge. New Taipei must handle transport, housing, long-term care, and waste for four million; Lienchiang must figure out how to stop its 13,000-person outlying island from continuing to lose population, how to maintain a single hospital, how to keep the last ferry running. The same laws, the same central policies, applied across a 297-fold population gap, produce completely different realities on the ground. When we talk "local government," we mentally default to urban scale, but a fair number of Taiwan's counties and cities operate an entire government machinery at speck-of-dust scale.

But beware an intuitive trap: small scale doesn't mean young population. You might assume outlying islands and small counties, losing young people and left with elders, are both small and old; and that big cities, with resources and opportunities, are both big and young. Real data overturns both assumptions. Lienchiang County has the smallest population, yet its aging rate is 17.14%, actually lower than several large counties; meanwhile Taipei City, fourth most populous and prosperous beyond measure, has the highest aging rate in Taiwan at 24.18%. Between scale and aging, there is simply no clean correlation. To understand how Taiwan ages, you need a different map.

## The Two Oldest Places: Taipei City and Chiayi County

In 2025, Taiwan crossed a threshold: national 65+ population share reached 20.06%, officially becoming a "super-aged society" per WHO definition. That translates to 4.67 million people over 65 — one in five Taiwan residents is an elder. And among the 22 counties and cities, 14 already have aging rates above 20%. Old has become the shared base color of the whole island.

But this national 20.06% average flattens violent internal differences entirely. The table below lays out all 22 jurisdictions' aging rates and aging indices side by side — the one table in this article most worth pausing to study. First, how to read "aging index": it equals 65+ population divided by 0–14 population times 100. 100 means equal numbers of elders and children; higher means increasingly "top-heavy" — more elders above, fewer children below.


By 65+ share, the oldest nationally is Taipei City at 24.18%, with Chiayi County at 24.11% just 0.07 percentage points behind. By aging index, first place becomes Chiayi County at 291.69 — nearly three elders per child — followed by Kinmen County at 255.57, Keelung City at 240.21, while Taipei City at 202.06 drops to eighth. The two columns differ in denominator: aging index compares elders to children; Taipei's elderly share is high but its child share is also higher than Chiayi County's and Kinmen County's, pulling its index down.

The five jurisdictions ranking in the top seven on both columns are Chiayi County, Nantou County, Yunlin County, Pingtung County — four agricultural counties — plus Keelung City, an old port city that lost its industries. All five are losing population: young people leave for work and study, those left behind slowly age, newborns can't replenish, and both columns' numbers surge together.

So where is Taiwan's youngest corner? The answer surprises many: not in some utopian hideaway, but beside tech factories. Hsinchu County's aging rate is only 15.08%, the lowest in Taiwan; aging index 101.88, almost perfectly balanced 1:1 between elders and children, standing out starkly in an island of aging. Hsinchu City at 16.16% and Taoyuan City at 16.72% follow close behind. These three places' youth share almost a single cause: Hsinchu Science Park. Semiconductor and tech industries draw large numbers of engineers and technical staff in prime childbearing years; they settle, have children, and forcibly pull these jurisdictions' population structures toward the young end. The Science Park props up not just Taiwan's export figures, but Taiwan's youngest population map.

Place the youngest Hsinchu County against the highest-aging-index Chiayi County, and the gap becomes even more glaring.

```tw-versus
Hsinchu County (Youngest in Taiwan) | Chiayi County (Highest Aging Index in Taiwan)
65+ Share 15.08% | 65+ Share 24.11%
Aging Index 101.88 | Aging Index 291.69
Population Still Growing | Population Continuously Shrinking
Propped Up by Science Park | Agricultural County, Newborns Can't Keep Up
Source: Ministry of the Interior Department of Household Registration, 2025 year-end
```

Hsinchu County 15.08% vs. Chiayi County 24.11% — aging rate differs by 9 percentage points; aging index 101.88 vs. 291.69 — nearly three times apart. One still growing, population flowing in; the other continuously shrinking, population flowing out. Two counties on the same island: one where elders and children are roughly equal, another where every child has nearly three elders behind them. In Hsinchu's parks you might see young parents pushing strollers; in Chiayi's countryside, entire streets of slow-walking elders. These two Taiwans are both calculated from the Household Registration data.

Many think aging is a rural and outlying-island specialty — big cities have young people and opportunities, so they should be immune. Taipei City's numbers say otherwise: this most prosperous, most resource-concentrated city in Taiwan has the highest elderly share nationally, and the highest aging index among the six special municipalities. The commonly cited explanation is housing prices: young families priced out to New Taipei and Taoyuan to marry and have children, leaving behind the generation that settled early and is now gradually aging. Urban prosperity doesn't block aging; it just ages in a different way. Aging is the shared condition of the whole island; the difference is only in timing and speed.

## One Island, Aging Together

Pull the lens from counties and cities to the national level, and Taiwan's aging story has another more alarming dimension: speed.

Taiwan didn't just start aging now. In 1993, national 65+ share exceeded 7%, entering "aging society" per international definition. In 2018, it reached 14.05%, entering "aged society." In 2025, it broke 20%, becoming "super-aged society." Three milestones look like just three years, but inside hides a slope getting steeper by the step.

```tw-timeline
1993 | Aging Society | 65+ exceeds 7%, Taiwan begins aging
2018 | Aged Society | 65+ reaches 14%, 25 years from previous threshold
2025 | Super-Aged Society | 65+ breaks 20%, this threshold took only 7 years
Source: Ministry of the Interior population statistics and international aging threshold definitions (1993 7%, 2018 14.05%, 2025 20%)[^1]
```

Look at the two intervals on this timeline. From 7% to 14%, Taiwan took 25 years; but from 14% to 20%, only 7 years. This latter segment took less than one-third the time of the former. Taiwan is aging, and accelerating. This is precipitous even globally — other countries had decades to slowly adjust long-term care, pensions, healthcare systems; Taiwan is compressed into a mere seven years, forced to build an entire elder-care system within a very short window.

```tw-line
National 65+ Population Share Rise (%)
Year | 65+ Share
2000 | 8.6
2010 | 10.7
2020 | 16.1
2025 | 20.06
Source: Ministry of the Interior, National Development Council
```

This rising polyline shows the acceleration more clearly. 2000 national 65+ share was 8.6%, 2010 10.7% — ten years for just over 2 percentage points; but 2010 to 2020, the figure surged from 10.7% to 16.1%, then to 20.06% in 2025. The line's tail is distinctly steeper than its head. Taiwan's aging curve is bending upward.

The flip side of aging is birth-rate collapse. In 2025, Taiwan's newborns fell below 110,000 for the first time — just 107,812. Low fertility and high aging are two sides of the same coin: more elders above, fewer children entering below, the whole population structure grows increasingly top-heavy. This is why those counties' and cities' aging indices are so high: elders multiplying above, children dwindling too far below.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> We easily understand "population decline" as "young people move from countryside to city, so countryside shrinks and city grows" — as if it's just an intra-island move. But 2025 data reveals something more fundamental: every single county and city in Taiwan has negative "natural increase." That is, in every Taiwan jurisdiction, without distinction between urban and rural, large or small, deaths exceed births. This is no longer about where people flow; it's about the whole island dying faster than it's born. Migration only redistributes an already-shrinking population; it creates no new people.

This fact deserves pause. In 2025, only four counties and cities still grew: Taoyuan, Hsinchu County, Taichung, Hsinchu City. And their growth came entirely from "social increase" — in-migration from elsewhere, absorbing other jurisdictions' people, not from their own births. Beyond these four, the other 18 all shrank. The steepest declines: Kinmen County, Lienchiang County, Taipei City — note, even the most prosperous Taipei City is on this loss list. When births can't cover deaths in every corner of the nation, those four still-growing jurisdictions are merely standing temporarily atop others' losses.

This is why total population has declined for 23 consecutive months, falling to 23,299,132. It's synchronous contraction across the whole island, every jurisdiction shrinking. The difference is only that some places use in-migration to temporarily prop up the scene, while others don't even have that buffer.

## The Portrait Data Paints

Return to that opening drive. From Taipei Xinyi to Taitung, people per km² drop from 8,975 to 59, like crossing two countries. Now you know that's not just a density gap. Along that route, population scale shifts from giant to dust. Aging doesn't follow density: the starting point Taipei has the highest elderly share nationally; the destination Taitung sits in the middle on both columns. The only constant: wherever you stop in any county or city, local deaths already exceed births.

This is Taiwan's 2025 portrait: a face highly polarized, yet aging together. Its internal gaps are staggering — density 151×, scale 297×, elderly-to-children ratio from 1:1 to nearly 3:1; yet its fundamental predicament is strangely uniform — the whole island dies faster than it's born, total population shrinking for 23 straight months, crashing from aged to super-aged society in seven years. The National Development Council's projection says, under medium variant, by 2070 Taiwan's total population will drop to 14.97 million, 65+ share reaching 46.5%; by 2028, the demographic dividend ends. This portrait won't improve on its own.[^3]

Acknowledging Taiwan's internal non-homogeneity is to see this island's true dilemma: a place with such vast internal differences must use one policy suite to simultaneously care for four million squeezed in a basin and 13,000 holding fast on an outlying island, while facing both the youngest Hsinchu County and the highest-elderly-share Taipei City and Chiayi County. It is polarized, yet must act as a whole; it is aging, yet must find solutions together. Data has drawn this face for us; how to respond to this face is the question this island must answer together.
