# Hsinchu: The Bamboo Fort of 1733, The Cradle Where TSMC Grew in 1980

> **30-Second Summary:** In the tenth year of Yongzheng (1733), the magistrate Xu Zhi-min planted _cangzhu_ bamboo around Zhuguan, enclosing a circular earth fort with a circumference of 440 zhang (approximately 1,408 meters) [^1]. The brick fort, started in 1828, was completed in the ninth year of Daoguang (1829), and the East Gate, Yingxi Men, still stands by the moat [^2]. 247 years later, on December 15, 1980, Chiang Ching-kuo cut the red ribbon for Taiwan's first science park, located 5 kilometers from Yingxi Men [^3]. Between June 15, 1953, and the end of December 1974, Black Bat Squadron carried out 838 special missions from the Hsinchu Air Base, resulting in 10 aircraft being shot down or crashing by accident, leading to the martyrdom of 148 personnel, which accounted for two-thirds of the squadron [^4]. The City God Temple was completed in the thirteenth year of Qianlong (1748), and in 1890, it received the imperial plaque "Jinmen Protection" from Emperor Guangxu, elevating the Hsinchu City God to the rank of "Wei Ling Gong Hsinchu Du Cheng Huang," making it the only temple in Taiwan to receive the title of "Du Cheng Huang" from the Qing court [^5]. This county seat, housing 450,000 people, supported the Cold War front line, was one of the earliest Han Chinese cities built in the North, hosted two top universities, and served as the starting point for the global semiconductor industry's 90% high-end chips [^6]. The nickname "Wind City" is literary, but all industries in this city are related to wind.

## Planting Bamboo in 1733 by the Tanwei Magistrate

The literal record from the Wikipedia entry on Zhuguan Fort states:

> **✦** "**When the Tanwei Sea Defense Office officially moved from Changhua County to Zhuguan in the tenth year of Yongzheng (1733), the magistrate Xu Zhi-min planted _cangzhu_ bamboo around it, forming a circular area with a circumference of 440 zhang (approximately 1,408 meters), featuring four gates—East, West, South, and North—marking the beginning of the construction of Zhuguan.**" [^1]

Note the wording: **planting _cangzhu_ bamboo**.

The raw material was _cangzhu_ bamboo, not bricks, stone, or rammed earth. The first-generation Zhuguan Fort was a green wall encircled by _cangzhu_ bamboo. This was not uncommon in Taiwan's history of city building (many county seats in the Qing Dynasty started as bamboo forts), but the name "Zhuguan" itself carries an odor of plants. It was later renamed "Hsinchu," retaining the character for "bamboo." The "bamboo" in Hsinchu Science Park, the "bamboo" at Tsinghua Campus, and the "bamboo" scent wafting from the Mifen Noodles stall all trace back to that circle of _cangzhu_ bamboo in 1733.

The Tanwei Office was not originally here. The Tanwei Office was separated from Zhulu County in the first year of Yongzheng (1723), and its initial seat was established in Shalu, Changhua [^7]. In the ninth year of Yongzheng (1731), the jurisdiction was officially defined as north of the Dajia Creek up to Jilong. It wasn't until 1733 that the Tanwei magistrate moved the office to Zhuguan [^7]. This means two things happened in the same year: the administrative center of Northern Taiwan was moved to this small town that had just grown _cangzhu_ bamboo, and the _cangzhu_ bamboo was planted. **The relocation and the city construction occurred in the same year.** From that day on, Zhuguan remained the administrative center of Northern Taiwan for a full 150 years.

96 years later, in the seventh year of Daoguang (1827), scholar Zheng Yong-xi and others proposed rebuilding with brick and stone. After approval, construction began in 1828, and the brick fort was completed in the autumn of 1829, measuring 860 zhang in circumference (approximately 2,752 meters) and 1 _zhang_ 5 _chi_ high (about 4.8 meters) [^2]. The four gates were named "Yingxi," "Yishuang," "Gaochun," and "Gongchen." The entire fort cost about 147,498 taels of silver.

_Yingxi Men, the East Gate of Hsinchu City. Photo: Jiang / Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hsinchu_East_Gate_(0421).\_

In the thirty-fifth year of Meiji (1902), the Japanese implemented urban street improvements in Hsinchu, demolishing the brick fort walls and the other three gate towers, leaving "only the East Gate Yingxi Men" [^2]. This remaining East Gate stood for 197 years until today, designated a National Historic Site in 1985. In 1999, underground structures of the moat were discovered during the beautification project of the East Gate Square; these stone piers are the only remnants of an ancient moat built with traditional methods currently visible in Taiwan.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The four characters "planting bamboo to build a city" might sound trivial to modern readers ("Oh, they just surrounded it with bamboo"), but in 1733 Taiwan, this was an **emergency construction plan that saved costs**. The Qing court did not want to spend money building a stone fort, nor were they afraid of indigenous people or pirates attacking the office. Magistrate Xu Zhi-min chose a quick, cheap material that would grow itself: _cangzhu_ bamboo. _Cangzhu_ bamboo has thorns, grows fast, and its roots are dense; after being woven into a fence, it took three to five years to take shape, which was ten times cheaper than mobilizing laborers to haul bricks and stones. The problem was that _cangzhu_ bamboo dries up, can be burned, and cannot withstand cannons. The 1827 reconstruction into a brick fort was the first material upgrade this city underwent in 96 years. From a "quick, cheap, self-growing green defense line" to a "brick fort built with 147,000 taels of silver," Hsinchu's logic of industrialization had already rehearsed itself here. The science park in 1980 was the same: first came the cheap schools and research institutes, then came the expensive wafer fabs. **Hsinchu has always been about planting first, then building.**

## Zhuguan Renamed Hsinchu in the Year Shen Baozhen Reorganized It

In the first year of Guangxu (1875), Shen Baozhen submitted a memorial to establish "Taibei Prefecture." The Tanwei Office was divided, and Zhuguan was renamed "Hsinchu," administratively falling under Hsinchu County within Taibei Prefecture [^8].

This renaming was part of the Qing court's administrative reform of Taiwan. In 1875, Shen Baozhen was also engaged in other matters: he built Yizai Gold City in Anping, Tainan; established Hengchun County in Hengchun; and promoted the "Opening Mountains and Pacifying Tribes" policy. From Shen Baozhen's perspective, Taiwan was the front line of the Qing dynasty's maritime territory and needed serious management. **The "Office" (Ting) of Tanwei was a transitional military administrative unit; the "County" (Xian) was the stable civil administration system.** Renaming Zhuguan to Hsinchu and changing the Zhuguan Office into Hsinchu County formally incorporated this 142-year-old office seat into the Qing court's regular administrative map.

In the fifth year of Guangxu (1879), the first governor of Hsinchu County took office, and the county government began to function in substance. In 1887, Taiwan was established as a province, and Hsinchu County was further divided to form Miaoli County. In 1895, the Qing court ceded Taiwan to Japan under the Treaty of Shimonoseki, ending the Qing administrative history of Hsinchu County. The Japanese established Hsinchu Prefecture in 1901 and upgraded the area to "Hsinchu Prefecture" in Taisho 9 (1920), with the prefectural capital set in Hsinchu. The Hsinchu Prefectural Office was completed in 1927 (started in 1925) and is the location of today's Hsinchu City Hall.

From 1733 to 1945, this city changed four identities: Tanwei Office Seat (Qing) $\rightarrow$ Hsinchu County Seat (Qing) $\rightarrow$ Hsinchu Prefecture (Japanese) $\rightarrow$ Hsinchu Prefecture (Japanese). With each change of identity, the administrative level was elevated. However, in 1950, after the war, the city was demoted for the first time due to county/city division. The Nationalist Government abolished the provincial city system, and Hsinchu City was designated as an urban area under Hsinchu County, losing its status as a provincial city [^9]. It wasn't until July 1, 1982, that Hsinchu City separated from Hsinchu County, merged with Xiangshan Township, and regained its status as a provincial city. In the same year, the county seat of Hsinchu County was moved to Zhubei Township (later upgraded to Zhubei City) [^9]. A 250-year-old city took 32 years after being separated by its own county before regaining provincial city status.

Today, Hsinchu City is composed of three districts: East District (Tsinghua, Hsinchu Science Park, Shibanjian Shan), North District (City God Temple, Glass Craft Museum, Old Town Area), and Xiangshan District (coastal wetlands, industrial zone). The population is approximately 450,000 in 2025.

## 1953 to 1974: 148 People Fled from the Hsinchu Base

The second chapter of Hsinchu's history is a secret story almost unknown outside the vicinity of the Hsinchu Air Base.

The Wikipedia entry on Black Bat Squadron states verbatim: "On June 15, 1953, the unit was transferred to the Hsinchu Air Base and renamed 'Air Force Special Operations Group'" [^4]. "In cooperation with the CIA's Taiwan-based agency, Western Company (in the East Road Office Building in Hsinchu City), carrying out low-altitude night electronic warfare reconnaissance missions into mainland China," "[the two sides used 'Western Company' as a cover, and the US side provided aircraft and necessary equipment]" [^4].

The name "Western Company" sounds like business, but it was actually the codename of the CIA in Taiwan. This sign hung on an office building on East Road south of the Hsinchu Air Base. Between 1953 and 1974, Taiwan received reconnaissance missions from the CIA at that building, accepted US-made aircraft, and planned routes.

The mission statistics are recorded verbatim: "**A total of 838 special missions were carried out; 10 aircraft were shot down or crashed by accident, resulting in 148 casualties**" [^4].

148 people—two-thirds of the entire squadron.

> **✦** "Two coconut trees were planted on either side of the entrance path to the barracks, with 3 on the left and 4 on the right, coincidentally corresponding to the unit number '34' of Black Bat Squadron." [^10]

These 7 coconut trees were planted in 1957. At that time, the camp where Black Bat Squadron lived was at No. 16, East Road Section 2, and it housed barracks, a club, and a sports field—a place caring for the families of 148 pilots. But after they flew out, they often did not return. Flying low-altitude missions across China's air defense net meant that being detected by PLA radar would result in targeting from anti-aircraft guns. The period from 1956 to 1964 was the most intensive phase of missions; B-17s, P2V Neptunes, and RB-69As were successively shot down.

At the end of December 1974, both Black Bat and Black Cat Squadrons ceased reconnaissance missions [^11]. This timing is not coincidental: Nixon's visit to China in 1972, the US withdrawal from Vietnam in 1973, and the realignment of strategic cooperation between the US and the Republic of China in 1974. The CIA no longer needed these reconnaissance agents.

However, this history was sealed in Hsinchu for 35 years before it began to be told. It wasn't until **November 22, 2009**, that the Hsinchu City Government opened the "Black Bat Squadron Museum" at No. 16, East Road Section 2 (inside Dongda Park), and the names of these 148 pilots were inscribed on a public wall for the first time. The building's exterior design is modeled after US military advisory group barracks; it is free to visit and open from Tuesday to Sunday [^12].

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The story of Black Bat Squadron shares a common structure with the story of the 1884 French soldiers buried in Keelung Zhongyuan Festival: **a city accepts the dead left behind by history, transforming them from "victims in an enemy-friend framework" into "the memory of this city."** But the time gaps are completely different. The Keelung Zhongyuan Festival has continued from 1855 to today for 170 years, and the French soldiers buried in 1884 were incorporated into the rites then. Black Bat Squadron flew from 1953 to 1974, and it was only publicly commemorated in 2009. **This 35-year gap is rooted in the Cold War structure.** The relationship between the ROC and the US was on the verge of collapse in 1974 (diplomatic ties were severed in 1979); openly acknowledging Black Bat Squadron meant openly admitting to the CIA having a station in Taiwan and challenging the cross-strait relations that were beginning to thaw at the time. It wasn't until after the Ma administration took office in 2008, economic liberalization across the strait, and the second and third generations of the martyrs began seeking compensation for their families, that this history had space to be told. **The timing of commemoration is always separated from the timing of death by a political menopause.**

## The Red Ribbon Cut by Chiang Ching-kuo: 24 Years of Academic Inertia Behind It

Hsinchu's third chapter begins with an unveiling ceremony.

The "Twentieth Anniversary" special issue of the Hsinchu Science Park Administration states verbatim: "**On December 15, the sixty-ninth year [of the ROC], the Science and Industrial Park was officially inaugurated, presided over by President Chiang Ching-kuo.**" [^3]

ROC Year 69 = 1980. On this day, Chiang Ching-kuo cut the red ribbon for Taiwan's first science park.

But why was the science park in Hsinchu? The Hsinchu Science Park Administration itself provided the answer verbatim: "**In view of the fact that the region already had National Tsing Hua University (specializing in atomic science), National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (specializing in electronic science), and the Industrial Research Institute (specializing in chemical engineering science), it was deemed possible to establish a scientific research park.**" [^3]

In other words: **Hsinchu had three academic institutions, so there was a park; not the other way around.**

National Tsing Hua University resumed operations in Taiwan in 1956 at Chituqi, East District of Hsinchu. National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University resumed operations in Taiwan in 1958, first establishing the Boai Campus in Hsinchu and later expanding to the Guangfu Campus. The Industrial Research Institute was established in Hsinchu County in 1973. These three institutions, along with the IC technology transfer agreement signed by the Industrial Research Institute and RCA in 1976 [^13], constitute the entire reason for selecting the site of the science park.

> **✦** "You must thank Minister Jiang (Yan-shi)... because NCTU didn't move... Mr. Ching-kuo's wise decision was to keep Tsinghua, NYCU, and IRI together." (Quoting Sun Yun-szean in the Hsinchu Science Park Twentieth Anniversary Special Issue [^3])

The background of Sun Yun-szean's statement is this: In the 1970s, there were proposals to move Tsinghua, NYCU, and IRI elsewhere. If they had moved, Hsinchu would have lost a park. **The choice made by economic bureaucrats like Sun Yun-szean and Lee Kuo-ding was not "to decide on semiconductors," but "to decide to keep the three universities in Hsinchu."** Only if the universities stayed did there become a reason to build the park.

On December 15, 1980, when the science park officially opened, **United Electronics was the first of the initial seven resident companies.** United Electronics was established on May 22, 1980, by the Industrial Research Institute and was Taiwan's first integrated circuit company, seven years before TSMC [^13].

On February 21, 1987, the Wikipedia entry for TSMC states verbatim: "**Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, Ltd. was officially established in Hsinchu Science Park, Taiwan Province of China on February 21, 1987**" [^14]. In 1985, Tseng Shih-mao returned to Taiwan at the invitation of Sun Yun-szean to serve as director of the Industrial Research Institute and chairman of United Electronics; in 1987, TSMC was founded with government support through a joint venture between the Industrial Research Institute and Philips.

From that day on, the story of Hsinchu Science Park was overshadowed by the three characters "TSMC."

In the fourth quarter of 2025, TSMC started mass production of its 2nm process (N2) at the Baoshan Fab (Fab 20), with a target monthly output reaching 6 to 6.5 million wafers by the end of 2026 [^15]. The total revenue of Hsinchu Science Park in 2025 was **NT$1.7 trillion**, growing by 12.25% year-on-year, with 584 companies and 179,000 employees [^6]. A provincial city of 450,000 people supports a semiconductor cluster whose revenue is more than half the GDP of Vietnam.

## Mifen Noodles and Meatballs in Wind City: Byproducts of Nine-Descending Winds

But the earliest specialty of this city was not chips; it was mifen noodles.

The name "Nine-Descending Winds" first appears in _Taiwan Prefecture Gazetteer, Geography_ from the thirty-third year of Kangxi (1694): "In September, the north wind is strong, sometimes lasting for a month, and is popularly called Nine-Descending Wind" [^16]. **Hsinchu is about 150 kilometers from the narrowest point of the Taiwan Strait (Quanzhou, Fujian)**. When the northeast monsoon crosses the Central Mountain Range from the Pacific and enters the Hsinchu plain, it encounters a topographical trap.

The story StoryStudio states: "**Because Hsinchu has a 'scoop-like' shape opening southeast to northwest, when the wind blows towards Hsinchu, it is restricted by topography, making it difficult for the wind to exit after entering.**" [^16]

The wind comes in but cannot go out; all of Hsinchu is blown into a giant dryer. The annual precipitation is much less than that of Keelung at the same latitude: 3,000 mm in Keelung versus only about 1,800 mm in Hsinchu. **In the background is the snowy mountain range, and the moisture stays in Yilan and Keelung; what blows to Hsinchu is the dry cold Nine-Descending Wind.**

This natural condition led to three things.

The first was mifen noodles. The National Cultural Memory Database states: "**Hsinchu City Gazetteer records that Mifen Noodles were introduced from Fujian. Many elderly noodle makers unanimously state that their ancestors came from Huian County, Fujian Province, and had already made mifen noodles in mainland China**" [^17]. But mifen noodles could be made everywhere along the coast of Fujian. The key difference for Hsinchu was the wind: "**While the technology to make mifen noodles across the province was generally similar, the reason Hsinchu Mifen Noodles dominated is due to the wind. Apart from Penghu islands, Hsinchu has the highest seasonal wind index among western counties... the strong wind and low rain created favorable conditions for drying mifen noodles, especially in October to January.**" [^17]

The ancestors of Lao Guo Mifen Noodles, Brother Guo Qian, brought mifen noodle technology from Huian, Fujian, with four children in 1858 during the late Qing Dynasty. Before Japanese rule, the noodles were "water noodles," coarse and requiring a long drying time—which suited Hsinchu's strong winds. Later, as technology improved, they could make fine and chewy "cooked noodles" (_cuifen_), which made Hsinchu Mifen Noodles famous. **Three parts sun-drying, seven parts wind-drying**, this is the statement from the Hsinchu Mifen Noodle Guild. The noodle makers were mostly concentrated in Dananxi (now Nansi Village), with over 100 noodle factories during the peak period [^17].

The second was meatballs. The Wikipedia entry on meatballs states: "**In the process of making meatballs, one step is to pound the meat paste with a stick to make it more Q-elastic; this action, pounding meat paste, is called _tian_ in Minnan dialect, so early spellings of meatballs were _tianwan_, until around ROC Year 50 when it changed to 'meatballs' due to automation.**" [^18]

The character "gong" (貢) is a modern usage. The original character was "tian," which meant pounding with a stick. In 1961, Huang Hai-rui, the founder of Hai Rui Meatballs, improved the meat-pounding machine to pound pork, increasing efficiency and stabilizing quality, turning Hsinchu meatballs from small handmade stalls into large-scale production [^19]. **Why are meatballs in Hsinchu? Because Hsinchu has sugarcane (pre-war sugar industry), black pigs (a northern breeding center), and a climate suitable for slaughtering and meat preservation due to the Wind City.** Jin Yi Meatballs, founded in 1938, is one of the oldest brands of meatballs in Hsinchu [^19].

The third was glass. In Taisho 14 (1925), Mr. Liao Qi-ming, a gentry member of Hsinchu, established a synthetic glass factory in Dingzhuiwei, Hsinchu City (now South Street) due to the availability of silica sand and gas, marking the beginning of the development of Zhuguan glass [^20]. During Japanese rule, the Japanese discovered abundant silica sand and natural gas in Hsinchu, both necessary raw materials for making glass. The geographical advantage of Wind City became a geological advantage this time. The glass industry developed rapidly in Hsinchu in the 1940s, and in 1999, the Hsinchu City Government converted the Municipal Assembly Hall built in 1936 during Japanese rule into the Glass Craft Museum [^20].

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Mifen noodles, meatballs, and glass—these three seemingly unrelated industries are all byproducts of Hsinchu's natural conditions. The strong wind dried the noodles; the silica sand underground allowed for glass production using natural gas. **This is exactly like the logic behind selecting the science park site**: the reason the science park was built in Hsinchu is because Tsinghua, NYCU, and IRI (talent gathered geographically) were already there, not the reverse causality. From mifen noodles in 1858 to the science park in 1980, every industry in Hsinchu originated from the basic sequence of "**natural conditions existing first, then industries growing**." This is the same pattern as Miaoli County's oil and gas, Yilan County's cold springs, or Pingtung County's tropical fruits: industries are geographical products, not planned ones.

## City God Temple in 1748: The Only Du Cheng Huang in Taiwan

Hsinchu has another ancient building that predates the East Gate by 81 years.

The Wikipedia entry on Hsinchu City God Temple states verbatim: "**In the twelfth year of Qianlong (1747), the magistrate Zeng Ri-ying submitted a memorial to the court, and ordered local gentry to raise funds to build a City God Temple as an official temple in the following year (1748).**" [^5]

A more precise timeline: construction began on February 17, 1747, was completed on August 16, 1748, and the City God statue was enshrined on November 29 of that year [^21]. The Tanwei Office took until 1756 to be completed. **This is the only example in Taiwanese history where a City God Temple was built before the government office.** [^21]

The status of Hsinchu City God Temple was elevated again in 1875. When Shen Baozhen established Taibei Prefecture, Hsinchu was temporarily placed under Taibei Prefecture, and the Hsinchu City God was renamed from the county-level "Xian You Bo" to the prefectural-level "Sui Jing Hou." In the sixteenth year of Guangxu (1890), it is recorded verbatim: "**Jiangxi Longhu Mountain's Master Zhang Tianshi observed celestial phenomena... The court approved holding a ceremony at Hsinchu City God Temple... Emperor Guangxu also bestowed the imperial plaque 'Jinmen Protection,' and promoted the Hsinchu City God to the rank of 'Wei Ling Gong Hsinchu Du Cheng Huang,' equivalent to a provincial governor.**" [^5]

The "Du Cheng Huang" rank is higher than the county-level "Xian You Bo" or the prefectural-level "Sui Jing Hou." The Hsinchu City God Temple is the only temple in Taiwan that received the title of "Du Cheng Huang" from the Qing court, commonly known as the "Chief City God of Taiwan" [^5].

The food at the temple entrance was more famous than the temple itself. Liu Family Braised Pork Rice was established in 1931; Acheng Hao has been operating for over 80 years; and Zheng Family Meatballs have been passed down for three generations or more: most vendors at the City God Temple were formed from the late Japanese colonial period to after restoration, combining meatballs soup, mifen noodles, braised pork rice, and _runbing_ (rice cakes) into Hsinchu's most representative snack map. Today, tourists almost always visit the temple entrance first in Hsinchu, but the historical logic of the temple entrance is: **first came the temple with the Du Cheng Huang rank, then the vendors formed around the temple, and finally the night market expanded by the second and third generations after the war.**

## 1956 + 1958: The Story of Two Universities Resuming Operations in Taiwan

Hsinchu has two universities, which are two key turning points in the post-war reconstruction of higher education in Taiwan.

National Tsing Hua University resumed operations in Taiwan in 1956. Tsinghua was founded in Beijing in 1911 and suspended operations on the mainland in 1949. In 1955, the Executive Yuan of the ROC decided to establish a "Preparatory Committee," with Hu Shi, then the director of the Atomic Energy Commission, as the preparatory director. The campus was selected in the Chituqi area of East District, Hsinchu, in 1956, initially establishing the Institute of Atomic Science, which is why the Hsinchu Science Park Administration later described Tsinghua's "specialty" as atomic science [^3].

National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University resumed operations in Taiwan in 1958. National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University was founded in Shanghai in 1896 (its predecessor, Nanyang Public School), and relocated during the war on the mainland in 1937. It resumed operations in Taiwan in 1958, selecting Boai Street, Guangfu Road, Hsinchu City. The specialties of NYCU are electronics, communications, and information technology, which is why the Hsinchu Science Park Administration described it as specializing in "electronic science" [^3].

The two universities were located 6 kilometers apart in Hsinchu, adjacent to both Shibanjian Shan, with students and faculty moving between them, collaborating in research, and spinning off companies, turning East District of Hsinchu into an academic peninsula for Taiwan. **The talent pool for the science park in 1980 came from graduates of these two universities**: United Electronics was established in 1980, TSMC in 1987, IC Design Institute in 1995 spun off from United Electronics, and Group Technology (which developed 5G) in 2009. All the IC design companies founded in the science park over these forty-five years had founders and core teams who came from Tsinghua or NYCU.

On February 1, 2021, National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University officially merged with National Yang Ming University to form "National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University" [^22]. Tsinghua had already merged with Hsinchu Education University in 2016.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Tsinghua in 1956, NYCU in 1958, IRI in 1973, Science Park in 1980, United Electronics in 1980, TSMC in 1987: there is one easily overlooked fact about this timeline. **The starting point of Taiwan's semiconductor industry was "finding a place for the two universities that resumed operations," and only then did the IC industrial policy grow.** In 1955, Hu Shi chose Hsinchu for Tsinghua partly because it had former higher education land from the Japanese colonial period and was far from the political center of Taipei. NYCU chose Hsinchu in 1958 because Tsinghua was already there, allowing them to share an academic circle. IRI chose Zhidu, Hsinchu in 1973 because graduates from Tsinghua and NYCU were local. The science park chose Hsinchu in 1980 because the Industrial Research Institute was local. **The location of the entire semiconductor industry was accumulated over 25 years of academic inertia, not the result of any single policy.** If Hu Shi had chosen Taichung instead of Hsinchu in 1956, TSMC would be in Taichung today. The contingency of history determined the fate of a city in this story.

## The Provincial City in Wind City: Home to the Cold War Frontline, Semiconductor Origin, and Two City Founding Anniversaries

Returning to the opening scene.

In 1733, the magistrate Xu Zhi-min planted _cangzhu_ bamboo around it. On December 15, 1980, Chiang Ching-kuo cut the red ribbon for Taiwan's first science park. **The physical distance between these two actions is less than 5 kilometers**: a 15-minute motorcycle ride from Yingxi Men to the entrance of Hsinchu Science Park. There are 247 years in between.

But you wouldn't feel this time gap while walking the streets. **What is strange about Hsinchu is that it compresses all layers of history onto the same geographical coordinate.** In the morning, eating mifen noodles and meatballs next to the City God Temple (1748 + 1858 + 1938 + 1961); in the afternoon, visiting the Glass Craft Museum to see products from Liao Qi-ming's synthetic glass factory in 1925 (1925 + 1999); in the late afternoon, visiting the Black Bat Squadron Museum to see the names of 148 pilots (1953 + 2009); in the evening, taking a walk at Tsinghua Plum Garden (1956); and returning to stroll in front of Yingxi Men (1829). All these sites are within a 30-minute walk of Hsinchu City.

A provincial city of 450,000 people. It supported the Cold War frontline, was one of the earliest Han Chinese cities built in the North, hosted two top universities, and served as the starting point for 90% of the world's high-end chips. **Scale never equals importance**, and this city is the most concrete evidence of that statement.

> **✦** "The reason Hsinchu Mifen Noodles dominated is due to the wind." (National Cultural Memory Database [^17])

The wind comes in but cannot go out. The _cangzhu_ bamboo planted by Xu Zhi-min in 1733 grew large because of the wind; the mifen noodles were dried by the wind; silica sand has been passing through for millions of years due to the wind; the northeast monsoon blows for half a year from September to March. That same northeast monsoon is blowing today. The maximum instantaneous wind speed measured at the Hsinchu Central Weather Station in October can reach over 25 meters per second, which is the lower limit of a tropical storm in Taiwan. People in Hsinchu do not say "the wind is very strong today"; they say "**Today we can dry mifen noodles.**"

From the perspective of the Central Mountain Range, Hsinchu is the last slope where the northeast monsoon reaches the western coast of Taiwan Island. The wind blows into the scoop-shaped mouth and blows out the _cangzhu_ bamboo planted in 1733, dries the mifen noodles, heats the furnaces of the glass factory, sweeps over the flight paths of the 148 Black Bat pilots who never returned, passes by the coconut trees at Tsinghua Plum Garden and NYCU Boai Campus, and blows past the surveillance cameras outside TSMC's 2nm facility.

Next time you visit Hsinchu, do not just look at the chip factories inside the park. Try standing in front of Yingxi Men around six in the morning to feel the wind. You will remember one thing: **the layers carried by this city far exceed the three characters "Tech City."** It is a city planted in 1733, sustained by the wind for 293 years, and it is still being blown upon today.
