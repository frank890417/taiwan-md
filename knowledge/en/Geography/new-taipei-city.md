# New Taipei City: The Ring Metropolis Surrounding Taipei, Sustaining the Capital's Commuting, Industry, and Memory with 4.01 Million People

> **30-Second Overview:** In 1628, the Spanish built St. Domingo in Tamsui; in 1643, the Dutch laid a new castle on the original site; and in 1867, Britain signed the "Red-Turban Permanent Lease" with the Qing court, paying only 10 _liang_ of silver annually. This brick predates Shen Baozhen establishing "Taipei Prefecture" in Mangga by 247 years. Today, New Taipei City, with its 29 districts surrounding the 12 districts of Taipei City, houses 4.01 million people in Taiwan's largest special municipality, having been upgraded only 15 years ago on December 25, 2010. However, the history of this "ring metropolis" stretches far back: Lin Family Sanluo Mansion in Banqiao in 1853; Dudde transporting 21,000 kilograms of oolong tea from Tamsui to New York in 1869; the first gold mine in Asia, Jiufen, in the 1890s; the Pingxi Railway that transported coal in 1929; the Sanxia Ancestral Temple carved by Li Meishu in 1947; and the Delan Clan of the Wulai Atayal people. While Taipei sees a satellite city, New Taipei sees its own history spanning 400 years.

## The Red Bricks of Tamsui Harbor, 247 Years Older Than the Founding of Taipei City

![Exterior view of the main building of Fort San Domingo in Tamsui. A square brick castle on a hill, with the British consulate built in 1891 next to it. Layered architecture spanning 400 years: Spanish Santo Domingo in 1628, Dutch Fort Anthonio in 1644, British lease in 1867, and returned in 1980.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/2017_Fort_San_Domingo.jpg/1280px-2017_Fort_San_Domingo.jpg)
_Fort Santo Domingo in Tamsui, where the Spanish laid the first brick in 1628. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Fort_Santo_Domingo)._

If you want to find a place that can speak for all of New Taipei City, go to Fort Santo Domingo in Tamsui.

The fort is located at No. 1, Lane 28, Zhongzheng Road, Tamsui District, and it is a square brick structure on a hill, with a two-story brick British consulate built next to it in 1891[^1]. From here, you can see the mouth of the Tamsui River, Bali, and the Taiwan Strait. Between 1628 and 1629, Spanish fleets sailed north from Manila and built the first generation fort at this location, using "clay, reeds, bamboo, and wood," naming it Fort Santo Domingo[^1].

The Spanish stayed in Tamsui for 14 years. In 1642, the Dutch East India Company attacked Fort San Salvador in Jilong from south of Taiwan and took control of Tamsui. On May 7, 1643, the Dutch laid the foundation for a new castle at the original site, "the first stone was officially laid, and construction began... until it was fully completed in 1646"[^1]. The Dutch spent three years converting the wooden fort left by the Spanish into Fort Anthonio. The red brick structure we see today has its framework from the Dutch who built it in 1644.

After the arrival of the Qing dynasty, this fortress changed hands again. In 1724, "Wang Tsi-ch'ing, the local magistrate of Tamsui in Taiwan Prefecture, began renovating Fort Santo Domingo"[^1]. In 1867, the Qing court signed the "Permanent Lease Agreement of Fort Santo Domingo" with Britain. "[The British established a consulate in Tamsui in 1861 and concluded the 'Permanent Lease Agreement of Fort Santo Domingo' with the Qing court in 1867],"[^1] paying only 10 taels of silver annually. The British stayed here for 105 years until they withdrew in 1972, entrusted management to Australia, then to the United States, and was officially handed back to the Republic of China government on June 30, 1980[^1].

From the Spanish (1628), Dutch (1644), Qing dynasty (1724), British (1867), Australian, and American, until its return to the Republic of China in 1980, one brick experienced six different administrations. It was designated a Grade I historic site in the Taiwan-Minnan area in 1983, and opened for public viewing on December 25, 1984[^1].

And what about the "founding" of Taipei? That wasn't until Shen Baozhen submitted a memorial to establish "Taipei Prefecture" in 1875, Chen Xing-ju began construction in 1879, the North Gate wall started construction in 1882, and was completed in 1884. This is 256 years after the first brick was laid at Fort Santo Domingo in 1628.

This is the first easily misunderstood fact about New Taipei City: **the history of being "surrounding Taipei" is actually two and a half centuries older than Taipei itself.**

## Opening Ports in 1858, Oolong Tea Reaching New York in 1869: The First Window to the Outside World

Next to Hongmao City is the mouth of the Tamsui River.

In June 1858, the Qing court signed the Treaty of Tianjin with foreign powers. This opened "the three ports of Taiwan (Anping, Tamsui, Dagou [Kaohsiung])" to foreigners for visitation and missionary work[^2]. The Qing dynasty sequentially signed the Treaty of Tianjin with Russia (June 13), the United States (June 18), Great Britain (June 26), and France (June 27). On July 18, 1862, the Tamsui Customs Office opened, making it the first port in Taiwan to officially open to global trade, 21 years before Keelung established its customs office (Keelung did so in 1883).

The British merchant John Dodd arrived around this time. In 1864, he came to Tamsui to inspect Taiwanese tea leaves and established the Baoshun Trading Company there, importing Anxi tea seedlings from Fujian to lend to northern farmers. In 1869, "21,000 kilograms of Taiwanese oolong tea were shipped directly to New York on two sailboats"[^3]. This was the first time Taiwanese tea was exported to the United States, traveling halfway around the world. After gaining a good reputation, oolong tea sold well in New York, and Tamsui followed suit.

"From 1864 to 1894, the total export value from Tamsui accounted for 57% of all of Taiwan's exports, with tea, sugar, and camphor being the major commodities"[^4]. Foreign trading staff speaking English on Tamsui streets, brokers writing letters back to Xiamen, textile workers from Fujian, and Taiwanese merchants procuring goods in Wanhua were all gathered along the same riverbank. "By 1878, tea accounted for 90% of the total northern exports"[^5]. Tamsui became a global window for Northern Taiwan.

George Leslie Mackay also arrived at this time. "[The missionary George Leslie Mackay from the Presbyterian Church in Canada landed in Tamsui on March 9, 1872]"[^6], and from the day he disembarked, he knew he would stay here for his life. In 1880, Mackay returned to Canada to raise funds, receiving $6,215 from residents of Oxford County in Ontario. "Completed on July 26, 1882, it was named 'The Grand Academy of Learning'"[^6]. This was the first Western-style school in Northern Taiwan, the predecessor of Trinity University (which established the "Tamsui Industrial and Commercial Management School" at the original Oxford College site in 1965 and upgraded to Trinity University in 1999).

But a war occurred in 1884.

On October 1, French forces attacked Hubei's mouth with four warships. Admiral Sun Kaihua used stones to sink ships and block the port, setting up mines to prevent the French from landing. "Official records from the French National Archives state that the French suffered 6 deaths, 11 missing, and 49 wounded"[^7]. The French occupied Keelung for eight months, but Tamsui was not captured.

"The Qing court recognized the strategic importance of Taiwan's coastal defense in this battle and subsequently declared Taiwan a province, appointing Liu Mingchuan as the first Governor of Taiwan while holding the position of Governor of Fujian"[^7]. After the war, Liu Mingchuan commissioned German military engineer Max E. Hecht to plan, and the Hubei Battery was completed in 1886 with the inscription "North Gate Lock," which can still be seen today[^7]. This is the most memorable contrast in New Taipei: **the direct trigger for Taiwan becoming a province was this small naval battle at Tamsui.**

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Taiwanese history textbooks often treat the "Treaty of Tianjin (1858) $\rightarrow$ Tamsui Port Opening (1860) $\rightarrow$ Oolong Tea Export to New York (1869)" as a single narrative. However, these three events compress the entire process of Tamsui transforming from a peripheral fishing port into a global trade node. The Baoshun Trading Company was near the Customs Wharf in modern Tamsui; Mackay's Oxford College is on the campus of Trinity University today; and the 1886 Hubei Battery is behind the Martyrs' Shrine in Tamsui. It only takes 10 minutes to walk from Hongmao City to Oxford College, but it spans over 200 years of world history. **Taiwanese people start talking about Taipei globalization after the 1980s. The people of Tamsui remember those 21,000 kilograms of oolong tea in 1869.**

## Banqiao Lin Family: The First Case of Indigenous Capitalism in 1853

![A corner of the courtyard at Lin Benyuan Mansion (Lin Family Garden), a late Qing dynasty garden built by Lin Wiyuan with significant investment in 1888 and completed in 1893. After 1949, it was occupied by over a thousand people from three hundred households; restoration began in 1982, completed in 1986, and is now a designated historic site.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Lin_Family_Mansion_and_Garden_2.JPG/1280px-Lin_Family_Mansion_and_Garden_2.JPG)
_Lin Benyuan Mansion (Lin Family Garden), completed in 1893, is the first case of indigenous capitalism spanning 133 years in Taiwan. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Lin_Family_Mansion_and_Garden)._

Half an hour's drive south from Tamsui is Banqiao.

If Tamsui is the international window of New Taipei, Banqiao is the starting point of indigenous capitalism in New Taipei.

The Lin family started early. "Lin Pinghou made a living transporting rice; when the Lin Shuangwen incident occurred, and rice prices rose, Lin Pinghou, as a rice merchant, gained extremely generous profits from the rice transport business" [^8]. He had five sons; his third son (this account) and youngest son (the source account) established "Lin Benyuan" as the family name, which is the official name of the "Banqiao Lin Family."

The Lin family settled in Banqiao in two steps. The first step: "In the 27th year of Daoguang (1847), the Lin family built Biyi Hall in Fangqiao (now Banqiao) to facilitate tenant rent collection, marking the beginning of the Lin family's construction in the Banqiao area" [^8]. The second step was the "Lin family building three residences next to Biyi Hall in Fangqiao in the first year of Xianfeng (1851), and moving the whole family there after completion in the third year of Xianfeng" [^8]. In 1853, during the third year of Xianfeng, the Lin family officially moved from Xinzhuang to Banqiao, marking the beginning of their identity as "Banqiao residents."

Next came the conflict between Zhang and Quan migrants. After Lin Guofang (the youngest son of Lin Pinghou) took over the family business in 1857, the Taipei conflicts between Zhang and Quan reached a peak. In 1861, Lin Guofang attempted to reclaim land rented by people from Quanzhou and re-rent it to people from Zhangzhou, causing great unrest among the populace of Greater Taipei. In the decades following this conflict, Banqiao became a base for migrants from Zhangzhou in Greater Taipei.

The true wealth of the Lin family belonged to the third generation, Lin Wiyuan and Lin Weirang. During the Sino-French War in 1884, "Lin Wiyuan donated 200,000 taels of silver as a subsidy and assisted Liu Mingchuan in managing Taiwan's defense affairs and served as an assistant minister of civil administration for Taiwan, becoming a 'Red-Turban merchant'" [^9]. The Lin family rose from rice merchants to political businessmen, dealing directly with high-ranking officials of the Qing court.

The Lin Family Garden was built during this period. In the 14th year of Guangxu (1888), Lin Wiyuan invested heavily in its construction, connecting various garden landscapes, and **it was finally completed in the 19th year of Guangxu (1893)** [^8]. This garden is still located on Ximen Street in Banqiao District today, covering a total area of 6,054 ping, and is Taiwan's best-preserved garden architecture.

However, after the Nationalist government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, the Lin Family Garden in Banqiao was almost destroyed. "After 1949, over a thousand refugees from three hundred households occupied it, causing severe damage" [^8]. Over a thousand provincial migrants lived in this late Qing garden; toilets, hearths, and clotheslines were crammed into pavilions and towers. It wasn't until the end of 1982 that "the restoration project for the garden began, lasting 4 years before completion. The total repair cost amounted to NT$156,433,218" [^8]. Designated a historic site in 1985, the Lin Family Garden we see today is the version completed after restoration in 1986.

From settling in Banqiao in 1853, investing heavily in the garden in 1888, completing it in 1893, refugees occupying it in 1949, starting restoration in 1982, and finishing in 1986—this garden spanned 133 years of Taiwanese history.

Banqiao, as the location of a county government (later a city government) in New Taipei, is inseparable from the Lin family. Today, Banqiao District has 549,000 residents (as of the end of 2025), making it one of the most populous administrative districts nationwide and the seat of the New Taipei City government [^10]. Yet, the core of Banqiao is actually located next to those three residences from 1853.

## Jiufen and Kangra in 1890: The Golden Age of Mining for 95 Years

![Terraced buildings of the Jiufen settlement, overlooking Keelung Island from the summit. A mining village that discovered gold in 1890, gathered 80,000 people in 1938, saw Taijin Company close down in 1987, and became a tourist hotspot after being filmed for "A City of Sadness" in 1989.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Jiufen_Old_Street_2017.jpg/1280px-Jiufen_Old_Street_2017.jpg)
_The transformation of Jiufen from a mining settlement to a tourist symbol. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Jiufen)._

North of Banqiao is the mountainous area of New Taipei.

In 1889, workers discovered alluvial gold in the Keelung River. Gold prospectors followed the stream upstream, and between 1890 and 1893, they reached Da Cukeng. In 1893, a small gold vein was found near the Jiufen mountains. The Kangra main ore body was discovered in 1894, marking the beginning of what was called "Asia's first precious metal mine."

When the Japanese colonial period began, the mining rights in Kangra were granted to Tanaka Gumi. On October 26, 1896, it was approved for Tanaka Chōbei to begin mining at the Kangra main body[^11]. The Japanese introduced mechanized extraction, rail lines, and chemical refining, transforming Kangra from a "scattered gold panning site" during the Qing Dynasty into an industrial mine under Japanese rule.

"[It reached its peak of nearly 70,000 taels in 1938, leading to Kangra being hailed as 'Asia's first precious metal mine,' and attracting up to 80,000 people who sought the dream of gold mining]"[^12]. A small town on a mountain summit housed 80,000 people in 1938. This was more than ten times the combined population of Wulai District (5,800), Pingxi District (4,400), and Shuangxi District (8,300) today.

After the war, mining continued in Kangra, managed by Taiwan Metal Mining Co., Ltd. (abbreviated as "Taijin Company"). However, gold production decreased year after year. In 1985, Taijin Company began downsizing, and mines and processing plants were gradually put up for sale. **"The Taijin Company finally declared bankruptcy in 1987, and the land of the Kangra mining area was taken over by Taiwan Sugar Company"[^13].** This is the precise time when the "golden age of Jiufen and Kangra" ended. From the discovery of gold in 1890 to Taijin's closure in 1987, it was a period of 97 years.

But the story of Jiufen did not end in 1987.

In 1989, director Hou Hsiao-hsien filmed "A City of Sadness" in Jiufen and Kangra. "Filming began on November 25, 1988, at the Eight Corners Pavilion in Keelung's Kangra," and after winning the Golden Lion at the Venice Film Festival in 1989, "[it made the filming locations Jiufen and Kangra tourist hotspots]"[^14]. The hillside settlement from the mining era was rewritten into a tourist symbol by a film. Starting in the 1990s, the old houses of Jiufen were converted into tea houses, and numerous tour buses appeared on the mountain summit; the "Five Mines," "Shengping Theater," and "Jishan Street" of the mining era became popular spots for Instagram check-ins.

In 2004, "[the Golden Museum Park officially opened under the guidance of the Taipei County Government]"[^15]. This was Taiwan's first park based on the concept of an ecological museum, integrating the former Imperial Guest House, the Five Mines, and the miners' dormitories into one place. It took 17 years for Kangra to transform from a mine into a museum.

From the perspective of tourists visiting Jiufen, this was a romantic transformation. But the descendants of the miners may not have felt that way. When Taijin Company closed down, the population of the Jiufen settlement dropped from 80,000 to less than 5,000. Miners dispersed to Taipei, Keelung, and Yilan to find work; many of those selling taro balls on Old Street in Jiufen today are descendants of those miners.

> **✦** The marker for the end of the mining era is the Taijin Company's closure in 1987, not the downsizing in 1985. 1985 was the beginning of slow boiling; 1987 was the moment the water truly boiled dry.

## The Industrial Chain of Pingxi Railway (1929), Yingge Ceramics (1804), and Sanxia Ancestral Temple (1769): Northern Taiwan's Craftsmanship

![Pingxi Line Old Street with Shifen, coal transport line operated by Taiyang Mining Company in 1921, acquired and converted to official operation and passenger service by the Governor-General's Office in 1929. The current tourist miniature train is based on the mining railway from the Japanese colonial period.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Shifen_Old_Street_2014.jpg/1280px-Shifen_Old_Street_2014.jpg)
_Pingxi Line Old Street, which continued from the official passenger service after conversion in 1929. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pingxi_Line)._

Beyond Jiufen and Kangra, there is Pingxi, located upstream on the Keelung River, which was another mining cluster.

"The Pingxi Line was fully completed in July 1921 (Taisho 10) and was originally built by Taiyang Mining Company as a dedicated coal transport railway"[^16]. During the Japanese colonial period, Taiyang Mining (run by the Yan family) discovered large amounts of coal in the stone bottom fields (the area of Pingxi and Jingtung), confirmed reserves in 1908 by Fujita's group, and built its own railway to transport the coal in 1921.

"On October 1, 1929 (Showa 4), the Taiwan Governor-General's Office Railway Department acquired the Stone Bottom Line for 1.5 million yuan and converted it into an official operation, naming it 'Pingxi Line,' after which it began operating passenger services"[^16]. This railway was originally a private coal transport line and was only acquired by the Governor-General's Office to become the "Pingxi Line" in 1929. The miniature train that tourists ride on the Pingxi Line today is based on the official operation version from 1929.

The sky lanterns of Pingxi are a later development. "During the Qing Dynasty, local people migrated through areas like Huian and Anxi in Fujian to the Shifenliao area. At the time, due to bandit unrest harassing the settlements, the migrants had to take refuge in the mountains. After the crisis passed, those who remained in the villages used sky lanterns as a signal to notify their families who had taken refuge"[^17]. This signaling system, originally for evacuation, began to be commercialized in the 1990s, and the "Regulations on Sky Lantern Release in New Taipei City" were established in 2012. Today, during the Lantern Festival every year, Pingxi's sky is filled with orange lanterns, which are beautiful but unrelated to coal mining.

Moving toward Sanxia and Yingge, there is another industrial chain.

The Zushi Temple of Changfu in Sanxia has conflicting records regarding its earliest construction date. ⚠️ The common official account states "Qianlong 34 (1769)"[^18]. The temple has undergone three reconstructions: the first after a major earthquake in 1833, the second after being burned as a resistance stronghold following Japanese military occupation in 1899, and the third in 1947. The person who led this reconstruction was painter Li Meishu.

"In 1947, the Zushi Temple underwent its third major renovation, with the temple authorities and local residents jointly promoting Li Meishu as the project director. Li Meishu personally oversaw the planning and reconstruction, opening up the astonishing decorative art of the Zushi Temple through his artistic expertise, earning it the reputation of an 'Eastern Art Hall'"[^18]. Li Meishu was from Sanxia and one of the founders of the Taiyang Fine Arts Association; he served as the chairman of the Sanxia Township Representative Council after the war. In 1947, he began sculpting this temple as a lifelong artistic work, treating every column, ceiling panel, and stone lion like an oil painting. **It was not finished until his death in 1983, and construction continues to this day**[^18]. A temple that took 79 years to sculpt is still not complete.

Yingge ceramics began with the Wu family at the end of the 18th century. "Yingge pottery originated in Jiaqing 9 (1804), when Wu An, from Quanzhou, first opened a kiln in the Dahu area"[^19]. Wu An came to Taiwan by sea from Quanzhou, Fujian, and initially lived in Tuzu Keng, Guishan, Taoyuan. Due to conflicts between Zhang and Quanzhou people, he moved to the vicinity of Jianshanpu Street in Yingge. Throughout the 19th century, the Wu family monopolized Yingge pottery. "Starting from 1917, organizations such as the 'Jianshan Pottery Association' and the 'Jianshan Pottery Production and Sales Cooperative' were successively established, breaking the monopoly held by the Wu surname. In 1939, Japanese 'Sifang Kiln' was introduced, greatly increasing the efficiency and quality of Yingge ceramics"[^19]. From 1804 to today, Yingge has produced ceramics for 222 years. The New Taipei City Yingge Ceramic Museum officially opened on November 26, 2000, as Taiwan's first museum focused on ceramics[^19].

Old Street in Sanxia is another story. "From 1886 to 1920, the golden age known as the 'Triangular Spring Dyeing period,' not only featured Lin Maoxing's dyeing workshop but also included 20 workshops such as Yongyu, Jinlianchun, Liu Chengfang, Li Yifa, Zhou Shenggfa, Lin Rongxiang, and Lin Yuanji, making it the most important indigo dyeing base in Northern Taiwan"[^20]. Sanxia's three major industries flourished simultaneously: indigo dyeing (indigo plants), camphor (produced from Sanxia forests), and tea (promoted by British merchant Dodd in 1868; a tea factory was established by Mitsui Co. during the Japanese colonial period). The architecture of Old Street in Sanxia, dating back to 1911, "retains commercial street buildings from the Japanese colonial era, continuous red brick arcades, and antique-style buildings"[^20].

From Jiufen and Kangra (mining) $\rightarrow$ Pingxi (coal transport) $\rightarrow$ Sanxia (indigo dyeing, camphor, tea) $\rightarrow$ Yingge (ceramics), the northeastern mountains of New Taipei City and the southwestern plains form a complete traditional industrial chain. This chain has gradually been commercialized since the 1980s, but its structure remains intact.

## Katagalug and Atayal: Two Indigenous Axes

The history of New Taipei is not one that begins with the Spanish.

In Bali, on the southern bank of the mouth of the Tamsui River, lies the Thirteen Factories Site. "The Thirteen Factories Site, located on the south bank where the Tamsui River meets the outlet in Bali District, New Taipei City, is one of the important archaeological sites in northern Taiwan. Its main inhabitants lived approximately 2,000 to 400 years ago and are prehistoric residents who are confirmed to have iron smelting technology in Taiwan; they are likely ancestors of the indigenous Katagalug people of Taiwan."[^21] In 1998, the exhibition hall was renamed the "Thirteen Factories Museum," and it officially opened in 2003.

The Katagalug people were not only in Bali. "Many place names in modern Taipei are phonetic transcriptions of the Katagalug language, such as: Dalongtong, Beitou, Mili'an, Bali, Wanhua, and Gayi."[^21] The place names Bali, Sanchong, Shimen, Tamsui, and Beitou are all Chinese characters transliterated from the Katagalug language. The name "Tamsui" itself may be a phonetic transcription heard by Fujian immigrants during the Qing Dynasty after hearing the Katagalug word _Tampsui_ (muddy water river).

However, contact between the Katagalug and Han people was asymmetrical. After Tamsui opened its port in 1858, Western missionaries and Han immigrants poured into northern Taiwan simultaneously; by the late 19th century, the Katagalug had largely Sinicized. Today, there are scattered descendants of the Katagalug in Bali District and Sanchong District, but unlike the "clear tribal structures" of the Amis or Paiwan, their language has almost disappeared.

The other indigenous axis in New Taipei is in Wulai.

Wulai District is the only mountainous indigenous township among the 29 districts of New Taipei, home to the Atayal people. Wulai mainly has four villages: Ulay Village (the main tourist area), Tampya Village, Rahaw Village ("dense forest"), and Tranan Village[^22].

"Tranan Village is pronounced _Deran_ in the Atayal language; it is the most mountainous village in Wulai and was the first settlement for the Atayal people who migrated from Bali in Hualien to Wulai."[^22] The location of Tranan is critical because it belongs to the same Atayal migration system as Lallan in Hualien. In the 17th and 18th centuries, the Atayal migrated north from Pinsbukan (the ancestral home of the Atayal) in Ren'ai Township, Nantou; some people crossed Lallan into Wulai, while others remained in Hualien. Today, the residents of Tranan Village in Wulai and the villages in Lallan, Hualien, still speak the same dialect of the Atayal language.

The history of Wulai hot springs began during the Japanese colonial period: "After being discovered by Chang Dan-ye Ying-qing in the South Creek Valley in 1903, a bathhouse was established by the Wulai Bureau Office."[^22] The Japanese turned Wulai into a backyard for people from Taipei. This identity continues to today, making Wulai the most touristed "indigenous township" among the 29 districts of New Taipei. However, the true core of Wulai is still in the deep mountain villages like Tranan and Rahaw—places that tourist buses cannot reach.

From the Katagalug (Tamsui) to the Atayal (Wulai), New Taipei encompasses two indigenous axes: one representing the "near disappearance" of the Pingpu people, and the other representing the "continued existence" of the highland peoples. Neither of these axes is located within Taipei City.

## Post-War Expansion: Three Waves of Growth in Yonghe (1958), Sanzhuan Industrial Migration in the 1970s, and the Ershang Floodway (1979)

The population explosion in New Taipei occurred in three waves.

The first wave was in Zhonghe and Yonghe during the 1950s. After the Nationalist government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, "the area of Zhonghe and Yonghe was designated as an evacuation zone due to air defense concerns for Taipei City, leading to rapid population growth" [^23]. By 1956, the population of Zhonghe had reached 44,325, several times that of 1945. On April 1, 1958, "the six villages in Guilanxi-zhou and one village each in Tanqian and Xiulang were officially established as a township by splitting from Zhonghe Township. The initial proposed name was Zhongxing Township, but due to the similarity with Zhongxing New Village, it was named Yonghe Township" [^23]. Zhonghe and Yonghe were formally split into separate townships.

Yonghe's true density came later. From the 1980s to the 1990s, "many Korean overseas Chinese from Shandong came to Taiwan, settling in Yonghe" [^24]. Zhongxing Street in Yonghe became a "Korean Street," selling Korean groceries, Korean cuisine, and ginseng tea. This street gradually transformed after the 1970s into a commercial area selling Korean goods and Korean food. Yonghe's culinary map, population density, and immigrant composition are all linked to its initial designation as an "evacuation zone of Taipei" in 1949.

The second wave was in Sanchong and Xinzhuang during the 1970s.

"The 1960s and 1970s were a period when the Ten Great Construction economic boom took off, and Sanchong became fully active. Small factories were located among neighborhood alleys, with three steps one house and five steps one room, mainly producing semi-finished industrial parts such as screws, molds, spot welding, electroplating, lathes, and plastic injection. The large-scale output supported half of the province's mechanical industrial parts" [^25]. Sanchong's factories represented the Taiwanese model of "home as a factory," with people making screws and operating lathes in every alley.

"Sanchong is located at the beginning and end of an expressway, offering convenient north-south transportation, and it is adjacent to prosperous Taipei City across a waterway. These two superior conditions made Sanchong one of the cities with the most migrants in the province" [^25]. During the 1970s, there was a large migration of rural populations from Central and Southern Taiwan northward, and Sanchong was their first stop: with Tamsui River on one side and Taipei on the other, rent was cheap and jobs were plentiful. Many second-generation migrants from Yunlin, Chiayi, Changhua, and Pingtung made Sanchong their first destination.

Industrial development in Xinzhuang also began in the 1970s: "The establishment of Touqian Industrial Zone and Xisheng Industrial Zone was a crucial turning point for Xinzhuang as it transitioned from an agricultural town to an industrial city... The industries with the largest proportion were chemicals, textiles, machinery, and metals. These industries attracted a large number of industrial workers, and people from other places continuously moved in, leading to rapid population growth" [^25]. Xinzhuang was connected to Sanchong, forming an industrial cluster extending west from the western bank of Tamsui River toward Linkou.

However, too many factories meant too much discharge, too much drainage, and too much flood risk. The "Ershang Floodway," which was approved by the Executive Yuan in 1979, implemented in 1982, and completed in 1984 [^26], was built to address this. It "primarily targeted flood control for Sanchong, Xinzhuang, Wugu, and Luzhou, while also accommodating relocated households from legal factories near the floodway" [^26]. This artificial waterway diverted excess floodwater from Sanchong and Xinzhuang toward the sea outlet, while also resettling residents from illegal factories. The green spaces and bicycle paths on both sides of the floodway were originally floodplains of a tributary of the Tamsui River.

The third wave was in Linkou and the Xinzhuang sub-metropolis during the 2000s.

Starting in 2003, New Taipei began the redevelopment of Linkou and the development of the Xinzhuang sub-metropolis, attracting the middle class who moved from Taipei City but could not afford Taipei housing prices. Within ten years, Linkou transformed from military barracks and farmland into a new town with 100,000 residents. National Taiwan Sport University in Linkou, Chang Gung Hospital in Linkou, Mitsui Outlet in Linkou, and the High-Speed Rail Station in Linkou were all built after 2000. The Xinzhuang sub-metropolis benefited from the dual transportation advantages of the MRT Xinzhuang Line (opened in 2010) and the Airport MRT (opened in 2017).

From Yonghe in the 1950s, Sanchong and Xinzhuang in the 1970s, to the Linkou sub-metropolis in the 2000s, New Taipei's population expanded in waves every 20 years. Each wave absorbed overflow from Taipei City, influx from those who migrated north (北漂), and migrants from Central and Southern Taiwan.

## The Day the County Became a Municipality: "The Largest in Taiwan" After 31 Years

After accommodating three waves of expansion, the population of Taipei County reached 3.86 million in 2009.

"Taipei County repeatedly sought to upgrade to a municipality starting in 1979 due to its population reaching the threshold for a municipality, but these efforts were all rejected"[^27]. However, in 1979, Taipei County only had 2.5 million people, making it the most populous local government in Taiwan compared to Taipei City (2.3 million) and Kaohsiung City (1.3 million) at the time. Yet, the central government continuously refused to elevate Taipei County's status. Elevation meant a redistribution of budget, personnel authority, and decision-making power—something both Taipei City and the central government were reluctant to relinquish.

After 31 years of striving, the Legislative Yuan passed an amendment to the Local Government Act in May 2007, which allowed Taipei County, with a population exceeding two million, to apply for municipal regulations. The Ministry of the Interior approved it on June 23, 2009. "On December 25, 2010, Taipei County was upgraded to the municipality of 'New Taipei City,' and the Taipei County government was renamed the 'New Taipei City Government' with the English name 'New Taipei City,' becoming the most populous municipality within the Republic of China"[^28]. On the same day, Taichung City, Tainan City, and Kaohsiung City underwent similar reforms (the Five Cities Reform).

The name "New Taipei" itself has a story. The original proposals from the Taipei County government included names such as "New Taipei City," "North Taiwan City," "Taipei County City," and "Northeast City." Ultimately, they selected "Xinbei" (New Taipei), which is a direct English translation. This differs from the three cities that underwent simultaneous reform—Taichung, Tainan, and Kaohsiung—which retained their historical names. New Taipei is the only municipality among the Five Cities to have a name implying "new life," and it is also the only example of an "upgraded county becoming a municipality" (not through county-city mergers)[^28].

Banqiao was changed from "Banqiao City" to "Banqiao District," yet it remains the location of the municipal government, making it the **"largest district in Taiwan."** Banqiao District has a population of approximately 549,000 (as of the end of 2025), exceeding two complete provincial cities, Chiayi City (267,000) and Keelung City (359,000)[^10]. A district being larger than a city demonstrates the scale of New Taipei.

Fifteen years have passed. Today, New Taipei City has 29 districts with a total population of 4,041,149 (as of April 2026), remaining the largest municipality in Taiwan[^10]. However, the number "29 districts" itself reflects internal fragmentation within New Taipei. The five major districts—Banqiao (549,000), Xinzhuang (over 400,000), Zhonghe (over 400,000), Sanchong (over 300,000), and Xindian (over 300,000)—are vastly different from the five mountainous districts—Wulai (5,800), Pingxi (4,400), Shuangxi (8,300), Gongliao (11,000), and Pinlin (6,300)—by a factor of 100.

This is the true face of New Taipei: a municipality containing three types of cities—the "metropolitan areas" like Banqiao, Xinzhuang, and Yonghe; the "new towns" such as Linkou and Tamsui; and the "mountain/coastal remote areas" like Ruifang, Pingxi, Wulai, Gongliao, and Pinlin. The disparity within the 29 districts is even greater than the gap between New Taipei and Taipei.

## The Ring Metropolis Surrounding Taipei: 390,000 People Sustaining the Capital Through Commuting

_Map of the 29 districts of New Taipei City, a ring structure surrounding Taiwan's largest municipality. Map: Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan_ROC_political_division_map_New_Taipei_City.svg)._

Viewing New Taipei from Taipei 101.

To the east: Nangang extends to Xizhi; to the north, it connects to Keelung. To the north of Tianmu is Beitou, and further out is Tamsui. To the west are Banqiao and Xinzhuang across the Tamsui River. To the south are Yonghe and Zhonghe, which border Gongguan, with Xindian further south.

The 29 districts of New Taipei form a ring surrounding the 12 districts of Taipei City. A municipality completely encircling another is a rare administrative geography worldwide.

The core function of this ring metropolis is commuting.

In April 2026, the total population of New Taipei was 4,041,149[^10]. It is estimated that over 390,000 people commute from surrounding counties to work in Taipei[^29]. Between 7 and 9 AM, trains, buses, and cars travel from Banqiao, Sanchong, Xinzhuang, Yonghe, Zhonghe, Xindian, and Xizhi toward Taipei City, causing the bridges across the Tamsui River to become completely gridlocked. In the evening, between 5 and 7 PM, the congestion happens in reverse.

The MRT network of New Taipei has grown gradually. The Tamsui Line opened in 1996 (from Taipei Station to Tamsui), followed by the Zhonghe Line (from Nanshijiao to Taipei) in 1997, and the Xindian Line (from Xindian to Taipei) in 1998. These were extensions of the "Taipei Metropolitan Area Mass Transit System" planned starting in the 1980s. The Airport MRT opened in 2017, the Tamsui-Hsinchu Light Rail Green Mountain Line opened in 2018, and the Circular Line officially began operation on January 31, 2020 (a total length of 15.4 kilometers with 14 stations; Phase I covers Xinzhuang, Banqiao, Zhonghe, and Xindian)[^30]. Today, only 16 districts within New Taipei have an MRT line; the remaining 13 districts (including Wulai, Pingxi, Gongliao, Shuangxi, Pinglein, Shiding, Shenkeng, Sanchong, Shimen, Jinshan, Wanli, Ruifang, and Pingxi) have no rail lines whatsoever.

This is the physical reality of "390,000 commuters." The narrative that New Taipei "supports the capital through commuting" is not just rhetoric; it is the real scene of people queuing up at Banqiao Station, Tuanzhuan in Xinzhuang, and under the Taipei Bridge in Sanchong every morning.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** A common online narrative suggests that "New Taipei is Taipei's sleeping city," but this narrative has two blind spots. **The first blind spot** is viewing New Taipei as a single city while ignoring the coexistence of three different eras within its 29 districts. The Atayal tribe in Wulai and the Lin Family Garden in Banqiao exist within the same municipality. **The second blind spot** is that the commuting relationship is actually bidirectional. While 390,000 people from New Taipei commute to work in Taipei, Taipei City also relies heavily on New Taipei for water supply, waste disposal, cemeteries, MRT maintenance depots, airport transfers, and agricultural products from its mountainous areas and river mouths. The water source for Tamsui comes from the Nanshi Creek in Wulai; Taipei City's landfill is in Xizhi, its cemetery is in Jinshan, and a public cemetery is in Banqiao. **The relationship between New Taipei and Taipei is one of mutual necessity, not unidirectional dependence.** When the Lin family moved from Xinzhuang to Banqiao in 1853, the "Taipei Prefecture" had not yet been established. History makes it clear who came first.

## The Gongliao International Ocean Music Festival in 2000: Another Axis on the Rock Beach

If you want to see contemporary culture in New Taipei, don't go to Banqiao or Tamsui. Go to Gongliao.

Gongliao Beach is located in the Gongliao District, the northeastern corner of New Taipei City, right by the Pacific Ocean. "The first International Ocean Music Festival was held at Gongliao Beach in Gongliao Township, Taipei County, on July 15, 2000, and lasted for only one day with approximately 8,000 participants" [^31]. This was the starting point of what later became the "Gongliao International Ocean Music Festival," one of Taiwan's most important annual events in independent music.

⚠️ The correct starting year for this festival is 2000 (Stage 0 record before Taiwan.md stated 1999, which has been corrected here).

"The Ocean Music Festival was initially initiated through a collaboration between the Taipei County government and the independent underground music production company Jiadou Music; the main figures behind the activities were Zhang Sisi-san, the owner of Jiadou Music, and Liao Chih-chien, then the director of the press office for Taipei County" [^31]. "Hohaiyan (吼海洋) comes from the Amis language, one of Taiwan's indigenous languages, and is an onomatopoeia related to ocean waves" [^31]. The name of the festival itself represents an indigenous axis.

From 2000 to today, the Ocean Music Festival has run 26 times (with a hiatus in 2020-2021 due to the pandemic). Bands such as Soda Green, Fire Extinguisher, Directors Chang, Mayday, Lunacy, Echo Band, Girls and Robots, Tizzy Bac, Constant Change, Sunset Rollercoaster, Hello Nico—all among the most important bands in Taiwan's independent music scene over these 25 years—have performed on Gongliao Beach.

The significance of this musical axis is that New Taipei provides a **public cultural space within the metropolitan area of Taipei City but outside of Taipei City itself**. Gongliao is an hour and a half by car from Taipei Station, or two hours by commuter train. Every summer, at the end of July or beginning of August, tens of thousands of young people travel by train from Taipei City, New Taipei City, and Taoyuan to Gongliao Station, walking from the station to the sea. This "short-term migration" is unique to New Taipei: there is no ocean in Taipei City, and the beaches of Taoyuan are too far from the city, but Gongliao is conveniently within 60 kilometers.

New Taipei has also attempted to extend this musical axis into the metropolitan area. The New Taipei Pop Music Center was inaugurated in Xinzhuang sub-center in 2022, but a pop music center is inherently different from the seaside. What makes Gongliao special is that it sits near the decommissioned Nuclear Plant No. 4 (built in 1979), White Sand Beach, the Pacific Ocean, and typhoon remnants in the summer—all these elements combine to create that feeling of a "rock beach just far enough from the capital."

## From 1628 to 2026: A City Surrounding Taipei for 400 Years

Let's return to the original brick.

From when Fort Tamshui (Hongmao Cheng) was first built in 1628 until today, 2026, it has been 398 years. During this period, it has seen Spanish, Dutch, Qing, British, Australian administration, American administration, and the Republic of China; nearly five times longer than the identity of "Republic of China" for Taiwan itself (counting from 1945).

Yet, the administrative name New Taipei City is only 15 years old.

But this metropolitan area surrounding Taipei was never created in 2010. From the Spanish Fort Tamshui in 1628, the first temple built in Sanxia Changfu (or the second reconstruction in 1769), the kilns opened by Wu An in Yingge in 1804, the Lin family's Three Fall Mansion in Banqiao in 1853, the opening of Tamshui port in 1858, Ulang tea reaching New York in 1869, Mackay landing in Tamshui in 1872, the discovery of gold mines in Jiufen in 1890, the completion of the Lin family garden in 1893, the opening of the Pingxi Railway in 1929, Li Meishu starting the ancestral temple in 1947, the division into Zhonghe and Yonghe in 1958, industrial migration to Sanchong in the 1970s, the closure of Taikin Company in 1987, the first Ocean Music Festival in Gongliao in 2000, and the county's elevation to a municipality on December 25, 2010—the history of New Taipei is an intensive timeline spanning 400 years.

With 29 districts, cities with three different ages, and 4.01 million residents who commute daily to work in Taipei, the city itself is two and a half centuries older than the capital. The easiest misconception about New Taipei City is that **it was never merely a "satellite city."**

From the perspective of Taipei, New Taipei is a satellite. But from the perspective of Fort Tamshui, Taipei is the neighbor that appeared later.

Next time you visit New Taipei, do not just walk through the corridors of the Lin family garden in Banqiao (although the garden from 1893 is still worth an afternoon). Do not only eat fish crackers on Old Street in Tamshui (even though Tamshui has a commercial structure dating back to the Treaty of Tianjin in 1858). Try walking from Fulong Station to the seaside and listening to the Ocean Music Festival, which started in 2000 and continues today (if you visit at the end of July). Try taking the Pingxi Line to Jingtung Station to see the coal fields discovered in 1908 that were converted into a tourist railway (there are also remnants of the coal transport tracks along the line). Try taking a bus from Wulai to Fusang Village and seeing the mountain trails of the Atayal people.

After walking, you will remember one thing: **New Taipei City is not a sleeping city surrounding Taipei. New Taipei City is the historical clock that contains Taipei—but the numbers on its face just happen not to be in Taipei's slot.**
