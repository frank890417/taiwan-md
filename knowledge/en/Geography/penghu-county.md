# Penghu County: Two Rejections of Casinos, the Choice Not to Be Poor

> **30-Second Summary:** The first gambling referendum in Penghu took place on September 26, 2009, with 56.44% voting against; there were 3,962 more votes against than for. This was the first local gambling referendum in Taiwan's history. Seven years later, on October 15, 2016, a second vote occurred, with 81.07% voting against, which was 25 percentage points higher than the first. Nothing happened in those seven years to suddenly make Penghu rich. It is an outlying island—with only about 100 million millimeters of annual rainfall and roughly 80,000 residents compared to 108,000 registered households (ranking second to last nationally)—that has rejected resort casinos twice through referendums. This article argues that the subjects who said NO twice were not the casinos, but the choice of "what Penghu wants to become."

On Magong Central Street at four in the morning, there was no one.

Central Street is one of Taiwan's oldest Han Chinese streets [^1]. The "Four-Eyed Well" in the north is said to be a well where residents drew water since the Ming Dynasty and is now a county-designated historical site [^2]. Two hundred meters further south is the Kaidae Goddess Temple. During renovations in 1919 (the 8th year of Taisho), a granite stele was unearthed by the temple staff, bearing nine characters: "Shen Yourong Edicting the Retreat of Redhead Barbarians Wei Malang, etc." [^3]. This stele dates to the 32nd year of Wanli, which is 1604 AD.

At four in the morning, the temple gate was closed, and the stele was inside a glass case at the Qingfeng Pavilion Museum. The people of Penghu were sleeping. Tourists would not arrive until their seven-thirty flight tomorrow. The northeast monsoon wind blew over the _laogushi_ (coral stone) walls of the old street from the northwest, blowing into the alleys. For two-thirds of the year, this is how it blows in Penghu.

This archipelago, covering 141 square kilometers, was the first place in Taiwan that European powers paid attention to over four centuries. The Portuguese called it Pescadores (Fisherman Islands) in the 16th century. The Dutch came for the first time in 1604 and a second time in 1622. The French visited in 1885. The Japanese landed here in 1895 to take control of all of Taiwan. Penghu was always the entry point. **The main island of Taiwan always arrived later.**

## The Stele from 1604: Shen Yourong's Edict on the Retreat of Wei Malang

![Tianhou Temple before Kaishi, 2014. It is a scholarly consensus that the temple existed in "Ming Wanli Year 32 (1604)," and the stele discovered by the temple in 1919 is the earliest surviving evidence. Photo: Outlookxp.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4e/Penghu_Tianhou_Temple_02.jpg/1280px-Penghu_Tianhou_Temple_02.jpg)
_Tianhou Temple, 2014. Photo: Outlookxp via Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Penghu_Tianhou_Temple_02.jpg)._

The construction date of the Tianhou Temple is a subject of debate. The compilation from penghu.info (Penghu History and Culture Website) states: "While there is no definitive consensus on when it was founded, it can be confirmed that it existed in Ming Wanli Year 32 (1604)."[^4] There are five proposed dates among scholars—1563, 1592, 1597, 1604, and 1622—but only the year 1604 has physical evidence: the Shen Yourong stele.

What happened in 1604?

In that year, Wybrand van Warwijck, commander of the Dutch East India Company, entered Penghu with two ships and sent envoys to Fujian seeking trade relations. The Ming court dispatched Shen Yourong, a prefect, with fifty warships for negotiations. After assessing the commercial prospects and military strength, Van Warwijck withdrew from Penghu on December 15, 1604[^5]. There was no battle. A stone stele recorded this event, inscribed by an official of the Ming dynasty at the time, and unearthed from under the temple's foundation 315 years later. In March 2022, the Ministry of Culture designated this stele as a national treasure[^6].

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Middle school textbooks often begin teaching early Taiwan history with the Dutch establishment of Fort Zeelandia in Tainan in 1624. However, 1624 was the Dutch "Plan B": they originally intended to occupy Penghu. When they returned a second time in 1622, they had already built a castle at Fengguiwei, called the "Penghu Fengguiwei Dutch Castle" (now Snake Head Mountain in Magong, where ruins still exist). Only after the Ming court reinforced its forces in Penghu in 1624 were the Dutch forced to retreat to Tainan. **The Dutch period on Taiwan's main island began only after they had been driven out of Penghu.** The Shen Yourong stele from 1604 records the first round of this two-decade struggle. To the Dutch, Penghu was "the island we wanted to occupy," while Taiwan's main island was "where they went because they couldn't take Penghu."

The stele was unearthed in 1919, 24 years after Japan had taken control of Taiwan. In 1920 (Taisho 9), during the local administrative reforms, the temple was renamed Magong and has retained this name to this day. The characters "Magong" are a Japanese abbreviation of "Magiang."[^7] Over three hundred years, the same temple changed its name three times: Magiang $\rightarrow$ Tianfei Temple $\rightarrow$ Tianhou Temple. The stele was buried under the foundation when the temple was built and was only found in 1919.

## Basalt Houses, Penghu

![Columnar basalt of Tongpan Island, 2008. Volcanic lava cooled and contracted 15 million years ago formed hexagonal columnar joints; Tongpan is called "Yellowstone in the Sea." Photo: Carrie Kellenberger (globetrotter).](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Columnar_Igneous_Rocks_on_Tongpan_island_in_Taiwan.jpg/1280px-Columnar_Igneous_Rocks_on_Tongpan_island_in_Taiwan.jpg)
_Tongpan Island columnar basalt, 2008-07-12. Photo: Carrie Kellenberger / globetrotter via Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY 2.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Columnar_Igneous_Rocks_on_Tongpan_island_in_Taiwan.jpg)._

The Penghu County Cultural Affairs Bureau created an online textbook titled "World Wonders: Basalt of Penghu." The first paragraph states: "Approximately 17.4 to 8.2 million years ago, erupted through surface fissures..."[^8].

The oldest basalt is on Wang'an Island, dating back about 17.4 million years. The youngest is on Dongyup Island, from 8.2 million years ago. Over the nine million years in between, magma continuously surged from underwater fissures, cooling, contracting, and fracturing into hexagonal or pentagonal columnar joints. This process formed the ninety islands.

Only one of the ninety is an exception. The Penghu County government's geology page states: "The geology of the Penghu archipelago is due to Cenozoic volcanic activity; besides Huashi, which has andesitic igneous rocks, most are basaltic igneous rocks."[^9] Huashi is the westernmost point, with older andesite geology. The island hosts nearly a hundred species of native plants, hence its name. Among the eighty-nine black islands, there is one older grayish-white island.

Basalt is a building material in Penghu. Traditional communities used basalt mixed with _laogushi_ (calcareous coral) to build walls, as seen in the Erkan community in Xiyu Township. The Chen Family Old House was designated a Grade III Historic Site (now a county-designated historic site) in 1988, making it Taiwan's first traditional preserved village[^10]. The roof is covered with thick stone slabs to withstand the northeast monsoon. Penghu receives only about 1,000 millimeters of rain annually (the lowest part of the western coast), but the winds are strong; from October to March every year, the northeast monsoon blows for half a year, and if the tiles are not weighted down, they can be blown away[^11].

The walls hold things inside. The _caizhai_ (vegetable gardens) of Penghu are a solution for winter farming. The Wikipedia article on Penghu _caizhai_ states: "Penghu's _caizhai_, also known as _zhai nei_, is an elaborate farm where four sides are walled to resist the strong northeast monsoon, allowing delicate crops to grow successfully in winter... The north wall is the highest, about 1.6 to 2.5 meters high... The stone walls are usually built using local materials such as limestone or basalt."[^12] From above, hundreds of _caizhai_ connected together look like a honeycomb. In winter, the wind from the Humen Strait blows outside, while cauliflower, cabbage, ginger, and sweet potatoes grow inside the walls.

The same stones are also used in the sea. "There are less than 600 trap fisheries worldwide; Penghu County currently has over 574 trap fisheries, with at least 109 around Jibei Island, making it the densest in the world."[^13] Fishermen stack basalt and coral reefs into crescent-shaped enclosures in the tidal zone. When the tide rises, fish swim in; when it recedes, they are trapped inside. The _Taiwan Prefecture Records_ from the 35th year of King Kangxi (1696) already recorded Penghu trap fisheries, and until the 1950s, these traps accounted for nearly eighty percent of the county's total fishery output[^14]. Today, most trap fisheries are merely cultural landscapes, but there is one of the most complete double-heart trap fisheries beneath the sea cliff in Donghu Village, Qimei Township, which was rebuilt by Master Balah of Jibei in 1937[^15].

> **✦** "There are less than 600 trap fisheries worldwide; Penghu County currently has over 574 trap fisheries, with at least 109 around Jibei Island, making it the densest in the world." (Ministry of Culture Taiwan World Heritage Potential Site [Penghu Trap Fishery Group][^13])

The Ministry of Culture listed both the Penghu basalt and the Penghu Trap Fishery Group as potential World Heritage sites for Taiwan. However, since Taiwan is not a member of UNESCO, these lists cannot be submitted. This list is one that Taiwan made for itself.

## 1622 Fengqiwei: The Dutch Stepping Stone

This is the second round following 1604.

On the morning of July 1, 1622, a Dutch commander named Reilens led twelve ships and 1,024 soldiers to invade Magong Port. The Penghu County Government History Evolution website states: "On the morning of July 1, 1622 (the second year of Tianqi in Mingxi-zong), the Dutch commander Reilens invaded Magong Port with 12 ships and 1,024 soldiers" [^16]. The Dutch learned their lesson this time; they did not demand trade. Instead, they directly built a castle at Fengqiwei (Snake Head Mountain, the southernmost point of modern Magong Peninsula).

The Ming court reacted slowly. Two years later, in 1624, Nanju Yi was appointed Governor of Fujian and reinforced Penghu to surround the Dutch. The Dutch could not hold out and negotiated with the Ming court. The Wikipedia article on the Dutch period states: "The Dutch and the Great Ming reached an agreement, agreeing to destroy the fortresses and batteries at Fengqiwei and move to Taiwan, which was outside the Ming territory; the Ming also did not interfere with the Dutch occupation of Taiwan. On August 26, 1624, the Dutch forces withdrew from Penghu and moved to Taiwan" [^17].

This contingent of Dutch troops, who had withdrawn from Fengqiwei in Penghu, later built Fort Zeelandia in Anping, Tainan. The Dutch period on the main island of Taiwan began here.

Next, this island changed hands between European and Asian powers. In 1683, Shi Lang attacked Penghu, and Penghu entered Qing rule along with the main island of Taiwan; this lasted for 212 years (1684–1895). On March 29, 1885, the Sino-French War reached Penghu, when French forces led by Commander Amédée Courbet seized Penghu. Another battle on the main island of Taiwan was ongoing: in the same year, French troops landed in Keelung but were repulsed by Liu Mingchuan and an epidemic [^18]. The situation in Penghu was no better. After the French occupied Penghu, cholera broke out; Courbet himself fell ill during a funeral for his subordinates on June 8, 1885, and died of illness aboard the flagship Bayard in Magong Port on the 11th [^19]. In the same month, the Sino-French Treaty was signed in Tianjin, leading to the French withdrawal.

Ten years later, in 1895, the Japanese arrived. Where did they come from? Penghu. The Penghu County Government History Evolution website states: "On April 17, 1895 (the 21st year of Guangxu), Li Hongzhang signed the Treaty of Shimonoseki with Ito Hirobumi, formally ceding Taiwan and Penghu to Japan" [^20]. However, the Japanese forces had already occupied Penghu from March 23 to 25, predating the signing of the Treaty of Shimonoseki by three weeks. On March 26, 1895, Japan established the "Penghu Islands Administration Office" in Magong, with Rear Admiral Tanaka Tsunori as its first director. It was not until the treaty was signed on April 17 that they landed on the main island of Taiwan on June 17.

The strategic logic remained the same: **capture Penghu first, then advance to Taiwan**. The Dutch failed to do this in 1622. The Japanese succeeded in 1895.

## Cross-Sea Bridge Unites Six Towns into Penghu

![A photo of the cross-sea bridge in Penghu spanning the Houmen Channel between Baisha and Xiyu, taken in 2015. The first generation, completed in 1970, was the longest deep-sea bridge in Southeast Asia; the operational second generation was completed in 1996, with a total length of 2,494 meters. Photo: Wing1990hk.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Bridge_across_the_Houmen_Channel.jpg/1280px-Bridge_across_the_Houmen_Channel.jpg)
_The cross-sea bridge in Penghu spanning the Houmen Channel, June 30, 2015. Photo: Wing1990hk via Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bridge_across_the_Houmen_Channel.jpg)._

The Penghu County government began building the cross-sea bridge in 1965. The official website of the Penghu National Scenic Area states: "Construction of the cross-sea bridge began in 1965, and it was completed and opened to traffic in 1970"[^21]. The first generation bridge was 2,478 meters long and was the longest deep-sea bridge in Southeast Asia at the time.

The bridge connects the Houmen Channel between Baisha Township and Xiyu Township, which is characterized by deep water and strong currents. Before the bridge was built, Xiyu could only be reached by boat. After the construction of the bridge, four townships—Magong City, Hoxi Township, Baisha Township, and Xiyu Township—were connected by land. The remaining two townships (Wang'an and Qimei) remained in the central South China Sea, requiring travel by boat or plane.

The structure began to suffer severe corrosion in 1984 because it was constantly exposed to saltwater and strong winds. In 1996, the second generation bridge was completed and opened, with "the total length of the Penghu cross-sea bridge being 2,494 meters"[^22]. It has two lanes and a width of 13 meters. For the 26 years from 1996 until the opening of the Kinmen Bridge in 2022, it was Taiwan's longest cross-sea bridge.

> **✦** The physical significance of the cross-sea bridge is not "connecting two places," but rather "stitching six townships into one Penghu."

What was stitched together was not just roads. Before 1970, if you asked a person from Penghu where they were from, they might say, "I am from Xiyu Township" or "I am from Wang'an Township." After 1970, the identity of being a "Penghu resident" gained a concrete physical basis. The townships still exist, but the people within those townships share a common identity.

## The Flight That Disintegrated in 2002, The First Fireworks Display in 2003

On the afternoon of May 25, 2002, at 3:28 PM, China Airlines Flight 611 took off from Taoyuan International Airport bound for Kai Tak, Hong Kong. Fifteen minutes later, it disintegrated and crashed at an altitude of 34,900 feet, 23 nautical miles northeast of Magong, Penghu. All 225 people died in the accident[^23].

The summer tourism industry in Penghu was severely damaged. The following year, China Airlines held a "Ten Million Charm on Juku Island" event during the seventh lunar festival as compensation for the impact on Penghu's tourism. In 2003, the Penghu County government took over and organized the first Penghu Sea Fireworks Festival. The Wikipedia entry for the Penghu Firework Festival states: "To compensate for the tourism impact caused by this air disaster, China Airlines held the 'Ten Million Charm on Juku Island' event during that year's seventh lunar festival. The county government held the first 2003 Penghu Sea Fireworks Festival the following year (2003)."[^24]

From that year onward, Penghu had a representative summer event. In 2019, the fireworks festival was held 22 times, attracting about 420,000 tourists, which was the highest in history. COVID brought it back to zero, and when it resumed in 2022, tourism revenue exceeded NT$322 million.

However, the firework festival has a structural problem: it is only held in the summer. An interview with a reporter from a Penghu tourism business reported: "**Summer for work, winter for rest, this is the typical pattern of most Penghu businesses**" [^25]. April to September is the peak season, while October to March is the low season. During the low season, flights are often canceled due to the northeast monsoon, and most restaurants and B&Bs are empty. A reporter once wrote a more precise sentence: "**Every year, another B\&B changes management**."

How outsiders see Penghu: Blue skies and blue seas in summer. How people of Penghu see Penghu: Summer is for work; winter is the real Penghu. Vegetable farms are still growing crops in winter. The northeast monsoon blows all winter long. In the evening, Magong Central Street has no tourists, only locals. **The two times of Penghu overlap on those 141 square kilometers.**

## 19 Islands, 80,000 People: Only 20 Residents on Dongji Island

In 2005, the Penghu County government commissioned National Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology to conduct a comprehensive island survey, which confirmed a total of 90 islands. Prior to this, the number used was 64, based on surveys from the Japanese colonial era. The distribution of these ninety islands is as follows: Magong City has 7, Hoxi Township has 10, Baisha Township has 37, Xiyu Township has 3, Wang'an Township has 32, and Qimei Township has 1[^26].

Out of the ninety islands, only 19 are inhabited. The children's list from the Penghu National Scenic Area includes: Penghu Main Island, Tongpan Island, Hujing Island, Mudu Island, Jibei Island, Qiao Island, Yuanbei Island, Baisha Island, Dacang Island, Zhongtun Island, Yuweng Island, Xiaomen Island, Jiangjun'ao Island, Wang'an Island, Hua Island, Xiyu Ping Island, Dongyu Ping Island, Dongji Island, and Qimei Island[^27]. The remaining seventy-one islands are uninhabited, with a total area of only 3.02 square kilometers—smaller than one community in Magong City.

Dongji Island is an example. It is the largest among the Southern Four Islands. A record from KKday states: "Dongji Island is the largest island among the Southern Four Islands. During its prosperous period, it had over 3,000 residents and was known as 'Little Shanghai'; currently, there are only about 10 to 20 people living there."[^28] Why did the population drop from 3,000 to 20? Before the war, Dongji Island served as a transit point between Taiwan and Xiamen. Boats, traders, and tavern owners gathered on this island. After the war, with the two sides separated, trade routes were cut off, and the island lost its economic base, leading people to move away in groups.

The Southern Four Islands National Park was officially announced in 2014. The Wikipedia entry states: "The Southern Four Islands National Park is Taiwan's ninth national park and the second marine national park in Taiwanese history... Officially announced on June 8, 2014."[^29] The area includes Dongji, Xiji, Xiyu Ping, and Dongyu Ping islands, along with surrounding waters, covering 35,843.62 hectares. The Marine National Park Management Office specifically noted that Xiyu Ping Island "has a coral coverage rate of over fifty percent, which is the highest among the Southern Four Islands."[^30] When people left, the corals began to grow back.

The demographic structure of all Penghu County reflects this trend. At the end of 2023, the registered population was 108,000, ranking second to last nationally. However, the permanent resident population from the 2020 census was only 82,000, which is 77% of the registered population. The aging index is about 194%, with 18.97% aged 65 and over, and only 9.76% under 15. Magong City concentrates six tenths of the population (about 64,000), while Qimei Township has only 3,937 people[^31].

Fisheries have also declined. A Central News Agency report from April 20, 2024, stated: "In the last 20 years, the catch volume has declined by three-quarters, with the annual output value decreasing from NT$4 billion to NT$2 billion."[^32] The reasons cited are climate change, overfishing, and coral habitat degradation. Currently, aquaculture (cages farming of sea bass, grouper, etc.) accounts for about 1.53 billion NTD annually, making up two-thirds of the total fishery output. Nearshore catches have shrunk to only 730 million NTD. The _shikōji_ (stone corrals) of Penghu in the 1950s once contributed eighty percent of the catch; now, they serve only as cultural landscapes.

The green sea turtles of Wang'an Township tell a similar story. The "Penghu County Wang'an Island Green Sea Turtle Nesting Habitat Protection Area," established in 1995, is one of Taiwan's few relatively stable nesting sites for green sea turtles. However, conservation status remains precarious: the number of nesting females has declined annually from a peak of 19; only one female was observed nesting ashore in 2014; it rose to three females laying six nests in 2022; and the first female was sighted on shore in May 2024[^33]. After thirty years of conservation, the numbers are still fluctuating in single digits.

## 81.07%: Saying NO the Second Time

Returning to the beginning.

On September 26, 2009, the first local gambling referendum in Taiwan took place. The question was: "Should Penghu establish an international tourist resort with associated casinos?" The results were:

- Yes votes: 13,397 (43.56%)
- No votes: 17,359 (56.44%)
- Voter turnout: 42.16%
- The "No" side led by 3,962 votes[^34].

This was the second local referendum after the passage of the Civil Referendum Act. The law was designed so that gambling could only be legalized after passing a local referendum. Penghu was selected because it met the criteria: an outlying island, small population, tourism focus, and sufficient hinterland. Business tycoons had placed their bets. Shi Chao-hui (a Buddhist scholar) of the Anti-Gambling Alliance in Penghu said something after the vote count that has been quoted to this day: "The little shrimp defeated the big tycoons; the people of Penghu saved Penghu with their votes, and they saved Taiwan."[^35].

In the following two years, tourism actually boomed. Data tracked by reporters showed: "From 2009's anti-gambling referendum to 2011, tourist arrivals in Penghu increased by 15%."[^25]. Tourism grew without casinos. But this frame is too simplistic—the people of Penghu do not live off the narrative of "tourism growth brought by the referendum."

Seven years later, on October 15, 2016, another vote was held. The question was the same, but the numbers were different:

- Yes votes: 6,210 (18.93%)
- No votes: 26,598 (81.07%)
- Less than 31% of townships supported it[^36].

What happened in those seven years? Nothing much. There was no massive wealth creation, no solution to the winter off-season problem, no resolution to youth migration, and no fix for declining fisheries. The people of Penghu voted "No" a second time, with 25 percentage points more opposition.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The common online narrative is that "the people of Penghu oppose casinos because they fear public safety issues." But this statement reverses the cause and effect. The true core of the people of Penghu's opposition lies in Shi Chao-hui's words from 2009: "The people of Penghu saved Penghu with their votes." The subtext of the word "save" is that Penghu was originally slated to become something else, and the people of Penghu chose not to be that thing. One airport service staff member said in an interview with a reporter: "That is just a money game played by tycoons; it offers no real benefit to us locals in Penghu."[^37]. A local specialty producer was more specific: "Casinos emphasize being a complete package of eating, drinking, and entertainment. The 'tourists' who come to the casino are confined within the casino's facilities and services; who comes out to wander the streets? We simply cannot earn money after casinos are established."[^38]. **The object of saying NO twice was not the casino, but the right to choose "who defines Penghu."** When outsiders decide what you should become, you say no. Even if you cannot see where things will go next, you still say no first.

_Tianxia Magazine_ interviewed several second-generation residents who had returned to Penghu. What they are doing is deep tourism, ecological tourism, and environmentally friendly tourism—they are trying to grow something else in the void left by the casino vision. One quote that was frequently cited was: "Pursuing deep tourism and an eco-friendly environment is what they value."[^39].

In another in-depth interview, Liu Yi-yang of the Penghu Youth Federation said something closer to the core: "In fact, every child from Penghu is looking for a way to go home."[^40]. Every child who left Penghu to study or work on the mainland has a version of what Penghu should become in their mind. The voters are the accumulation of these versions.

## Vegetable Houses in Winter, the Northeast Monsoon Blowing Outside

Returning to the scene at the beginning.

Magong Central Street at four in the morning. There is water in Siyanjing (Four-Eyed Well), although it is no longer for daily drinking. The temple doors of Kaodai Tianhou Temple are closed, and the Shen Yourong stele from 1604 rests quietly in a glass case inside Qingfeng Pavilion. The Northeast Monsoon blows in from the northwest, blowing over walls built with _laogushi_ (coral stone) and basalt.

By the Guanyin Pavilion seaside, two hundred meters away, the stage of the summer fireworks festival is empty in winter. On Snake Head Mountain, twelve kilometers away at Fenggui, the castle built by the Dutch in 1622 is only a ruin. Thirty kilometers further, there are no cars on the Baisha Cross-Sea Bridge. A little further is Jubei Island, where 109 _shihu_ (fish traps) are exposed in heart and arc shapes during low tide. Further still is Qimei Island, where the two hearts of the twin fish traps are clearly visible above the sea surface at low tide.

Further south are the Southern Islands. On Dongji Island, 10 to 20 people are sleeping. The coral on Xiyu Ping Island is still growing. Within the entire area of the marine national park, spanning 35,843 hectares, there are few people and much coral.

The stone walls of every vegetable house were built later than that Dutch castle. Every vegetable house has also been in use longer than that Dutch castle.

The Northeast Monsoon is blowing outside. The people of Penghu learned this as children: **you cannot stop the wind, but you can decide what kind of wall to grow crops within.** Shen Yourong forced the Dutch to retreat in 1604. The Dutch moved to Tainan in 1622. French forces died at Mazuport in 1885. Japanese forces began taking Taiwan from Penghu in 1895. China Airlines Flight 611 crashed in 2002, and the fireworks festival was held in 2003. Anti-gambling referendums resulted in 56% opposition in 2009 and 81% in 2016. Penghu has repeatedly been chosen by others as an entry point, a springboard, a bargaining chip, a resort area, or a gambling site. **Every time, Penghu says: what you think is not enough; I think for myself.**

Winter. Inside the walls of a vegetable house, a cauliflower grows slowly in the leeward side. Outside are the Houmen Waterways, volcanic rock from 17.4 million years ago, nineteen inhabited islands and seventy-one uninhabited islands, 80,000 permanent residents, and two referendums.

It takes only 50 minutes to fly from Taipei to Magong. The next time you visit Penghu, do not just go in the summer. Go again in winter, and look at those stone walls. Look at what grows inside them.
