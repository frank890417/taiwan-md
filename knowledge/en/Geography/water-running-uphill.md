> **30-Second Overview:** "Water Flows Upstream" in Duduan Village, Donghe Township, Taitung County, is an irrigation ditch less than half a meter wide, yet it is one of Taiwan's most famous "counter-intuitive attractions." Ground measurements show the ditch actually slopes downward by approximately 0.5 to 0.6 degrees; however, the adjacent road has a steeper angle of inclination. When these two slope lines are viewed together, the brain adopts the more steeply inclined road as the horizontal reference plane, thus perceiving the less inclined ditch as "flowing upward"—this is an illusion of the visual system's reference frame, not a failure of gravity or magnetic fields. This irrigation channel was originally excavated by local Amis people; it was only after the domestic tourism boom of the 1980s that officials installed a stone monument to package it as a spectacle. In recent years, it has sparked a new wave of discussion due to online reviews calling it "Taiwan's most useless attraction."

In the tourism landscape of Taiwan's east coast, "Water Flows Upstream," located in Duduan Village, Donghe Township, Taitung County, is undoubtedly a presence full of tension. It is both what many tourists call the "most useless" attraction and one of the most vivid experimental grounds for geography and psychology. This irrigation ditch, only dozens of meters long and less than half a meter wide, is linked to the history of ethnic reclamation and a "visual error" accidentally designed by the terrain within the human perception system.

## Geographical Environment and Geological Background

"Water Flows Upstream" sits at the eastern foot of the Coastal Range, facing the Pacific Ocean. The geological structure of this area is complex, primarily composed of volcanic breccia and agglomerate. As the highest peak in the southern section of the Coastal Range, Mount Duduan is not only a sacred mountain for the Amis and Puyuma peoples, but its dramatic topographical fluctuations also create diverse slope characteristics in the local area.

The local ground surface is covered with a large amount of stone excavated during reclamation, which is the origin of the name "Atolan" (meaning "place of piled stones") used by the Duduan tribe. In such fragmented and uneven terrain, guiding water sources became an extremely challenging engineering feat. These unique geographical conditions provided a natural stage for the subsequent formation of the "slope illusion"[^1].

## Amis Survival Technology: The Origin of an Irrigation Ditch

While modern tourists view it as a spectacle, this ditch, locally known as the "Duduan No. 4 Irrigation Channel," was originally an irrigation project by the Amis people of the Duduan tribe[^2]. The local place name "Atolan" (Duduan) literally means "place of piled stones." To plant rice and dry-land crops on slopes covered in scattered rocks, the settlers first had to solve how to divert water from the foot of Mount Duduan into the fields. In an era without modern surveying instruments, the tribespeople relied on long-term observations of the terrain to guide water down the mountain following gravity. The gradient of the irrigation channel had to be kept extremely gentle—if it were too steep, the flow would erode the earthen embankments; if too slow, silt would accumulate and halt the flow. This almost exacting requirement for a "minute gradient" is precisely the physical foundation that allows it to serve as teaching material for optical illusions later on. The exact era of excavation and engineering details still lack cross-verifiable historical records; this history currently relies largely on local oral traditions and tourism literature, awaiting further verification through local chronicles or oral histories.

## The Brain's Betrayal: The Psychological Mechanism of the Slope Illusion

The "Water Flows Upstream" phenomenon is known in psychology as "The Slope Illusion," a typical type of perceptual distortion. When the human brain judges "horizontal" and "vertical," the vestibular system in the inner ear is only one clue; the "reference frame" provided by the visual environment often carries greater weight.

A science fair research project from Baosang Junior High School in Taitung County performed on-site measurements: the ditch itself slopes downward by about 0.5–0.6 degrees, while the adjacent road has a more obvious inclination, measured at between 3 and 5 degrees[^3]. When visitors stand beside the road, the brain unconsciously treats the more steeply inclined road as the horizontal reference line. Under this visual suggestion, the much shallower ditch is perceived as an "uphill" slope.

This phenomenon reveals the "reference frame dependency" of the visual system. When background cues in the environment are too strong, the brain prioritizes visual information, thereby suppressing the body's true perception of gravity. This is why even when we know rationally that water flows downward, our eyes continue to transmit the erroneous message of "flowing upward"[^4].

## Transformation from Agricultural Facility to National-Level Attraction

How did this irrigation channel transform into a nationally famous attraction? The key lies in the domestic tourism boom following Taiwan's economic takeoff in the 1980s.

At that time, the management office of the Eastern Coast National Scenic Area keenly captured the "counter-intuitive" nature of this landscape and erected a stone monument beside the ditch engraved with the words "Spectacle." This official positioning and naming imbued the site with a sense of mystery, elevating it from a simple agricultural facility to a must-visit destination for mass tourism. In an era before information was transparent, rumors regarding "magnetic field anomalies" or the "failure of gravity" added to the legendary status of this place[^5].

## Contemporary Tourism Reflection: The Aesthetics of "Useless Attractions" and Authentic Experience

With the development of social media and the popularization of knowledge, "Water Flows Upstream" has undergone an interesting shift in public opinion in recent years. On platforms such as Threads and PTT, the site is frequently listed at the top of "Taiwan's most useless attractions."

This shift in evaluation reflects a change in how contemporary tourists define a "spectacle." When the sense of mystery disappears, leaving only a ditch, some visitors feel disappointed. However, another perspective holds that this "sense of disappointment" is itself a precious travel experience—it forces us to confront the limitations of our perception and contemplate the gap between tourism packaging and authentic landscapes. This "useless attraction aesthetic" is gradually becoming a new cultural label, attracting groups seeking atypical travel experiences[^6].

## Environmental Protection and Local Coexistence

In recent years, controversies regarding land development around "Water Flows Uplam" have also drawn social attention. Due to its proximity to Gamuziban and the Duduan tribe, large-scale resort development plans have sparked discussions regarding threats to the local ecology and indigenous ancestral domains. This reminds us that while appreciating this visual magic, we should not ignore the true weight of this land as a carrier of ethnic culture.

The "horizontal" seen by the eyes is the result of a negotiation between the brain and its surrounding environment—which is actually entirely unrelated to the original reason why this irrigation channel was first excavated.
