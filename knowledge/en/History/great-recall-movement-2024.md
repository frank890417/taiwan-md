# The Great Recall: The Largest Recall Wave in History Where All 33 Cases Failed, Measuring Every Increment of Taiwan's Democracy

> **30-Second Overview:** In 2025, the people of Taiwan did something rare in the world: using the constitutional right of recall to launch a referendum-style accountability movement against more than one-quarter of the legislators. Through three waves of voting from July to August, not a single one of the 33 recall cases passed; the largest recall wave in history failed to remove even a single seat. But looking at the bigger picture: this movement was more like a stress test for direct democracy. The 55.83% voter turnout on the night of July 26 set a new record in recall history, while simultaneously measuring how high the recall threshold is and the cost a polarized society must pay. It removed zero seats, but it measured a great deal.

As night fell on July 26, 2025, vote counts began pouring in from all over Taiwan. On this day, the political fates of twenty-four KMT legislators and Hsinchu Mayor Kao Hung-an were handed to voters to decide all at once—twenty-five cases were voted on the same day, marking the highest number of simultaneous recall votes ever held in Taiwan's history. In the early evening, some constituencies briefly showed a lead for "yes" votes, and voices could be heard shouting during the recall groups' live streams. However, by late night, as the twenty-five reports were completed one by one, the conclusion was unanimous: none passed[^1].

The voter turnout that night surged to 55.83%, a new high in Taiwan's recall history[^2]. The largest mobilization of citizens in history resulted in a "zero seats removed" outcome. On a night where nothing was recalled, what was measured filled the entire film strip.

This movement later became commonly known as the "Great Recall." To understand exactly what it measured, one must first understand the increments on the scale.

## Recall Requires Passing Three Thresholds: 1%, 10%, and One-Quarter

In Taiwan, recalling an elected official requires sequentially crossing three numerical thresholds. The first is the "proposal" stage: the number of proposers must reach 1% of the total number of eligible voters in the original constituency. The second is the "signature collection" stage: the number of signatories must reach 10%. Only after passing these two stages is a case established and moved to a vote. The third is the vote itself—the "yes" votes must outnumber the "no" votes, and furthermore, the "yes" votes must reach one-quarter of the total number of eligible voters in the original constituency for the recall to pass[^3].

These three thresholds also determine who has the opportunity to be pushed onto the recall stage. Because at-large legislators are allocated based on the proportion of party votes and do not have an "original constituency," they cannot be recalled under this system[^4]. This is precisely why all thirty-one legislators facing recall votes in 2025 are district legislators. Those eligible to vote are voters who are at least twenty years old and have been registered in the constituency for at least four months. High school students aged eighteen cannot vote, nor can overseas citizens without a Taiwan household registration[^5].

The height of this third threshold was only adjusted to its current form in 2016. Before that, recalls used a "double-half" threshold: the voter turnout had to exceed 50%, and the "yes" votes had to exceed half of those cast for the recall to pass. During the 2015 "Ge Lan Wei" (Cutting the Appendix) movement to recall legislator Tsai Cheng-yuan, the voter turnout was only 24.98%; it failed to even reach the voting stage, and the recall automatically failed[^6]. In November 2016, the Legislative Yuan changed the threshold to "yes votes must outnumber no votes AND reach one-quarter of the eligible voters," removing the strict requirement for a 50% voter turnout[^7]. The proposals for this amendment came from multiple versions submitted by the New Power Party caucus and Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) legislators at the time, which were passed after being merged for review.

Ironically, Huang Kuo-chang—a representative figure of the New Power Party and one of the parties that proposed lowering the threshold—became the first legislator to be pushed to a recall vote under the new system in 2017. In that instance, he received 48,693 "yes" votes but failed to cross the threshold of 63,888 votes, thereby retaining his seat[^8]. The very people who lowered the threshold became its first test subjects. That time, the threshold was not met. This institutional debate over "how easily incumbents should be challenged" had already gone through a round of contention long before the Great Recall wave, planting the seeds for the 25% threshold seen in 2025 (for more, see [Taiwan's Elections and Party Politics](/en/history/taiwan-elections-and-party-politics)).

The ruler has been set: the question now is why the energy has accumulated to the point of needing to use this ruler—and that starts with the single-seat margin in the Legislative Yuan.

## A Parliament Divided by a Single Seat

The January 2024 legislative election resulted in a Legislative Yuan where no single party held a majority. The Kuomintang (KMT) secured 52 seats, the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) 51 seats, and the Taiwan People's Party (TPP) 8 seats, plus 2 independent seats leaning toward the Pan-Blue camp, totaling 113 seats[^9]. With only one more seat than the DPP, the KMT leveraged its cooperation with the TPP to send Han Kuo-yu to the position of President of the Legislative Yuan in the February 1 election, winning by a vote of 54 to 51[^10]. This parliament, where no three parties held a majority, has been a tug-of-war from day one.

This struggle quickly escalated from personnel issues to legislation: In May 2024, the Blue and White camps pushed for amendments to the _Legislative Yuan Power Act_, expanding the parliament's powers of investigation and interpellation and adding the "crime of contempt of Parliament." On the day of the third reading in late May, large crowds gathered outside the Legislative Yuan to protest, marking the beginning of what later became the "Bluebird Movement." The Executive Yuan filed a reconsideration motion, which was rejected by the Legislative Yuan on June 21 by a vote of 62 to 51[^11]. The controversy proceeded to the Constitutional Court; on October 25 of that year, Constitutional Court Judgment No. 9 ruled that several provisions (including the crime of contempt of Parliament and certain interpellation and investigation powers) were unconstitutional and invalid, but also determined that while there were flaws in the legislative procedure, they did not reach the level of unconstitutionality. This was a "partially unconstitutional" ruling, not a total reversal[^12].

What truly fanned the flames was the night of December 20, 2024, when the Blue and White camps joined forces to pass the third readings of three laws: The _Public Officials Election and Recall Act_ required photocopies of both sides of ID cards for recall petitions and increased the criminal penalty for forged signatures to a maximum of five years. The _Constitutional Court Act_ raised the threshold for deliberation in the Constitutional Court to at least ten justices. The _Organic Law of Budgetary Allocation_ significantly adjusted the distribution of financial resources between central and local governments; according to calculations by the Ministry of Finance, the central government would have to release an additional NT$375.3 billion to local governments[^13][^14]. One month later, on January 21, 2025, the Legislative Yuan passed the third reading for a budget that cut NT$207.5 billion and froze NT$260 billion from the central government general budget (these are two different actions and cannot be added together)[^15][^49]. Throughout the entire session, these battles over bills and budgets turned the parliament into a pressure cooker.

Before the scale could even begin to register, the needle was already shaking. The pressure that had accumulated in the chamber for a year spilled over the walls, first transforming into the "Bluebirds" outside the Legislative Yuan, and then into petitions being mailed into inboxes one after another.

## 1,300 Signature Forms in the Mailbox

The energy of the Bluebird Movement does not instantly transform into recalls. Chen Hsiao-wei, a pastor and leader of the recall effort against Niu Hsu-ting in Taoyuan, remembers the trajectory of that curve: "Taoyuan began collecting recall signatures last July, but within four and a half months, the mailbox only received 9 signatures for Tu Chuan-chi and 11 for Niu Hsu-ting. After the Bluebird Movement in December, we started receiving 100 to 400 forms every day. When I opened the mailbox in early February after the Lunar New Year, it was overflowing with over 1,300 forms." [^16] The sea of mobile phone lights on the streets took nearly seven months to settle into individual signature forms—each accompanied by a photocopy of an ID card—sent into mailboxes.

On January 22, 2025, UMC founder Robert Kao mobilized groups such as the Southern Society and the Taiwan Professors Association to establish the "Anti-Communist Taiwan Defense Volunteer Alliance," creating an umbrella organization for scattered recall groups across the country [^17]. Robert Kao himself plays two roles: the convener of the alliance, and the leader of the "Eliminate Black Xin" team for the Hsu Chiao-hsin recall case. The latter is just one of over thirty recall groups, not the entirety.

![Robert Kao speaking at a protest outside the Legislative Yuan on May 21, 2024](/article-images/history/recall-tsao-hsing-cheng-rally-2024.webp)
_Robert Kao speaking at a protest outside the Legislative Yuan on May 21, 2024; eight months later, he mobilized recall groups across the country to establish a volunteer alliance. Photo: TMYAO / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0._

The volunteers catching this wave of energy look different than those in the past. Field observations by reporters found that the proportion of female volunteers in various constituencies is as high as 70% to 90%, whereas previous recall volunteers were "at most 60% or 70%" [^16]. The age demographic has also shifted: from the junior high and high school students involved in the 2015 Sunflower Movement, to the fresh graduates involved in the 2020 Han Kuo-yu recall, the main force of 2025 consists of people in their 30s and 40s—a generation that interviewees describe as "almost all middle management in their respective fields." From the Sunflower Movement to the Bluebird Movement and now to these recall groups, a clear curve is visible showing a street-level generation de-studentizing and moving toward middle age (see [Social Movements and Civic Participation](/en/society/social-movements-and-civic-participation) for more). Some recall groups implement very strict vetting; for instance, "Shan Chu Wei Hai" requires volunteer applicants to record a video of themselves saying "Xi Jinping step down" to be kept on file for verification [^16].

This new batch of volunteers carries two distinct characteristics. One is an efficiency-oriented organizational culture. Alliance advisor Chien Chia-yu describes it: "In the past, our discourse would involve discussing with everyone 'why we should recall this legislator'; but now, recall groups seem like companies, emphasizing a KPI-driven mindset." The other is deeply personal conviction. Bang Bang-bang, a volunteer for the Banqiao "Great Deletion of Yuan" movement, remembers that when she decided to volunteer, her husband initially asked: "It won't work, will it? What can you change by yourself?" Xiao-shi, a Hong Kong immigrant and deputy leader of the Taoyuan recall group, said that participating in a recall is the third time she has truly felt like a Taiwanese person. The first sentence of _Tâi-gí_ (Taiwanese) she learned was "Recall to save Taiwan" [^16].

The cultural sphere has also been drawn in. A petition initiated by writer Yang Shuang-tzu grew from about 200 people in early February to 1,043 writers. The petition statement quoted Brodsky: "Literature has the right to interfere in politics until politics stops interfering in literature" [^18].

The first reading on the ruler is out: the mobilization energy is real, and it has set a record. Once that energy surges, both sides begin using the system as a weapon against one another.

## Who Launched the Great Recall? Both Sides Give Different Answers

The most fundamental question of this movement—who actually launched it—receives completely different answers from the two opposing camps.

The recall groups' self-narrative is that this is a spontaneous citizen action, independent of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), centered on the theme of "defending Taiwan against the CCP." This view has been echoed by some external observers; the _Taiwan Insight_ academic blog from Nottingham University described it as a "citizen-initiated" movement that is not merely a confrontation between political parties[^19].

The opposing narrative is different: Kuomintang (KMT) legislator Hsu Chiao-hsin pointed the finger at the ruling party in her recall defense statement, alleging that "DPP Legislative Yuan caucus leader Ker Chien-ming has repeatedly admitted personally that the Great Recall movement was planned and launched by him," calling it a "fake citizen movement manipulated by the DPP"[^20].

It should be noted that this is an allegation attributed to Ker Chien-ming via Hsu Chiao-hsin's defense statement, representing the opposing camp's view. As for verifiable public statements made by Ker Chien-ming himself, the only relevant remark was after the voting concluded on August 24: "The Great Recall is still a huge success; 1.3 million signatures prove that public opinion has risen"[^21]. The legislators facing recall mostly defended themselves by citing their political achievements; Fu Kun-chi listed in his defense statement having "achieved a 77.2% administrative satisfaction rate" and "creating the only five-star, nine-term consecutive streak in Taiwan"[^50]. KMT Chairman Chu Li-luan set the tone during a rally in Banqiao on July 19: "In the past, Taiwan has never had a ruling party launch a great recall to target the opposition; you won't find any other democratic country in the world... only President Lai dares to do this."[^22]

What can be confirmed are the structural facts: in all thirty-three recall cases, the legal proposers and lead organizers are all individual citizens. Conversely, the KMT's "using recalls to counter recalls" against DPP legislators originated from resolutions made during KMT caucus meetings[^53]. The forms of mobilization differ; whether this constitutes "political party manipulation" is a judgment left to the reader.

The Taiwan People's Party (TPP) occupies its own position: they publicly oppose this action, which they call an "evil recall," yet they also refuse to cooperate with the KMT to raise the threshold for recalls[^23]. During the voting period, TPP Chairperson Ko Wen-je, who was being held in the Tucheng Detention Center, received a recall voting notice. Huang Kuo-chang countered on Facebook: "I ask Lai Ching-te: what kind of black humor is this? Notifying Ko Wen-je that he has the right to vote, but not letting him actually vote!"[^24]

The second mark on the ruler begins here—measuring the depth of polarization: halfway through the test, there isn't even a consensus on who pressed the start button. And the most fierce battlefield of offense and defense is the petition signatures themselves.

## 1,784 Deceased Signatures vs. 160 Forgeries

First, let's clarify one thing: both camps had signature issues identified, but the two sets of records track different things.

One type is "deceased signatures": names of deceased individuals appearing on the signature sheets. This issue stems from discrepancies found during the verification of household registration data and does not necessarily imply intentional fraud. The other type is "forgery": someone using another person's personal information or impersonating them to sign a petition. This is an act of active fraud. These two have different legal natures and cannot be conflated.

In April 2025, the Central Election Commission (CEC) referred 41 recall cases involving forgery or deceased signatures to the Supreme Prosecutors Office for prosecution. Among these, 15 cases involved recalls against pan-Blue legislators, 17 against DPP legislators, 8 against pan-Green councilors, and 1 was the Kao Hung-an case[^25]. There is a linguistic trap here that must be exposed: regarding the same matter, different numbers will emerge depending on the verification stage and the unit used for calculation. Measured by "number of signatures," the 17 cases targeting the DPP involved 1,784 deceased signatures and 42 forgeries during the first-stage signature verification in April. The pan-Blue cases involved 12 deceased signatures and 160 forgeries, with the Lin Pei-hsiang case having the highest number of forgeries at 116[^26]. However, by the time the CEC officially audited them on June 21, the unit changed to "cases"—there were 16 cases involving deceased signatures and 12 cases involving forgery[^27]. A "signature" (件) is not the same as a "case" (案); any number cited must clearly state which stage and which unit of measurement it refers to.

The denominator should also be considered: all 17 cases targeting the DPP were found to involve deceased signatures, whereas in the 35 pan-Blue cases, only 15 involved such issues. When these numbers are placed side by side, readers can judge their significance for themselves; this article does not draw a conclusion on its own.

One case has already reached conviction: 11 KMT party workers, including Chuang Chan-kui, Deputy Chairman of the KMT Tainan City Branch, used party member rosters, filled in personal information without consent, and forged signatures to gather 3,000 proposals each for the recalls of Lin Chun-hsien and Wang Ting-yu within ten days, totaling 3,663 forged signatures. On July 10, 2026, the Tainan District Court sentenced Chuang Chan-kui to two years in prison with five years of probation under the Personal Data Protection Act, and ordered him to pay NT$600,000 to the public treasury. The other ten individuals received sentences ranging from six months to one year and seven months, with probation periods between two and five years. The ruling stated that such behavior not only violates the informational privacy of those whose identities were forged but also undermines the legitimacy of the democratic recall system[^28]. It is important to distinguish that the prosecution targets the act of forgery; normal citizens participating in petitions do not incur criminal liability.

The KMT has questioned whether investigators are "targeting the Blue and ignoring the Green," pointing out that the second-stage signatures for various pan-Blue legislator recalls totaled over 150,000 invalid entries, and questioning why they have not been investigated with equal vigor[^29]. The CEC responded that "as long as there are instances of death or forgery prior to signing, they will be formally reported for prosecution according to law," emphasizing that there is "no differential treatment"[^27]. At the time of this article's verification, the cases that have reached conviction or indictment are concentrated on the KMT party worker side. However, this may also reflect that most DPP-targeted recall cases were not submitted for review, resulting in a smaller sample size; both interpretations require more complete judicial statistics to reach a definitive conclusion.

The reading for the second column is completed here—the cost of polarization: even the numbers have two sets of books, and social trust depreciates accordingly. After six months of political combat, in the end, the supporters, the opponents, and the accused all walk into the same polling station.

## "Yes" Votes Surpassed the Threshold, Yet Lost to "No" Votes

The voting occurred in three waves. The first wave was on July 13, involving the recall of Nantou County Councilor Chen Yu-ling—the only case to reach a formal ballot under the KMT's "using recalls to counter recalls" strategy; the target was from the green camp, making its direction the exact opposite of the following two waves. The result was 12,160 "yes" votes and 5,867 "no" votes; the "yes" votes failed to reach the threshold of one-quarter of the eligible voters[^30].

The second wave occurred on July 26 with twenty-five cases: twenty-four KMT legislators plus Hsinchu Mayor Kao Hung-an. The national voter turnout was 55.83%, a record high in recall history. The "yes" vote mobilization rate was 23.75%, while the "no" vote mobilization rate was 32.08%[^2]. All twenty-five cases failed.

The motivation behind those 2.18 million "no" votes was not singular: there was a base of supporters for their local legislators—districts that the KMT had already won in 2024; there was reservation regarding the use of recalls as political tools; and there was an exhaustion stemming from a desire to return life to normalcy.

In this wave, seven cases actually saw "yes" votes cross the 25% threshold, but because the "no" votes were higher, the seats were defended. The vote margins for these seven individuals formed a spectrum: the narrowest defense was by Yeh Yuan-chih in New Taipei, who won by only 3,560 votes. The widest margin was Zheng Cheng-ling in Hsinchu, with a lead of 29,335 votes[^31]. Despite both being cases where "the threshold was met but the recall failed," the tightness of their fates differed by more than eightfold.

Kao Hung-an's case involved two different clocks running simultaneously: she had been found guilty in a first-instance corruption case and suspended under the Local Government Act, meaning at the time of the recall vote, she was an "acting mayor under suspension." This recall received 86,291 "yes" votes and 124,360 "no" votes, failing to pass[^32]. Had the recall passed, she would have been removed from office immediately and would not have been reinstated even if her conviction were overturned later. Because the recall failed, it instead preserved space for her to await judicial results—on December 16, 2025, the second instance overturned the corruption conviction, allowing her to apply for reinstatement[^33]. The political clock and the judicial clock intersected on a single person for a year and a half.

The third wave consisted of seven cases on August 23, all involving KMT legislators, held on the same day as the Nuclear Three extension referendum. It is important to distinguish: the "726" group refers to "seven cases that met the threshold but were rejected," whereas the "823" group refers to a different set of seven people and seven cases that "did not even meet the threshold"; these two groups of seven are entirely different individuals. On the same day, the Nuclear Three referendum received over 4.34 million "yes" votes, accounting for 74.17%—far exceeding the "no" votes—yet it failed to pass because it did not reach the threshold of one-quarter of the national electorate (approximately 5 million votes), with a turnout of only 29.53%[^34].

During the vote counting that night, TTV News provided [live coverage of the entire process](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nbVvhSZD1Y8). The third mark on the ruler revealed itself on election day: the threshold was not an abstract number, but 2.18 million "no" votes. Once the voting ended, the bills and reports had only just begun to arrive.

## The Battlefield Moves Back to the Legislative Yuan

Voting has concluded, and the stress test report is out. This report consists of four pages: the bill, the deadlock, the laws yet to be amended, and external interpretations.

The first page is the bill: the election administration expenses for the 31 legislator recall cases totaled approximately NT$400 million; the Nuclear Three referendum held on the same day cost an additional NT$1.14 billion; and the approximately NT$31.01 million for the Kao Hung-an case was covered by local budget allocations from the Hsinchu City Government[^35]. A movement that failed to recall anyone has, first and foremost, delivered a stack of invoices.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Reading the "Great Recall" as "a failed mobilization that changed nothing" is the most convenient and common narrative—the political map didn't shift, seats weren't swapped, yet the bills arrived in abundance. However, this reading uses "results" as the sole metric, precisely missing what the stress test was actually doing: it measures how much weight a bridge can bear. The zero results and the record-breaking voter turnout coexist, providing the most honest set of readings from this test.

The second page is the deadlock. Ker Chien-ming called the outcome "the Great Recall remains a huge success"[^21]. Conversely, Yu Ying-long, Chairman of the Taiwan Public Opinion Education Foundation and a critic within the green camp, described it as a "catastrophic democratic civil war" and demanded that the ruling party apologize to society[^36]. The political landscape has not actually changed: Lai Ching-te announced "four adjustments"[^37], the Cho Jung-tai Cabinet reshuffled sixteen members[^38], Cheng Li-wen was elected KMT Chairperson in October[^39], and Ker Chien-ming completed his term amidst waves of criticism, with a regular election and handover to Tsai Chi-chang scheduled for February 2026[^40][^51]; meanwhile, the opposition alliance still holds the majority in the legislature. The most concrete consequence of this deadlock falls on public welfare: for the first time in history, the 2026 central government general budget has crossed into a new year without being passed, causing government policy execution to stall[^41].

The third page covers the laws that have yet to be amended: the threshold provisions that sparked the greatest controversy—such as "recall affirmative votes must exceed the number of votes cast for the elected official"—were not addressed during the wave of amendments at the end of 2024[^42]. As of July 2026, when this article was fact-checked, relevant drafts remain on the Legislative Yuan's pending list and have not entered plenary session for deliberation[^52]. Brookings scholar Kharis Templeman judged after the vote that "Pandora's box has been closed again" regarding the recall tool, suggesting it will be used less frequently in the future[^23]. The box may be closed, but the lock—the threshold—has not yet been changed.

The fourth page covers external interpretations; most international analyses focus on the "deadlock between the Presidency and the Legislature": the government remained just as divided before and after the recalls. Templeman warned that "Taiwan cannot afford another 30 months of political deadlock"[^23]. As for overseas reactions, these should be viewed independently and clearly labeled by their nature—these are statements from foreign governments regarding Taiwan's internal affairs and should not be equated with domestic Taiwanese perspectives. Since April 30, spokespersons for the PRC's Taiwan Affairs Office have repeatedly claimed that the Great Recall created "green terror" within the island[^43]. The Mainland Affairs Council refuted this on July 23, stating that Beijing is engaging in "naked and overt interference, without any concealment"[^44]. After turning the final page of the report, some things remain that cannot be quantified by numbers.

## Taiwan Developed on Film

Three waves of voting, thirty-three cases, and not a single one passed.

In terms of battle results, this largest wave of recalls in Taiwan's history was almost an empty victory. In the history of Taiwan's constitutional development, there has still only been one legislator successfully recalled to this day—Chen Po-wei in 2021[^45]. If measured by "how many people were removed," the limits of this tool are crystal clear.

However, on that same piece of film, something else was developed. The 55.83% voter turnout on the night of July 26 set a new record in recall history. Observations from the Taiwan Lab found that as recall groups mobilized, they gradually realized that "protecting Taiwan against China" narratives were too far removed from the general public. Consequently, they shifted the focus of their dialogue toward livelihood issues such as rental subsidies, long-term care, and education, actively reaching out to people across different social strata—a phenomenon not commonly seen in past election battles[^46]. Where this entire generation of citizens, who have learned how to organize and how to speak with those holding different stances, will go after the movement exits the stage remains an open question for _The Reporter_ to this day; no one can write a definitive answer for them[^47].

An author from _Taiwan Insight_ borrowed an old saying from over 170 years ago to summarize that complex sentiment. In 1848, U.S. President James K. Polk described the territorial expansion following the Mexican-American War as: "Everyone was glad with it, but no one was proud of it." The author says this sentence holds equally true for Taiwan today[^48].

On the late-night vote-counting screens, the words "Not Passed" lit up one after another. The next day, the petition tents were dismantled one by one. What remained was that record-breaking turnout figure and an entire generation of volunteers who had learned for the first time how to organize strangers. Thirty-three failures are the result of this test. The true value of the test lies in the fact that it measured so much all at once—the energy of mobilization, the height of the threshold, and the cost of polarization. In the end, what was measured far outweighed what was removed.
