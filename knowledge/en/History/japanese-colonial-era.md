# Japanese Colonial Period

> **30-Second Overview:** Following the Treaty of Shimonoseki in 1895, Japan ruled Taiwan for 50 years, implementing comprehensive reforms through the Governor-General's Office system, including infrastructure, education, and industrial development. Simultaneously, the _Kominka_ (Japanization) movement was promoted to assimilate the Taiwanese people. During this era, various anti-Japanese movements were launched by the Taiwanese until Japan's surrender at the end of World War II in 1945.

From 1895 to 1945, the fifty years of Japanese rule in Taiwan brought rapid modernization alongside colonial suppression and cultural assimilation policies. The interplay of these two forces constitutes an important underlying layer of contemporary Taiwanese society and culture. [^1]

## Colonial Administrative System

The Office of the Governor-General of Taiwan was located in Taipei and served as the highest governing authority in Taiwan. The Governor-General was appointed by the Emperor and held concentrated executive, legislative, and military powers. Under the "Rule by Ordinance" (the 63rd Law), the Governor-General was authorized to enact decrees with legal force. Furthermore, the _Baojia_ system under the police regime organized every household into a mutual surveillance network, ensuring that colonial control penetrated the grassroots level.

During the initial phase of Japan's takeover in 1895, they faced armed resistance from the Republic of Formosa. Local armed struggles continued to erupt until the "Xilaian Incident" in 1915, after which armed resistance largely ceased and colonial rule entered a relatively stable period of modernization.

Administratively, Taiwan was divided into various prefectural offices (_shucho_) such as Taipei, Hsinchu, Taichung, Tainan, and Kaohsiung. This coincided with land surveys to confirm property rights across the island, providing an institutional foundation for modern industry and taxation.

## Modernization Efforts

Regarding transportation infrastructure, the North-South Railway was fully operational by 1908, connecting Keelung to Takao (modern-day Kaohsiung), making it one of the largest colonial railway systems in East Asia at the time. During the same period, the ports of Keelung and Kaohsiung were reconstructed into modern commercial ports, significantly enhancing Taiwan's foreign trade capacity.

In terms of water conservancy and agriculture, the Chianan Irrigation System—designed by Japanese engineer Hatta Yoichi and constructed over a decade (1920–1930)—transformed 150,000 hectares of farmland in the Chianan Plain into a stable irrigation zone. Hatta Yoichi is still highly commemorated in Taiwan today. [^2] Additionally, the Sun Moon Lake hydroelectric power plant supplied electricity to the entire island.

In education, the _Kōgakkō_ (public school) system saw the **school enrollment rate** for school-aged children rise from less than 5% at the start of colonial rule to approximately 71% by the end of the period (according to 1944 statistics). It is important to note that "literacy rate" and "enrollment rate" are different indicators: the Japanese colonial government's literacy statistics were based on Japanese reading and writing proficiency, which ran parallel to the traditional Chinese character literacy system; these two standards should not be conflated. Taipei Imperial University (now National Taiwan University), established in 1928, was the predecessor of Taiwan's higher education institutions, though opportunities for Taiwanese students to advance or find employment were consistently limited by ethnic discrimination.

One of the drivers of modernization was **Gotō Shinpei** (Civil Administrator, 1898–1906). Based on his "biological theory of colonial rule," he used scientific surveys as a precursor to gradually transplant Japanese systems, laying the framework for Taiwan's railways, land surveys, and public health.

## Social Movements and Cultural Resistance

Political resistance took on new forms in the 1920s. The "Petition Movement for the Establishment of a Taiwanese Parliament" (1921–1934), led by Lim Hsien-tang (Lin Xian-tang), sought political participation within the colonial system, submitting fifteen petitions to the Imperial Diet. Chiang Wei-shui (Jiang Weishui) co-founded the Taiwan Cultural Association with Lim in 1921, focusing on cultural enlightenment to promote national consciousness; he later formed the Taiwanese People's Party in 1927 to promote political organization. [^3]

The **1923 Police Incident** was a major crackdown by colonial authorities on political movements: the Japanese authorities arrested core members of the Parliament Petition Movement (including Chiang Wei-shui) on charges of violating the _Public Peace Police Law_. A sensational debate took place in court, which ultimately resulted in guilty verdicts. This incident significantly increased the social visibility of the petition movement and accelerated the fragmentation of Taiwanese political consciousness. The **Taiwanese Communist Party** was established in Shanghai by individuals such as Hsieh Hsueh-hung (Xie Xuehong) as an auxiliary to the Japanese Communist Party, advocating for Taiwanese national independence; however, it was swiftly suppressed by colonial authorities and disbanded by 1931. [^4]

In 1929, Japanese scholar **Yanaihara Tadashi** published _Taiwan Under Imperialism_, analyzing the exploitative structure of colonial Taiwan through the lens of capitalist political economy. It remains one of the most frequently cited critical works on colonial history.

The 1930 Wushe Incident was the largest indigenous armed resistance against Japanese rule during this period. The Seediq leader Mona Rudao led approximately three hundred tribesmen in an uprising; the subsequent Japanese suppression and policy responses sparked widespread controversy.

In literature, writers such as Lai He, Yang Kui, and Lü Heruo wrote in both vernacular Chinese and Japanese to express critiques of oppression and an identification with the land, establishing the local tone of Taiwan's New Literature movement.

## The _Kominka_ Period and Post-War Era

After the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, the colony entered a wartime mobilization system, and the _Kominka_ (Japanization) movement was launched on a massive scale.

The use of "National Language" (_Kokugo_) restricted the use of Taiwanese; "name changes" encouraged the adoption of Japanese names; Shinto shrine worship was mandated; and Taiwanese men were conscripted as military dependents or soldiers. It is estimated that over 200,000 people served or worked in support roles during WWII.

In 1945, following Japan's defeat, Taiwan returned to the governance of the Republic of China. The modern infrastructure, educated talent, concepts of the rule of law, and administrative efficiency left behind from the Japanese period served as important starting points for Taiwan's post-war development. Conversely, the complexity of ethnic identity (the cultural gap between the Japanese-speaking generation and the groups arriving from mainland China) became one of the roots of post-war political conflict in Taiwan.

## Historiographical Perspectives: Colonial Modernization vs. Colonial Exploitation

Interpretations of the Japanese colonial period remain a subject of ongoing debate in academia. "Colonial Modernization Theory" emphasizes the positive legacy of infrastructure, education, and health systems, arguing that Japanese rule laid the material foundation for Taiwan's industrialization. "Colonial Exploitation Theory," on the other hand, points out that land surveys stripped indigenous peoples and small farmers of their traditional land rights, that the export structures of the sugar and rice industries served the needs of the Japanese Empire rather than Taiwan's development, and that _Kominka_ policies systematically destroyed the transmission of Chinese culture. Local Taiwanese historiography (such as the works of Wu Mi-cha and Wakabayashi Masao) tends to integrate both perspectives to avoid reductive affirmation or negation.

**Note on Terminology:** In Taiwanese historiography, "Japanese Rule" (_ri-zhi_) and "Japanese Occupation" (_ri-ju_) reflect different historical stances; the former is considered more neutral and academic, while the latter emphasizes the nature of colonial occupation. This article uses "Japanese Rule," without pre-supposing a political stance.
