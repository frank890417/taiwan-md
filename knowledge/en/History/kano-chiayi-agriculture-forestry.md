> **30-Second Overview:** In 1931, the Chiayi Agricultural and Forestry School baseball team represented Taiwan at Japan's Koshien Stadium, becoming the first Taiwanese team to reach the finals of the Summer Tournament and ultimately finishing as runners-up. The team consisted of Han, Indigenous, and Japanese students. This is not merely an underdog sports story; it is a story of how a group of students under colonial rule were arranged on the same field, and how they made the entire island feel as though the outcome was their own business. [^1] [^2] [^3]

On July 23, 1931, approximately 15,000 spectators crowded into Taipei's Yuanshan Baseball Stadium. Chiayi Agricultural and Forestry defeated Taipei Commercial 11 to 10 in the Taiwan representative playoff, earning their ticket to Japan's Koshien. On that same day, radios broadcast the sounds of the game across the island. On August 21, they lost to Aichi Commercial in the Koshien finals, finishing as runners-up. Although this team did not win the championship, they became known thereafter as "The Peerless Kano" (_Tianxia Jianong_). [^1] [^2]

This contrast is worth noting: Kano's most important game was not a victory, but a "nearly there" runner-up finish. It allowed Taiwan to appear on the national stage of the Japanese Empire and prompted later generations to continually ask whether what was being seen back then was truly Taiwan, Chiayi, or simply a school team that happened to play exceptionally well.

## An Agricultural and Forestry School: Finding a Common Language on the Dirt First

Chiayi Agricultural and Forestry School was founded in 1919 with the mission of cultivating agricultural talent. The baseball club was established in 1928. Baseball was originally a leisure activity for the Japanese in the colony; after 1919, it was integrated into school physical education, giving Taiwanese students gradual access to the sport. With the establishment of the Taiwan Sports Association in 1920, domestic competitions began to gain more stable organization. Based on oral history interviews, the emergence of baseball in Taiwan is closely linked to Japanese colonial rule. It first entered schools and Japanese communities, then was adopted by Taiwanese students for competition and practice, eventually growing into a sport that no longer belonged solely to the colonizers. [^2] [^3] [^9]

The uniqueness of Kano lies not in the fact that it "naturally" brought three ethnic groups together. The composition of Han, Indigenous, and Japanese members described in official exhibitions occurred within the framework of schools and competitions provided by the Japanese colonial system. Players still lived within different social hierarchies. While the field allowed them to be on the same team, it did not eliminate the power disparities of the colony. [^3] [^4]

This is also where the concept of "harmony among the three races" is most easily oversimplified. It can be a beautiful name given by later generations to describe cooperation, but it cannot be retroactively used to claim that society was already equal at the time. The reason Kano's story carries such weight is precisely because this brief moment of fighting together coexisted with institutional inequality outside the field.

Kano's coach, Kondo Hyotaro, was originally a Koshien coach for Matsuyama Commercial School in Japan. According to records from the Chiayi City Memory Bank, players recalled his training as being almost Spartan-like: repetitive daily practice, refining basic movements until they were flawless, and then identifying the individual talents of each player. Kondo did not just give students "passionate" slogans; he used extremely specific training to turn a team that struggled for even a single win into a squad capable of surviving in nationwide competitions. [^2] [^5]

> **Curator's Note:** The Kano spirit is not "everyone can succeed as long as they work hard." It is more like a question: When the rules are not written by you, can you still develop your own way of playing within them?

## Wu Ming-chieh's Hand: Securing Taiwan's Representation for Kano First

In July 1931, Kano first played in the Taiwan selection tournament at Taipei's Yuanshan Baseball Stadium. Pitcher Wu Ming-chieh (吳明捷) threw a no-hitter during a game against Taichung First Middle School, which official records mark as the first perfect game in Taiwanese baseball history. By July 23, Kano defeated Taipei Commercial 11 to 10 to secure the right to represent Taiwan. [^1] [^2]

That final was not a clean line of victory. Kano had to rely on defense, speed, and an offense that clawed back point by point to secure the final slot against Taipei Commercial. In photos preserved by National Chiayi University, Luo Bao-nong (羅保農) scores at home plate, Wu Ming-chieh receives an award, and players return to Chiayi to offer thanks at the Chiayi Shrine. These photos pull "representing Taiwan" away from being an abstract title and back to a group of people wearing baseball uniforms. [^1]

After securing representation, Kano departed from Keelung on August 5 aboard the _Takasago Maru_ bound for Japan. There is one detail in this journey that is easily overshadowed by cinematic narratives: the players did not depart from an abstract "Taiwan," but from Keelung Port by ship, carrying the expectations of their school, their families, and the local society of Chiayi toward a field defined by the rules of a Japanese national tournament. [^1] [^5]

The Koshien tournament opened on August 13. Kano defeated Kanagawa Commercial, Sapporo Commercial, and Kokura Technical, reaching the finals on August 21. Historical photos from National Chiayi University record them lining up before the final and their eventual receipt of the runner-up shield. [^1] [^4]

In the final, they lost to Aichi Commercial and did not bring the championship flag back to Taiwan. However, "runner-up" was not a consolation prize at the time: it was the first time a Taiwanese team had reached the finals of the Koshien Summer Tournament, and the last game played by colonial students on a Japanese national stage. Indigenous literature notes that four of Kano's starting nine were from Taitung, and Luo Bao-nong became a key hitter of that tournament with a .533 batting average. [^4]

## Koshien is Not a Neutral Field

Writing about Kano as "Taiwanese defeating the Japanese" is dramatic but inaccurate. Kano encountered Japanese schools in both the Taiwan selection tournament and Koshien, and they were named, trained, and represented within the colonial system. The team's own Japanese students, coaches, and Taiwanese students cannot be simply placed into two opposing camps. [^3] [^5]

Similarly, describing it as "the colonial government successfully creating ethnic integration" is incomplete. Research papers on the film _KANO_ point out that the movie has made Kano a subject of discussion regarding post-colonial identity. The values of cooperation in the film provide an imagination of Taiwanese agency, but they also require addressing how the film reconfigures the experience of Japanese colonialism. [^6]

This contradiction does not need to be smoothed over. Kano's runner-up finish can simultaneously be a product of colonial rule, the hard work of Taiwanese students, and a moment where Indigenous players were recruited and seen; it is also the starting point for later Taiwanese people repurposing external systems into collective memory. History is not a choice between "glory" and "oppression." It often ties both to the same ball.

## After the Team Left, the Story Remained in Taitung and Chiayi

Kano's influence did not end when the final whistle blew in 1931. Records from the Chiayi City Memory Bank note that Chen Keng-yuan (陳耕元) returned to Taitung to teach baseball to children, and Yang Yuan-hsiung (楊元雄) also provided voluntary training for the Hongye Little League team. This thread, picked up by Kano players and local coaches, ensured that the "Kano spirit" was not just a movie slogan, but something sustained through the passing of skills from person to person. [^2]

This thread also connected Chiayi and Taitung. Kano's players did not just leave their names in their own hometowns; they brought their training methods, match memories, and understanding of the ball to the next generation of children. Later little league teams did not need to replicate 1931, but they could borrow an imagination from that team: that "local places can also take the stage." [^2] [^4]

After Kano, baseball in Taiwan often played two roles simultaneously: it could be a routine school practice, or it could be packaged by the government as national glory on the international stage. The National Archives Exhibition placed the Hongye Little League, Jinlong Little League, and professional baseball on the same historical line from _yeyu_ (informal baseball) to _bangqiu_ (organized baseball), reminding us that a team's wins and losses never belong solely to the players; they are also used by different eras to explain "who we are." [^3]

This legacy was not exclusive to Kano. Indigenous literature places the Nenggao Group before Kano: this team, composed of Indigenous players, went on an expedition to Japan in 1925, and Luo Dao-hou (羅道厚) later became the first Taiwanese player to join Japanese professional baseball. When Kondo Hyotaro went to the eastern part of Taiwan to find players, he was building Kano along this pre-existing path of Indigenous baseball. [^4]

Therefore, Kano should not be told as a story of Japanese coaches "discovering" Taiwanese talent. A more accurate way to say it is: people from different regions and ethnic groups in Taiwan were already playing; Kano brought these scattered paths into a single school team, and Kondo's training pushed them toward Koshien. Who was seen depended on who had the opportunity to enter that stadium. Who is remembered also depends on whether later generations are willing to write their names back in.

## In 2014, the Movie Brought the Runner-up Back to the Streets

In 2014, the film _KANO_, produced by Wei Te-sheng and directed by Ma Chih-hsiang, was released. The production moved Kano from alumni associations, local museums, and baseball archives onto the silver screen of popular culture. After seven weeks, the box office exceeded NT$300 million, and in connection with the film's premiere activities, a statue of Wu Ming-chieh pitching was installed at the Chiayi City Roundabout fountain. [^2] [^5] [^6]

The statue represents an interesting turning point. In 1931, Wu Ming-chieh was the captain receiving awards and the ace pitcher in photographs. In 2014, he became a physical presence passed by pedestrians every day near the city roundabout. History is no longer just kept in school archives but is placed on the daily transit routes of citizens. [^1] [^2]

This kind of preservation also carries risks. When a person is made into a statue, their complex identity can easily be reduced to four words: "never give up." When a multi-ethnic team is filmed as an inspirational movie, the specific experiences of the colonial system and Indigenous players may recede into the background. This is why analyses from _Taiwan Insight_ both affirm the film's power in reclaiming baseball and Taiwanese identity, while also organizing criticisms regarding the beautification of colonial modernity. [^6]

The film brought Kano into the memory of a new generation of viewers, but it also changed how people imagine it. The real 1931 did not have every line of dialogue found in the movie, nor did it have a clean arc that could organize colonial experience into "never giving up." Researchers therefore remind us that when watching _KANO_, one should maintain a post-colonial distance: respect its power to reclaim memory, but also recognize how it selects, compresses, and reorganizes history. [^6]

The fact that Kano was remade into a film does not mean that 1931 has finally found the single correct version. On the contrary, the movie gives more people the chance to return to names originally preserved only in alumni photos, local memories, and baseball history: Wu Ming-chieh, Chen Keng-yuan, Tuo Hong-shan (拓弘山), Luo Bao-nong, Lan De-he (藍德和), and the other teammates who did not stand at the center of the poster. [^1] [^4]

> **Curator's Note:** A movie allows Kano to be seen again, but true preservation is not turning players into myths; it is letting their identities, their era, and their differences remain together.

## A Commemorative Ball Brings 1931 Back to Chiayi

Kano's history exists beyond movies and text. In 2024, marking the 100th anniversary of Koshien Stadium, the Hanshin Koshien Historical Museum curated a special exhibition on Taiwan and Chiayi Agricultural and Forestry, displaying the "Kano Koshien Match Commemorative Ball for the Kanagawa Commercial Game." In 2026, when the president of Hanshin Electric Railway returned to visit Chiayi, the Chiayi City Government once again placed this ball within the narrative of urban exchange. [^7]

A ball does not weigh much, yet it can layer several places together: a school in Chiayi, a stadium in Japan, an opponent in Kanagawa, and two cities that are still exchanging artifacts and stories a hundred years later. It is more complicated than the four words "The Peerless Kano," and closer to history, because what remains on the ball are specific opponents, not abstract glory. [^3] [^7]

The National Development Archives also preserve a 1937 document regarding subsidies for the repair of the Chiayi City Baseball Field, amounting to NT$1,752. The field opened in 1918 and was later used by the Chinese Professional Baseball League (CPBL). This document moves Kano's story away from "that one Koshien trip" and back to how a city builds and maintains ballfields so that the next game can continue to happen. [^3]

Because of this, the core objects of Kano are not just a runner-up shield. Boat tickets, photos, commemorative balls, a pitcher's statue, and a repaired baseball field each preserve different versions of memory. Only by putting them together can one see how Kano transformed from a single match into the public history of Chiayi.

## Why a Runner-up Stays in Taiwan Longer Than a Champion

The most enduring aspect of Kano might be precisely that it did not achieve a perfect ending. It did not win the final game at Koshien, it did not turn colonial limitations into an overnight miracle, and it did not make "harmony among the three races" automatically equal to equality. It simply sent a school team from Chiayi to the final game of a Japanese national tournament in the summer of 1931. This was not the only time Kano walked that path: research data shows that Kano represented Taiwan at Koshien four times, in 1931, 1933, 1935, and 1936. While the 1931 runner-up finish is the most legendary, the subsequent appearances remind us that Kano was not a summer story that accidentally stumbled into history, but a school that continuously treated baseball as part of school life. [^8]

But that "nearly there" was enough to change an island's imagination. Baseball originally entered Taiwan via the Japanese colonial system, but it was later used by Taiwanese people to record their own speed, bodies, localities, and names. After Kano, the Hongye Little League, Jinlong Little League, professional baseball, and players abroad continued to push this sport toward new international stages. The National Archives Exhibition calls this line the transition from _yeyu_ to _bangqiu_; it does not truly suggest that only the name changed, but that the people using the sport changed. [^3]

Thus, Kano is not merely the "first success story" in Taiwanese baseball history. It is more like a cracked mirror: one side reflects the field arranged by a colonial empire, while the other reflects the paths run by Taiwanese students there. What readers ultimately remember may not be the scores of 11-10 or 19-7, or the runner-up shield, but that harder question—if a stadium was not originally built for you, how do you still bring it back to your own city after you have left the field? In 2026, the Chiayi City Government recorded the visit of the Hanshin Electric Railway president to Chiayi to view Kano artifacts; this connection spanning nearly a century demonstrates that Koshien is no longer just a distant venue from 1931, but a local memory that Chiayi continues to use to tell its own story. [^7]
