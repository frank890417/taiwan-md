> **30-Second Overview:** In late 1786, a local conflict in Daliqi, Changhua, escalated into one of the largest civil rebellions during the Qing period in Taiwan. The actions of figures such as Lin Shuangwen, Zhuang Datian, and Jin Niang forced the Qing court to mobilize troops across provinces and subsequently restructured policies regarding ferry ports, _tunding_ (militia), and city fortifications. Rather than treating this merely as "the Qing army suppressing a rebellion," this article investigates how a war fundamentally changed the empire's method of governing Taiwan.

In late 1786, Lin Shuangwen of Daliqi, Changhua, became embroiled in a local conflict that may not have been initiated by him. By January 1787, this conflict had captured Changhua County and drawn the military attention of the Qing Empire to the western half of Taiwan. [^1] In the spring of 1788, Lin Shuangwen was arrested and transported to Beijing in a prisoner cart. [^2]

This history is most easily written as "the Qing army suppressing a civil rebellion," but what truly warrants questioning is something else: why could an island viewed by the empire as a frontier—one where they were long unwilling to invest heavy governance costs—force the Qing court to mobilize troops across the sea for over a year, ultimately changing its policies on ports, militia, and city defenses? [^3]

> **Counter-intuitive Core Sentence:** While the conclusion of the Lin Shuangwen Rebellion appeared to restore the status quo, it actually ensured that the Qing Empire could no longer govern Taiwan in its previous manner.

## From Banning Secret Societies to Local Loss of Control

Lin Shuangwen was originally from Pinghe, Zhangzhou, Fujian. He moved to Taiwan with his father in his early years and began reclaiming land in Daliqi, Changhua. StoryStudio's compilation of his post-arrest testimony indicates that he joined the _Tiandihui_ (Heaven and Earth Society) in the eighth month of the 51st year of Qianlong's reign, originally to provide mutual support within local society. Later, when local officials conducted crackdowns on secret societies, bailiffs took the opportunity to extort residents; when the homes of Lin Shuangwen's companions, including Lin Pan, were burned by government troops, the conflict shifted from association and investigation into armed resistance against the authorities. [^4]

One must not simplify the _Tiandihui_ as a suddenly appearing rebel organization. Qing-era Taiwan was a frontier settlement society where local administrative capacity, ethnic relations, and land interests were deeply intertwined. When public authority expanded its searches under the guise of crackdowns, scattered local grievances found a common enemy. Research abstracts from the Airiti Library also list the overly aggressive arrest methods used by officials as a major reason for the escalation of the event. [^5]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Lin Shuangwen did not start at the center of the historical stage. He was pushed to the front after the local order had already failed. If we restore this sequence, the event is no longer a story of a hero suddenly rallying the masses, but rather a story where local conflicts first lost the institutions capable of containing them, forcing individuals to become banners.

In the eleventh month of the 51st year of Qianlong's reign, Lin Shuangwen raised his flag against the officials. In early 1787, the insurgents captured Changhua and killed the Taiwan Prefect, Sun Jingsuì. Responders in the north then moved into Zhùqiè (modern-daylukang), while in the south, Zhuang Datian gathered followers, creating a separate front of warfare around the Fengshan area. Historical exhibitions by the National Taiwan Literature Center link these sieges and responses together, showing that the event was not a single village riot, but a rapidly interconnected regional war. [^1]

## Why One City Held Out for So Long

Lin Shuangwen's forces conducted a long siege of Zhuluo City. English reports indicate that after Zhuluo fell, it was besieged again for about six months; the reinforcements sent by the Qing court made limited progress and were forced into a defensive posture. This detail is important because it shows that the war did not end immediately upon the arrival of Qing troops in Taiwan; rather, local cities, food supplies, reinforcements, and terrain collectively determined the course of battle. [^3]

![Relief of Zhuluo in "Maps of the Pacification of Taiwan," depicting the battlefield where Qing troops broke the siege and the city's topography. Image is a public domain file from Wikimedia Commons, used directly via hotlink. [^10]](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1787_to_1788_Lifting_the_Siege_at_Zhuluo.jpg)

Zhuluo was later renamed Chiayi, which is usually recorded as a result of the Qianlong Emperor rewarding the residents for defending the city. While this place name is still in use today, it leaves behind another question: when a city receives a new name because it assisted the authorities in defense, how are the survival choices of local residents rewritten into an imperial narrative of loyalty and righteousness? [^4]

The Qing court dispatched naval and land admirals and governors to oversee the troops across the sea, yet they still could not end the fighting quickly. It was not until Fu Kang'an led elite troops mobilized from other provinces to Taiwan that the situation gradually reversed. Because different sources record troop compositions and numbers inconsistently, this article does not treat a single figure as the sole answer, but confirms the fact pointed to by all sources: the Qing court had to deploy cross-provincial military force and spend over a year to regain control of Taiwan. [^3] [^5]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The significance of Zhuluo lies not just in "holding out." It showed the empire that Taiwan's cities, ports, and local societies must be governed together; one cannot rely solely on a written ban. Subsequent policies regarding ports, militia, and city defenses were the results of this city pushing the weight of local society back in front of the empire.

## The Archives Contain More Than Just Lin Shuangwen

Research from the National Palace Museum shifts the gaze from the leaders to the strategists, advisors, and grassroots figures. Articles point out that Qing archives record not only battles but also many individuals who played pivotal roles in the event. These figures may not remain in later textbooks, but they allow us to see how a civil rebellion operated through intelligence, testimony, local networks, and military division of labor. [^6]

One of the most compelling characters is Jin Niang, a woman from the Tamsui community in Fengshan. Based on her testimony after being escorted, StoryStudio describes how she was invited by Zhuang Datian's camp to serve as a female strategist due to her practice of healing with talismans and her ritual activities. She was not a passive "indigenous female representative" appearing in the event, but someone who entered the war through her own skills, beliefs, and local relationships. [^4]

Jin Niang's story also reminds us that archives are not synonymous with transparent truth. Her testimony consists of official text formed after her arrest; the narrative contains both her own defenses and the limitations left by the interrogation system. Being able to read about her does not mean we have heard her fully. This is the distance that must be maintained when Palace Museum researchers trace small figures through vast amounts of memorials and archives. [^6]

Research published by the Institute of Taiwan History at Academia Sinica discusses how the Qing court produced war imagery in "Maps of the Pacification of Taiwan." The study points out that these images were not merely battlefield records; they were produced under the orders of the Qianlong Emperor to propagandize the military achievements of the Qing court. The indigenous people, cities, and terrain depicted in the frames are all viewed through the lens of imperial victory. [^7]

## How One Map Turns War into Victory

!["Maps of the Pacification of Taiwan (VII): Capture of the Rebel Chief Lin Shuangwen," 1793, painted by Yao Wen-han et al., National Taiwan Art Museum collection. Image is a public domain file from Wikimedia Commons, used directly via hotlink. [^12]](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Capture_of_the_rebel_chief_Lin_Shuangwen.jpg)

![Battle map of Fangliao in "Maps of the Pacification of Taiwan," arranging mountains, forts, and armies within a single field of vision. Image is a public domain file from Wikimedia Commons, used directly via hotlink. [^11]](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1787_Battle_at_Fangliao.jpg)

According to descriptions provided by the National Taiwan Art Museum via Google Arts & Culture, the _Maps of the Pacification of Taiwan_ were originally sketched by Fu Kang'an under imperial orders, then redrawn by court painters into twelve battle maps, completed in 1793, and presented for imperial inspection. This set of images arranges battlefield geography, architecture, flora, people, and military dynamics within a single field of vision, transforming the war into evidence of victory for the Emperor to observe. [^8]

The captured Lin Shuangwen is not the only protagonist in the image. The larger scene consists of mountain ranges, ambushes, horses, flags, and the government troops surrounding him. This composition places individual failure within a narrative of restored imperial order, making the "capture" the punctuation mark for the entire war. However, the more complete the image, the more one must ask what it does _not_ draw: the burned houses, the starving citizens, the followers forced into participation, and those dead who left no names. [^7] [^8]

The "Traces of the Lin Shuangwen Incident" preserved in the National Digital Memory Bank describes the event as a widespread and large-scale anti-government incident in Taiwan during the Qianlong era, noting that the excessive crackdown by local officials was one factor in its escalation. While such local cultural materials can supplement memories outside the battlefield, terms like "revolution" or "anti-Qing" should be cross-referenced with Palace Museum, academic, and museum data rather than being taken as the sole explanation. [^9]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> What an empire leaves behind most often is how it describes its victory. When reading Taiwan's history, one must also shift their gaze to the people at the edges of the victory maps. The battle maps fill the terrain and troop numbers densely, but they do not leave equally clear spaces for burned houses, starving citizens, or nameless dead.

## After the Event, Taiwan Was Reorganized

Lin Shuangwen's arrest did not mean that governance returned to how it was before 1786. Research included in Airiti Library categorizes the changes following the event into three areas: the Qing court opened formal ferry ports in northern Taiwan, implemented the _tunding_ (militia) system, and relaxed city fortification policies. These three measures addressed transportation, military mobilization, and urban defense, respectively, showing that the Qing court began to view Taiwan as a strategic space requiring long-term management. [^5]

Palace Museum articles also note that after the incident, the Qing court readjusted ferry ports, indigenous borders, and the _tunfan_ (militia for indigenous people) system, while actively building fortifications. More notably, the event led to a massive increase in memorials, imperial edicts, and military archives involving Taiwan. In other words, the Lin Shuangwen Rebellion changed not only Taiwan's institutions but also the density with which Taiwan was recorded by the empire. [^6]

These policies were not all stories of progress. The _tunding_ system incorporated indigenous groups into military and governance arrangements, while simultaneously creating new social hierarchies and population pressures. Opening up city fortifications made cities more defensible, but it also meant Taiwan was placed under tighter military control. Policies can bring both security and restriction; they cannot be summarized by the single word "development." [^5]

## Why It Is Still Worth Rereading Today

The Lin Shuangwen Rebellion is not the same historical experience as the February 28 Incident, the White Terror, or democratization; one should not force them into a straight line for the sake of continuity. However, it poses a question that recurs throughout Taiwan's history: how does local society respond when central power treats a region as a frontier to be managed? And when the center is forced to increase its governance capacity, whom do the new institutions protect, and whom do they restrict?

Lin Shuangwen himself was eventually sent to Beijing; Jin Niang and others left only fragments in testimonies and archives. The Qing court wrote this war as one of the "Ten Great Campaigns," while the local area left behind walls, steles, and place names. Later researchers find those suppressed voices within the gaps of the archives. This is not a simple opposition between "official" and "folk," but an acknowledgment that multiple memories can exist for the same event simultaneously. [^2] [^4]

Therefore, the importance of the Lin Shuangwen Rebellion lies not just in it being one of the largest rebellions during the Qing period. It is more like a stress test, revealing that the Qing Empire's original method of governance was no longer sufficient for Taiwan. After the rebellion was suppressed, ports, militia, fortifications, and archives all changed together. The island did not gain freedom because of this, but it was forced to become clearer within the empire's calculations.
