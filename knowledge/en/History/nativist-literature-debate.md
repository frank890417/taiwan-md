> **30-Second Overview**: In April 1977, a special issue of _Cactus_ (仙人掌) magazine ignited the Nativist literature debate in Taiwan. That August, Peng Ke, chief editor of _Central Daily News_, wrote "Without Discussing Humanity, How Can There Be Literature?" in the _United Daily News_, specifically naming Wang To, Wei Tien-ts'ung, and Chen Ying-chen. Three days later, Yu Kwang-chung published "The Wolf is Coming" in the supplement of the same newspaper, linking the "worker-peasant-soldier literature" advocated by some in Taiwan to Mao Zedong's Yan'an talks. From July to November alone, official sources and the _China Times_ and _United Daily News_ published 58 articles attacking Nativist literature; on August 29, the Kuomintang held its "Second Literary Conference," where President Yen Chia-kan called on writers to "adhere to an anti-communist literary stance." At the National Army's literary conference in January 1978, Wang Sheng called for uniting Nativist sentiments and expanding love for one's roots into love for the nation, effectively bringing the debate to a halt. Ten years later, Yeh Shih-tao published _A General Outline of Taiwan Literary History_ (臺灣文學史綱), and Nativist literature became a central pillar in the narrative of Taiwan's literary history; forty years later, following the passing of Yu Kwang-chung, that controversial label was once again brought up for discussion.

## April, A Special Issue

In April 1977, the second issue of _Cactus_ (Xiānrénzhǎng) magazine was published under the theme "Nativism and Reality" with the subtitle "Where is Nativist Culture Heading?", featuring 11 articles presenting opposing viewpoints[^1]. The debate began with this very issue.

The authors in this issue included Wang To, Chiang Hsun, Tang Wen-piao, Wei Tien-ts'ung, Chen Ying-chen, Yin Cheng-hsiung, and Chu Hsi-ning. Wang To, Wei Tien-ts'ung, and Chen Ying-chen stood on the side of nativism, while Yin Cheng-hsiung and Chu Hsi-ning criticized nativist literature. The list included writers, poets, and critics alike; this was an internal clash within literary circles, and at its onset, no one expected it to escalate into a political case.

Wang To's article, "It is 'Realist' Literature, Not 'Nativist' Literature," served as the fuse for the entire debate. He argued that the term "nativism" (_xiangtu_) easily evokes imagery of urban-rural opposition, regionalism, and nostalgia literature, suggesting that "realist literature" would be a more accurate term. A month later, writing under the pseudonym Li Zhuo in _China Forum_, he published "Trends in the Development of 20th Century Taiwan Literature," linking the movement to protect Taiwan's seat in the UN and Taiwan's withdrawal from the United Nations to the awakening of the younger generation and the popularity of nativist literature, stating that "literature should correctly reflect social contradictions"[^2].

In the same issue, Yin Cheng-hsiung's "Where Do the Bell Sounds Come From in a Graveyard?" began by discussing one of Wang To's short stories, claiming that nativist literature faced the "crisis of becoming a tool for expressing consciousnesses such as hatred and animosity"[^3]. Meanwhile, Chu Hsi-ning's "Where to Return? How to Return?" accused certain commentators of having separatist tendencies and being suspected of supporting Taiwan independence[^4].

This is the meaning behind the statement that "the 1977 April nativist literature debate officially exploded": the flashpoint was not a single article, but rather several different stances speaking out simultaneously within the same issue of the magazine.

## Two Hats Within Three Days

The literary debate raged for four months, only moving beyond the internal circles of the literary world in August.

On August 17, 1977, Peng Ke, then Chief Editor of _Central Daily News_, published "Without Discussing Humanity, How Can There Be Literature?" in the _United Daily News_ weekend supplement, serialized over three days in three parts: upper, middle, and lower[^5]. This is a crucial detail: **Peng Ke held a position at _Central Daily News_, yet his article was published in _United Daily News_**. Using his status as Chief Editor of _Central Daily News_, he named Wang Tuo, Wei Tian-tsung, and Chen Ying-zhen in the _United Daily News_ supplement, criticizing them for "failing to distinguish good from evil and only discussing class," and claiming their views were linked to the Communist Party's theory of class[^6].

One sentence in the article stated his position very bluntly:

> I do not approve of literature degenerating into a political tool, and I even more strongly oppose literature degenerating into an enemy's tool. If one fails to distinguish good from evil and only discusses class, without acknowledging universal humanity, where is the literature?[^7]

Three days later, on August 20, Yu Kwang-chung published "The Wolf is Coming" in the _United Daily News_ supplement[^8]. The article did not name names; instead, it opened with: "After returning to the country for half a month and meeting many literary friends, the most alarming topic everyone discussed was: 'Someone in Taiwan is already openly advocating for worker-peasant-soldier literature!'"[^9] He then cited Mao Zedong's 1942 "Talks at the Yenan Forum on Literature and Art," suggesting that several points in Mao's words "seem to coincide somewhat" with certain domestic "literary criticisms" in recent years[^10].

The article concluded as follows:

> To cry "the wolf is coming" when no wolf is seen is to invite trouble. To see the wolf and not cry "the wolf is coming" is cowardice. The problem lies not in the hat, but in the head. If the hat fits the head, it is not called "being labeled," it is called "finding one's head."[^10]

This phrasing left the question of "who is the wolf" to the readers and caused the phrase "finding one's head" to be read as a threat. In the same paragraph, Yu Kwang-chung also asked rhetorically: how many hats such as "refined guest," "comprador," or "worshipper of foreign things" have been thrown at domestic writers by the opposition in recent years?[^10] In his writing, it was the opposition who first placed the hats on people.

At the time, Yu Kwang-chung was teaching at the Chinese University of Hong Kong; his opening remark "After returning to the country for half a month" referred to this trip to Taiwan[^11]. Since then, the Nativist Literature Debate has often been used to ask one question: At that moment, was the poet Yu Kwang-chung an anti-communist intellectual issuing a warning based on his own convictions, or was he acting as an informant for the Party-state?

After that hat fell, scholar Chen Ming-cheng described the literary scene at the time: For a moment, the heavy "hats" (labels) metaphorically likened to "blood-dripping blades" caused a state of alarm throughout the literary world, permeated by a murderous, bloody atmosphere[^12]. Shortly after the publication of "The Wolf is Coming," the first issue of _Poetry Tide_ (_Shi Chao_), run by Gao Zhun, was banned, leaving the Nativist Literature camp in even greater fear[^10].

## July to November, 58 Articles

According to data from Yang Pi-chuan, from July 15 to November 24, 1977, the Kuomintang (KMT) government and two major newspapers, _China Times_ and _United Daily News_, published a total of 58 articles attacking Nativist literature[^13]. The scale of this debate far exceeded the norm in literary circles.

Nativist literature did not remain silent, and Wang Tuo's counterattack came quickly. Starting on September 10, he published a series of three articles titled "Embracing the Healthy Earth" in _United Daily News_ to rebut Peng Ke's criticisms, expose the plight of farmers and workers, and point out that Taiwan's economic growth was built upon the foundation of low rice prices[^14]. Later, the collection that compiled articles from both sides was the _Collection of Discussions on Nativist Literature_, edited by Wei Tien-ts'ung and published in 1978; it included "The Wolf is Coming" as well as articles by Yin Cheng-hsiung and Chu Hsi-ning[^15].

Hu Chiu-yuan's attitude is worth noting separately. This veteran intellectual from the party-state camp published "Talking about 'Human Nature' and 'Nativism' and Such" in _China Magazine_ to criticize Peng Ke, calling on the government not to dance to Yu Kwang-chung's tune by suppressing Nativist literature. He wrote: "If someone reports that 'the wolf is coming,' one must also take a look—bring in an expert to see if it is truly a wolf, or perhaps just a small deer?"[^16]

On August 29, 1977, the Kuomintang held the "Second National Literary and Artistic Symposium" at the Jiantan Youth Activity Center in Taipei, with over 270 participants. None of the writers considered to be "problematic" were invited[^17]; this absence was itself part of how the meeting's tone was set.

During the meeting, President Yen Chia-kan demanded that writers "adhere to an anti-communist literary stance." The meeting's conclusion emphasized the need to "coordinate with national policies, take the same steps as the anti-communist and nation-saving efforts, submit to the Three Principles of the People, and coordinate with the Chinese Cultural Renaissance Movement," while requiring works to "exert a free and humanistic spirit, and eliminate slave-like, materialistic class literature"[^18]. According to the recollections of Wang Chien-chuang, who was present, an official from the Garrison Command in charge of culture warned at the meeting that regarding those who do not heed the government's advice, "it is not that the government will not act, it is just that the time has not yet come"[^19].

## "What is Nativism" Was a Battlefield in Itself

While the debate appeared to be a clash between two opposing factions, there was actually no consensus on what "nativism" (_xiangtu_) even meant. This is why it proved much more difficult to resolve than typical literary disputes.

One side, represented by Yeh Shih-tao, equated "Taiwanese nativist literature" with the entirety of "Taiwanese literature," arguing that nativist literature should use Taiwan as a center to view the whole of world literature. Chen Ying-chen also criticized the Kuomintang, but believed that Taiwan's history and literature should be understood from the perspective of Chinese nationalism; he argued that what Yeh Shih-tao and others called "Taiwanese nativist literature" should be renamed "Chinese literature in Taiwan." The magazine 《夏潮》 (_Summer Tide_) held a similar stance. While both sides advocated for nativist literature, their historical foundations differed, and their definitions of the term "nativism" were worlds apart[^23].

Despite both opposing the Kuomintang, Chen Ying-chen and Yeh Shih-tao stood on opposite sides regarding where nativism should lead. In May 1977, amidst the heat of the debate, Yeh Shih-tao published 〈An Introduction to the History of Taiwanese Nativist Literature〉 in 《夏潮》. He explicitly proposed a "Taiwanese consciousness," arguing that Taiwan's nativist literature should consist of works written with Taiwan at their center—viewing world literature from a Taiwanese standpoint—and maintained that nativist literature must reflect the historical experiences of colonization and oppression faced by the people of Taiwan[^24].

Under the oppressive atmosphere of martial law at the time, the wording of this article was already quite circuitous, yet the concept itself represented a bold step forward. Phrases like "Taiwanese consciousness" and "with Taiwan at the center" substantively directed the meaning of "nativism" toward "Taiwan," foreshadowing the wave of the rise of "Taiwanese literature" in the 1980s and 1990s. The article quickly drew accusations centered on "separatism" [^24]; however, according to the research of Sung Tse-lai, veteran writers such as Yeh Shih-tao and Chung Chao-cheng were unwilling to get embroiled in the conflict and soon fell silent or withdrew from the debate, and official literati rarely criticized them directly[^32].

## The Poetry World Had Almost No Part in the Debate

In debates where novelists were the primary combatants, the situation of the poetry world is often described as "engaging in simultaneous clashes," but this description needs correction.

Members of the [Lishi Poetry Society](/en/art/li-poetry-society/) themselves have pointed out that they played almost no role in promoting the return to roots movement and the development of _xiangtu_ (nativist) literature in the 1970s, nor in the subsequent nativist literature debates[^25]. While the Lishi Poetry Society had a tradition of local realism, its primary discourse at the time focused on the language and canons of poetry itself, rather than the struggle over literary directions.

Another group often used for comparison is the Dragon Clan Poetry Society. Founded in 1971 by initiators including Hsin Mu, Lin Huan-chang (born 1939), Chiao Lin, Chen Fang-ming, and Kao Hsin-chiang, the group championed a nationalistic style with the slogan: "We beat our own gongs, we drum our own drums, and we dance our own dragons." The poetry journal _Dragon Clan_ ceased publication in May 1976, meaning it was already gone by the time the debates broke out[^26].

Yu Kwang-chung, a representative of the modernists in new poetry, stood on the anti-nativist side when he published 〈The Wolf is Coming〉; his position is particularly worth examining. Others in the anti-nativist camp included Peng Ko and Yin Hsueh-man[^27]. Part of the shock caused by 〈The Wolf is Coming〉 stemmed from the author's status within the poetry community. At the literary symposium on August 29, Yu Kwang-chung served as a member of the presidium, while Peng Ko served as the convener for the fourth group[^27].

## Co-option: Nativism Becomes Patriotism

The debate did not end with a victory for either side. Instead, the authorities employed a different tactic: co-option.

In January 1978, the "National Armed Forces Literary Convention" was held in Taipei. Admiral Wang Sheng, Director of the General Political Warfare Bureau of the Ministry of National Defense, emphasized the need to unite through _xiangtu_ (nativism), expanding the love for one's native soil into love for the nation and the people[^20]. According to accounts by attendee Tseng Hsiang-to, Wang Sheng also stated that there was nothing inherently wrong with "pure nativist literature," and that more radical young writers should be understood and united rather than having "red hats placed on all of them" [^20]. This convention was viewed as the official termination of the critique against nativist literature, placing a temporary pause on the nativist literary debates[^21].

Song Tse-lai's compilation mentions three factors regarding why the authorities shifted their stance: First, Ren Zhuo-xuan, a senior figure in the KMT party-state, believed that nativist literature did not violate the Three Principles of the People; in August 1977, he wrote an article stating that nativist literature was "completely different" from the worker-peasant-soldier literature of the Communist Party. Second, Hu Chiu-yuan opposed government intervention in the debate. Third, following the Zhongli Incident on November 19, 1977, the authorities no longer had the capacity to attend to the literary world[^20].

As a result, the term _xiangtu_ survived, but its meaning changed. In the official version, what was permitted to exist was "pure and anti-communist nativist literature," reincorporating the discourse of nativism into the official framework[^21]. In the version held by the nativist literary camp, _xiangtu_ wrote about farmers, workers, prostitutes, fishermen, and colonial economic structures. The same term, but two entirely different contents.

This is also why the debate did not truly end after it concluded in 1978. It merely shifted from the literary circles to the arenas of publishing and academic research.

## Ten Years Later, This Term Was Written into Literary History

Ten years after the debates, Yeh Shih-tao published _Outline of Taiwan's Literary History_ in 1987, expanding his discourse—which began with "An Introduction to the History of Taiwan's Nativist Literature"—into a complete literary history[^24]. The novels that were once branded as "tools of the enemy" during the debates became indispensable works for anyone reading Taiwan's modern literature. [Huang Chun-ming](/en/people/huang-chun-ming-taiwanese-literary-master/) wrote 〈The Days of Watching the Sea〉, 〈Sayonara, Goodbye〉, and 〈The Taste of Apples〉[^22]; Yang Ching-chu wrote about workers, and Wang Tuo wrote about fishermen[^9]. Each of these writers focused on their own specific niche, which later formed the backbone of Taiwan's literary narrative.

On the poetic side, researcher Tsai Ming-yen argues that when Wu Sheng published his "Impressions of My Homeland" series of poems in 1972, both form and theme still largely followed the characteristics of Modernism; his _xiangtu_ (nativist) poetic style was only truly established after the "Soil Chapters" (1974) and "Speaking to Children" (1977) series[^28]. Tsai also believes that Wu Sheng's nativist poetry was completed before the Nativist Literature Movement itself; it was when he gazed at the external homeland with intuitive artistic sensibility, rather than constructing verses through ideological consciousness, that he shaped what remains a unique paradigm of nativist poetry in literary history[^29].

That debate did not result in a clear winner or loser, but it left behind a pillar. From _Outline of Taiwan's Literary History_ to the localization movement after the lifting of martial law, postwar Taiwan's literature has been supported by that very pillar—the one built by this group of people who endured under martial law in 1977. The description of this process in the article [Postwar Taiwan Literature](/en/art/postwar-taiwanese-literature/) can be read alongside this text.

## The Debate Did Not End; It Only Changed Battlefields

During the debate, due to the Kuomintang's propaganda regarding Chinese nationalism, intellectuals were accustomed to self-censorship; discourse that emphasized "Taiwanese subjectivity" did not dare to surface formally. It was not until the 1980s, as the _dangwai_ (outside the party) movement fought for democracy and freedom, that the conflict between Chinese stances and Taiwanese stances among Nativist writers officially erupted, evolving into the "Taiwanese Consciousness Debate" starting in 1983 [^30]. After the 1980s, the history of Taiwanese literature narrated by Taiwanese nationalists treated Nativist literature as the starting point for the localization of Taiwanese culture, viewing it as having laid the foundation for a Taiwanese consciousness with nationalist tendencies.

By the 1990s, as politics became more liberalized and the struggle over unification versus independence intensified to the point where public debates on ethnic and national identity were virtually without taboo, another interpretation emerged: Nativist novelists were Chinese nationalists, and the Nativist literature debate of 1977–1978 was an internal strife and confrontation among Chinese nationalists [^31].

Neither of these interpretations is wrong, but what they reveal is more noteworthy than the conclusions themselves. The generation of the 1980s read Nativist literature as the starting point of Taiwanese consciousness; the generation of the 1990s read it as internal strife among Chinese nationalists. From the same set of texts, separated by a decade or more, opposite affiliations were read.

The reason lies not only in the literature itself: the mainstream on the Nativist side during the debate consisted of leftists concerned with the grassroots, most of whom identified with Chinese nationalism [^32]. When Song Tse-lai organized this history, he pointed out that those participating in the debate, whether they supported or opposed Nativist literature, held a Chinese consciousness; he believed this made official denunciations difficult and forced [^32]. He also noted that the only relatively unique figure was Yeh Shih-tao: Yeh was also someone with a Chinese consciousness, but he advocated that Nativist literature must be centered on Taiwan and possess a Taiwanese consciousness [^32].

[Chen Ying-chen](/en/people/chen-yingzhen/)'s position in the debate best illustrates these divergences. On one hand, in 〈Establishing the Style of National Literature〉, he criticized Zhu Yan's remarks, questioning how someone with no affection for their own land or the people around them could truly care about a suffering China. On the other hand, in 〈The Blind Spot of "Nativist Literature"〉, he criticized Yeh Shih-tao's "Taiwanese consciousness," emphasizing that the very foundation of this "Taiwanese consciousness" was indeed "Chinese consciousness" [^33]. Facing the authorities, Chen Ying-chen and Yeh Shih-tao both carried the banner of Nativist literature. But as soon as one asked the question, "Which _xiangtu_ (native soil)?", the two men stood on different sides.

## Forty Years Later, That Hat

With the passing of Yu Kwang-chung in 2017, this period of history has been re-examined. To this day, no consensus remains on his legacy: was what he wrote a piece of literary criticism, or an act of political identification? Reflecting on it in 2004, Yu Kwang-chung himself said that at the time he had "lost control of his emotions, and not only were my words crude, but my tone was also fierce," and that his political analogies and insinuations were "overextended"; however, he also denied being instructed by the Kuomintang[^11]. The "hat" (label) placed upon the Nativist literature camp meant the risk of publications being banned or even people being arrested at the time, yet ten years later, it became a ticket to entry into literary history.

The shape of that hat has also changed over the years. Today, when discussing Nativist literature, we speak of a school of thought in literary history, shifts in writing techniques, and experiments with Taiwanese languages, rather than a political injustice requiring vindication.

There is one detail worth mentioning once more at the end: Peng Ko was the chief writer for the _Central Daily News_ at the time, but the article was published in the _United Daily News_; 〈The Wolf is Coming〉 was also published in the supplement of the _United Daily News_. When the literary symposium was convened on August 29, an editorial in the _United Daily News_ titled 〈Current Literary Policies〉 stated that under the umbrella of "combat literature," there was "no such thing as 'Nativist literature' or 'realism,' let alone 'worker-peasant-soldier literature'"[^27]. This may be the layer of this debate most easily overlooked: **it began in a magazine, but it was amplified into a political event on the pages of newspapers.**

To measure what this debate left behind, perhaps the most honest approach is to calculate the merits of literature and politics separately. Regarding literature: Huang Chun-ming's novels brought the people of fishing villages and small towns into the vision of Taiwanese readers; Chen Ying-zhen, Yang Ching-chu, and Wang Tuo turned labor and factory life into experiences that could be carried by the novel. Regarding politics: it demonstrated how a literary debate can be amplified into an ideological project, and showed why the authorities during the martial law period needed to use formats like literary symposiums to co-opt a specific term.

This discourse "quickly triggered questioning and refutation from other writers using 'separatism' as their main theme," and led to the 1987 publication of _A General Outline of Taiwan Literary History_, see [Good Citizen Cultural Action Association](https://www.ccat.tw/topic-article/906).

[^30]: [Taiwanese Nativist Literature Debate - Wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hant/%E5%8F%B0%E7%81%A3%E9%84%89%E5%9C%9F%E6%96%87%E5%AD%B8%E8%AB%96%E6%88%B0) — Records how, due to the Kuomintang's Chinese nationalism propaganda at the time, intellectuals practiced self-censorship and dared not officially voice discourse emphasizing 'Taiwanese subjectivity'; it was not until the 1980s, driven by the opposition movement's struggle for democracy and freedom, that the conflict between the Chinese position and the Taiwanese position officially erupted, evolving into the 'Taiwanese Consciousness Debate' starting in 1983.

[^31]: [Research on the Nativist Literature Debate](https://www.scribd.com/document/922705682/%E9%84%89%E5%9C%9F%E6%96%87%E5%AD%B8%E8%AB%96%E6%88%B0%E4%B9%8B%E7%A0%94%E7%A9%B6) — Records how Taiwanese nationalists developed another understanding in the 1990s: that nativist novelists were Chinese nationalists, and the 1977–1978 Nativist Literature Debate was an internal strife and confrontation among Chinese nationalists; this interpretation contrasts with the view of the 1980s that saw nativist literature as the foundation for the development of Taiwanese consciousness.
