> **30-second overview:** Taichung Provincial Administration Hall was built in phases beginning in 1913 and completed in its present scale in 1934; after the 1920 reorganization of local administration, it became the administrative core of Taichung Province. The main building follows a Neoclassical/Second Empire style with a street-corner configuration, forming an official complex together with ancillary halls and a conference hall building. It was officially designated a national historic monument in 2019. What is most worth observing is not only its white façade but how the same building first served administrative power and was later incorporated into urban cultural preservation, restoration craftsmanship, and public use negotiations. [^1] [^2] [^3]

## First, Look at Where It Stands

Walking around the area near Taichung City Hall, Taichung Provincial Administration Hall first strikes visitors not by its era but by its location. The building stands at the intersection of major streets, facing the road with a symmetrical façade; within the urban official compound layout of the Japanese colonial period, it formed the center of administrative and financial power together with the city hall, the Taiwan Bank, and the post office. The National Cultural Heritage Administration database records this location in its designation value, because an official compound is not just any building erected on an arbitrary plot of land—it must allow administration to arrive, be seen, and operate through roads and other institutions. [^3]

This street-corner configuration is easily obscured by today's photographic gaze. Cameras usually aim at the central entrance, the piano nobile wall, and the roof, but do not necessarily capture how the building guides the street toward the porch. A street corner is a spatial syntax: pedestrians approach from two roads, first encountering symmetry, axes, and stairs, then meeting the management at the doorway. The solemnity of the building does not come solely from decoration but from its pre-arranged human movement. The official compound here does not need to explain itself to every passerby; the façade, scale, and entrance have already declared its representation of a certain system.

Taichung Provincial Administration Hall is therefore related to the formation of Taichung City, but it should not be written as a naturally grown urban backdrop. Administrative zoning, road construction, land use, and public facilities determine which activities gather around the official compound, and also determine which residents must come here to handle matters, pay fees, apply, or wait. The history of the building is not only on its façade but also on every person who enters the door, whether forced or willing.

## A Building Grown in Stages into a Province

Taichung Provincial Administration Hall is not a single, completed work. Official records state that it was constructed in phases under the supervision of the Civil Affairs Department of the Colonial Government Office from 1913 to 1934; local cultural heritage data places the 1913 Taichung Provincial Office building, the 1920 reorganization of administrative districts into Taichung Province, and subsequent additions in the same chronological entry. [^1] [^2]

This time difference is important. If one only writes "built in 1913," readers will assume the current building was fully fixed in that year; if one only writes "completed in 1934," the early administrative needs of the Taichung Provincial Office era will be erased. Staged construction allows us to see how an official compound expanded along with the scope of governance, the number of departments, and the demands of work. Architecture is not a pre-existing aesthetic blueprint that waits for the city to use it; it is continuously added to, adjusted, and rearranged during the growth of administration.

The 1920 reorganization of local administration divided Taiwan into the five provinces of Taipei, Hsinchu, Taichung, Tainan, and Kaohsiung, and the role of the Taichung Provincial Office building changed accordingly. This is not merely changing a signboard. The provincial level, jurisdiction, and departmental division of labor all required a hall large enough to represent the new administrative scale. The "permanent hall" character of Taichung Provincial Administration Hall was formed precisely within this institutional attempt: it aimed to make administration no longer like a temporarily borrowed room, but like an institution that will long exist and can demand obedience from residents and the locality. [^3] [^8]

```tw-timeline
1913 | Taichung Provincial Office building begins phased construction | Gradually forming a complete official compound from local provincial office spaces
1920 | Reorganization of local administration into five provinces | Taichung Provincial Office building becomes the administrative core of Taichung Province
1934 | Present scale completed | Main building, ancillary halls, and meeting spaces operate together
2019 | Designated as a national historic monument | Preservation scope includes the main building, southern ancillary halls, and rear meeting hall building
Source: Taichung City Cultural Heritage Bureau, National Cultural Heritage Administration
```

The easiest thing to overlook in the timeline is "operating together." Historic monument names often only retain the most prominent main building, but administrative work requires files, meetings, police, military service, and various ancillary spaces that will not appear in the center of a postcard. The Taichung City Cultural Heritage Bureau records that the national historic monument preservation scope includes the main building, the southern ancillary halls, and the rear meeting hall building, restoring the preservation target from a single frontal photo back to a working group. [^1]

## The White Façade Is Not a Neutral Garment

The appearance of Taichung Provincial Administration Hall has a strong sense of order: the corner towers are gathered with triangular piano nobile walls, the center uses a mansard roof, and the roof is dotted with tiger windows; the piano nobile walls, column heads, corners, and balustrades are layered with various decorations. The main building is a combination of wooden frame, reinforced concrete floor slabs, and brick structure; the rear meeting hall building uses a steel frame and reinforced brick construction. [^1]

These terms may seem like architectural vocabulary, but they are also describing a political tone. Neoclassicism and the Second Empire style placed the official compound within a set of public architecture vocabulary originating from Europe; Kiyoshi Mori formed a recognizable body of work among Taichung Provincial Administration Hall, Taipei Provincial Administration Hall, and Tainan Provincial Administration Hall, allowing the colonial government to present its rule in the forms of "modern," "permanent," and "civilized." This does not mean the building lacks craftsmanship value, but rather reminds us: aesthetics have never been a neutral packaging detached from institutions. [^1] [^3]

Its modernity is not simply a "Western influence." Wooden frames, brick walls, reinforced concrete floor slabs, and steel frames were combined under the hot and humid climate, earthquake risk, and local construction conditions. The building had to be constructible in Taiwan, affordable to use, and able to accommodate administrative equipment and personnel. Scholars studying the history of the Taichung Provincial Office point out that many guides only focus on the visible building today, ignoring the changes before the establishment of Taichung Province in 1920; this reminder also applies to the history of materials: behind the "style" of an official hall, there are also the collaboration of technology, supply, craftsmen, and institutions. [^8]

## The Official Complex Speaks More Than the Main Building

If the history of Taichung Provincial Administration Hall is reduced to the central porch, half of the administrative work is cut off. Data on the ancillary building complex shows that the police department hall is based on a classical style, forming a clear vertical composition from the base, walls, horizontal decorative bands to the eaves; some spaces even use continuous arc arches. [^7] These buildings may not be as easy to become urban postcards as the main building, but they are closer to the daily life of the system: different departments handle population, public safety, transportation, taxation, and local affairs in different rooms.

The power of administration is often not completed in a grand ceremony, but in forms, seals, queues, instructions, and waiting. The main building is responsible for representing the state, while the ancillary spaces are responsible for making the state function. Only by preserving the entire complex together can we have the opportunity to write back how "power lands" into urban history, rather than only leaving behind "an architect designed a beautiful house."

The 1921 Japanese colonial map preserved by the National Cultural Memory Library also places Taichung Provincial Administration Hall and its ancillary building complex back on the map. The value of the map is not only in confirming the location, but more in that it allows the building to re-establish relationships with surrounding roads, streets, and other public facilities. When we cut the provincial hall from the map into a single photo, it looks like an object; placed back into the city, we see that it was originally a node in an administrative network. [^6]

## Regime Change, the Address Remains

After the war, Taichung Provincial Administration Hall did not immediately withdraw with the end of colonial rule. Official and research data both place it within the context of post-war Taichung City Government use, the continuation of local administration, and later cultural preservation. This continuity is neither "the same building representing the same country" nor "completely different after changing the signboard." The bodily memory of the address, rooms, and doorway remains, while the system renames and arranges itself within it. [^2] [^8]

For those who once handled matters here, it may be a window of the city government; for researchers, it is material evidence of the colonial provincial hall system; for nearby residents, it may just be a building in the city center they pass by every day. Different identities do not naturally share the same historic site story. When calling it "urban memory," we must continue to ask: whose memory is being displayed? Whose administrative experience is left only in documents? Who can return to this space after restoration is completed?

This is also why Taichung Provincial Administration Hall cannot be simply summarized as "preserving Japanese colonial architecture." What is preserved is a building, but what is explained is how multiple powers, administrative systems, and civic life overlap. If one only praises its classical proportions, one may easily forget that these proportions once served colonial rule. If one only treats it as colonial heritage, one will miss how the post-war local government, staff, and citizens let it continue to serve as a public space.

## Restoration Is Not Erasing Time

In 2019, Taichung Provincial Administration Hall was designated as a national historic monument, with the preservation scope including the main building, the southern ancillary halls, and the rear meeting hall building. The Taichung City Cultural Heritage Bureau also pointed out that Taichung Provincial Administration Hall was included in the "Taichung Historical Space Reconstruction Project of the Cultural City Center," with the goal not only to repair the exterior wall but also to connect with surrounding cultural heritage. [^1]

The difficulty of restoration is that the building cannot be treated as a single old photo. Official engineering assessment data distinguishes between main body restoration, surrounding environment, internal space reuse, and living facility construction, and points out that different stages of projects have different scopes. These project boundaries may seem like administrative details, but they actually determine when the historic site can be opened, which areas can be used, how fire safety and accessibility are handled, and whether "restoration completion" is equivalent to "can live." [^5]

Restoration also brings to the foreground a kind of labor that is often overlooked. The Taichung City Government reported on the 2026 historic site restoration project's plastering craftsmanship and artisan experience in 2026, allowing people to see that curves, walls, and decorations are not "originally like that"; they can only be preserved through material judgment, manual skills, and on-site collaboration. The historic site is therefore not only something left from the past, but also contemporary workers deciding every day how to approach the work of the past. [^10]

The ethics of restoration appear here as well: how much trace should be retained? Should the modifications of different periods be left behind? Should the electromechanical equipment be hidden, or should the conflict between old and new be made visible? Official data does not give a single answer to all questions, but the project phases, the preservation of ancillary buildings, and the records of artisans all remind us that historic site preservation is an ongoing negotiation, not a restoration to a "pure era."

## From Administrative Entrance to Public Entrance

In the 2019 explanation by the Cultural Heritage Bureau, the restored Taichung Provincial Administration Hall was described as a cultural highlight of Taichung, and it was hoped to be connected with surrounding cultural heritage. [^1] This vision has its public value: a seat that originally required people to come for official business can be transformed into an entrance for understanding urban history, architectural techniques, and local governance. But "opening" should not only mean opening the door for photography; it should also let people know how this building once arranged who could handle matters, who had to obey, and who worked in the ancillary spaces.

Good reuse should allow the complexity of the building to be seen. Exhibitions can explain the relationship between Taichung Provincial Administration Hall and the provincial administrative system, and can also place maps, engineering files, ancillary buildings, and post-war office use side by side. Guides do not need to present colonial modernization as a single line of progress; they should allow visitors to see at the same time the efficiency of roads and systems, the oppression of administrative power, the adjustment of local society, and the modern cost of preservation projects.

For citizens, public entrances also include less formal uses: staying under the shade, participating in local activities, understanding how a block is formed, or simply seeing the roof from a familiar road. If a historic site can only be explained by a specialized guide during specific time periods, it may still be an isolated exhibit. If it can establish connections with surrounding streets, schools, archives, local shops, and cultural activities, it may truly return to urban life.

This return to life does not mean commercializing all spaces. The restoration application, design changes, dismantling investigation, and construction records listed by the National Archives Information Network show that historic site preservation requires long-term administration and professional supervision. [^9] If reuse only pursues rapid entry, it may sacrifice structural safety, historical layers, and public accessibility. If it only pursues original restoration, it may turn the building into a specimen that no one can use. There is no simple answer between the two, and it must be corrected through public data, professional judgment, and citizen use experience.

## How a Building Holds Contradictions

Placing the 1921 map and today's street block together, we can see another scale of Taichung Provincial Administration Hall. The map leaves the relationship between the building complex and the roads, while today's walk leaves how people bypass the wall, cross the street corner, and stop in front of the main façade. The former explains how the official compound was embedded in the urban plan, while the latter explains how citizens turned the space originally serving administration into daily orientation. The two scales cannot replace each other: a map cannot explain queuing and waiting, and a walk cannot replace the understanding of administrative zoning and project phases. [^6] [^8]

Restoration projects also need to handle both scales at the same time. The main structure, surrounding landscape, internal space, and living facilities often belong to different project scopes, and any delay may cause a gap between "repaired" and "usable." This is not only a construction management issue but also a public issue of cultural heritage: if a historic site can only retain its appearance but lacks safety, accessibility, fire safety, and understandable display conditions, what citizens see is still an isolated symbol. [^5] [^9]

The most powerful aspect of Taichung Provincial Administration Hall is precisely that it cannot be summarized in a single sentence of "a century-old historic site." It was once a machine for the colonial administration to order the city, and is also a building jointly used by designers, builders, administrative staff, and local residents. Its façade displays state power, while its ancillary spaces support daily administration. It continued to be used after regime change, and was renamed within the cultural heritage system. It is now preserved, but still must answer how preservation should not only serve tourism gaze.

From the street corner, this building tells us that the city is not naturally grown but organized by roads, official compounds, finance, post, and administrative boundaries. From the frame, it reminds us that grand public architecture must rely on concrete materials and the bodies of craftsmen. From the restoration site, it turns "preservation" into a series of contemporary choices: which traces to leave, which facilities to add, which stories to tell, and who can enter.

Therefore, Taichung Provincial Administration Hall does not need to be packaged as a flawless urban glory. It is worth preserving precisely because those cracks allow us to see that Taiwan's modernization has never had a single source, nor has it been completed only by a mayor, an architect, or a government. Institutions enter life through architecture, and life changes the meaning of architecture over a long time. Today, walking toward its doorway again is not returning to 1913, but bringing with it the wars, regimes, urban expansion, restoration techniques, and civic memories that came later, and re-deciding how this administrative hall should become a public place.
