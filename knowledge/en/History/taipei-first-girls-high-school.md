> **30 Second Overview:** The history of North High School for Girls (Taipei Municipal First Girls' Senior High School) is not just a list of prestigious schools; it is a microcosm of modern Taiwanese history. From being a screener for Japanese elites in 1904, to the Guangfu Building completed in 1933; from the "green uniforms" born out of Cold War anti-aircraft needs in 1952, to today's status as a "border school" next to the Presidential Office. This campus has preserved how female bodies were disciplined, how Cold War science education unfolded, and how students developed unique worldviews on the periphery of power centers.[^1] [^2]

At an intersection in Taipei's Boai District, figures in green uniforms and black skirts are always precisely located at the boundary between the red-brick school buildings and modern streets. This school, established in 1904, is officially named "Taipei Municipal First Girls' Senior High School," but in the collective consciousness of Taiwanese society, it is more often simplified as "First High Girl" or "North High Girl." However, hidden behind the halo of academic prestige is a complex history concerning colonial screening, bodily discipline, and the preservation of public space.

## The "Aristocratic School" under Colonial Screening

The predecessor to North High School for Girls was the "National Language School Third Affiliated Girls' Secondary School," founded in 1904, initially intended to provide secondary education for Japanese women residing in Taiwan. In 1921, it was reformed into the "Taipei Municipal First Girls' Senior High School." During the Japanese colonial period, this institution was a distant dream for many Taiwanese women. Although "Japanese-Taiwanese co-education" was ostensibly opened in 1922, the proportion of local Taiwanese students was extremely low, forming a sharp contrast with "Third High Girl" (now Zhongshan Girls' High School), which primarily recruited local Taiwanese students.[^1]

Before the war, Taiwanese students admitted to First High Girl were often the favored daughters of prominent families and elites, such as Mei Yan, daughter of the mining magnate An Kuo-nian from Keelung, or Su Chun-shun, daughter of Dr. Tu Tsungming, Taiwan's first medical doctor. The curriculum at the time emphasized not only academics but also home economics, crafts, and bodily discipline. Students were required to study gymnastics, swimming, and volleyball; this demand for physical quality was intended not just for health, but to shape a "new woman" who met modern standards.[^2]

📝 **Curator's Note:** When we speak of North High Girl's "elite status," we often overlook that it initially functioned as a screening tool for the colonial ruling class. This process—shifting from lineage-based selection to examination-based selection—is a microcosm of social stratification mobility in modern Taiwan.

## Cold War Camouflage: The Politics of Green Uniforms

Many people assume that North High Girl's "green uniforms" were chosen for aesthetics or to symbolize longevity, but the history behind them is highly colored by the Cold War. Before 1952, North High Girl wore white dresses and black skirts like many other schools. However, during a period of extreme tension in cross-Strait relations, North High Girl, located near the Presidential Office, was considered a primary target for air raids.[^4]

The then-principal, Jiang Xue-zhu, proposed changing the uniforms to dark green to protect student safety. This was not a fashion choice but an attempt at "camouflage"—when air raid sirens sounded and students took refuge in the dense foliage of the campus, the dark green uniforms allowed them to blend into the environment, reducing the risk of detection by enemy aircraft.[^5] This attire, later nicknamed "Little Green," is essentially a school-version camouflage uniform that witnessed Taiwan's existential anxieties on the front lines of the Cold War.

📝 **Curator's Note:** The birth of the green uniforms represents the most direct intervention of spatial politics into the female body. A single garment simultaneously carried the fear of survival during air raids and later evolved into a symbol of honor.

## Guangfu Building and Spatial Politics: Neighbor to the Presidential Office

The most representative building on campus, the Guangfu Building, was completed in 1933 and designed by the Taiwan Governor's Office Construction Division. This structure employs an "Eclectic Modernism" style, with an L-shaped plan and a façade of popular "patterned bricks" made of pure RC construction.[^6] The design of the Guangfu Building is not merely an aesthetic choice but an embodiment of function and order. Its high corridors and symmetrical windows create a rigorous academic atmosphere.[^7]

However, what makes North High Girl unique in terms of space is its geographical location—being adjacent to the Presidential Office. This relationship as "power neighbors" was materialized during a poetry recitation competition on campus in 2018. A sophomore student titled "When We Became Neighbors to the President," satirized the barricades often erected during protests, renaming the school as "Taipei Municipal First Border High School."[^8] This daily existence on the periphery of power centers in Boai District led students to develop a unique perspective: watching the national flag wave beneath the border courtyard and reconstructing their worldview amidst political tension.

## Scientific Drive in the Cold War: Jiang Xue-zhu and the Science Museum

After the regime change in 1945, the school was consolidated and renamed. In 1949, the legendary principal Jiang Xue-zhu took charge, initiating a period of transformation spanning 22 years. In the early 1950s, Taiwanese society was still generally patriarchal with severe material shortages, yet Jiang Xue-zhu resolutely overcame difficulties to build a dedicated "Science Museum" on campus.[^10]

This action is embedded in the global context of the Cold War. Influenced by the US and Soviet arms race competition, educational policies began shifting toward science. Under Jiang Xue-zhu's leadership, North High School for Girls actively introduced American aid resources for scientific education experiments, established physics and chemistry laboratories, and promoted science exhibitions.[^11] This not only broke the prejudice that women were only suited for domestic education but also cemented North High Girl's leading position in post-war Taiwanese elite education. In 1948, Education Minister Chu Chia-hua visited Taiwan and rated North High School as "the best school in all of China."[^12]

## Leadership Data: The Social Footprint of Female Elites

The influence of North High School for Girls on Taiwanese society can be seen through concrete data. According to informal statistics from the alumni association and related studies, the employment rate of North High Girl graduates is as high as 97%, with over 60% having served in administrative or middle-to-high-level management positions.[^13] More importantly, among female academics and fellows who grew up in Taiwan at the highest academic institution—the Academia Sinica—graduates from North High School account for the highest proportion in Taiwan.

This formation of leadership stems not only from the screening mechanism of the education system but also from a highly autonomous club culture within the school. The military band, established in 1963, was not just about discipline; it was a field where students learned cooperation and leadership through collective training.[^14] The band transformed militaristic drill into precise aesthetics, symbolizing the process of women reclaiming subjectivity within order.

## Public Memory Across Generations

Today's North High School for Girls has transitioned from a colonial screener during the Japanese administration to an important starting point for social mobility and female public participation in Taiwanese society. From early literary figures like San Mao (Teresa Teng) and Chu Tianxin, to modern scientists and politicians, the female elites cultivated by this campus challenge established orders in various fields.

North High School for Girls is not just a school; it is a living museum that preserves a century of educational transformation, changes in architectural aesthetics, and the politics of the female body in Taiwan. When students run through the corridors of the Guangfu Building or practice throwing at the field, they are engaging in a dialogue with history from a hundred years ago, searching for their own freedom within order.
