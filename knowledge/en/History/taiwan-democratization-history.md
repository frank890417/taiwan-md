# Democratization

> **30-Second Overview:** On December 10, 1979, amid the tear gas smoke on the streets of Kaohsiung, 29-year-old Chen Chu was dragged into a police car. In prison, she wrote her final will, believing she would be executed by firing squad. Forty years later, she became the President of the Control Yuan, the head of Taiwan's highest supervisory organ. Taiwan's democratization was not a simple switch, but a forty-year experiment: moving from the world's longest period of martial law (38 years and 56 days) to becoming one of Asia's freest democracies (Freedom House 2025 score: 93/100, 6th in the world). The entire process occurred without a bloody revolution or a military coup.

On March 18, 1980, the Taiwan military court began the trial of the Formosa Incident. Sitting in the defendant's dock were Shih Ming-teh, Huang Hsin-chieh, Chen Chu, and Lu Hsiu-lien; the gallery was packed with international journalists, and U.S. Senator Ted Kennedy included Shih Ming-teh's case in the Congressional Record.

The Kuomintang (KMT) originally intended to hold a public trial to demonstrate judicial legitimacy. The result was exactly the opposite.

The trial provided the defendants with a national stage. Shih Ming-teh had prepared a 60,000-word defense, but upon learning in court that [Lin Yi-hsiung](/en/people/lin-yi-hsiung-democracy-advocate-tragedy-survivor/)'s mother and twin daughters had been murdered outside of prison ([the Lin family massacre](/en/history/228-incident/)), he abandoned his defense on the spot and demanded the judge sentence him to death. This scene spread across the island through the media.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The most absurd consequence of the Formosa trial was that the group of young lawyers defending the defendants—[Chen Shui-bian](/en/people/chen-shui-bian-controversial-president/), Frank Hsieh, Su Tseng-chang, Chang Chun-hsiung, and Yu Chin—almost all became Taiwan's president, premier, or mayors of special municipalities within twenty years. The trial, meticulously planned by the KMT, unintentionally cultivated an entire generation of political elites for the opposition movement.

## Zheng Nanrong's Seventy-One Days

In the decade following the Formosa trial, Taiwanese society was like a pot of water being slowly heated. The _dangwai_ (outside the party) movement continued to expand, but the person who truly pushed the temperature to a boiling point was an _waishengren_ (mainlander) named [Zheng Nanrong](/en/people/zheng-nanrong/).

On December 10, 1988, International Human Rights Day, Zheng Nanrong published a full text of a "Draft Constitution for the Republic of Taiwan" in the 254th issue of the magazine he edited, _Freedom_ (_Ziyou Shidai_). At the time, this was equivalent to treason and carried the death penalty.

After receiving a court summons, Zheng Nanrong locked himself inside the magazine office and refused to appear in court. He said his final words to his wife, Yeh Chu-lan:

**"The rest is up to you."**

At 7:30 AM on April 7, 1989, nearly two hundred police officers surrounded the magazine office. At 9:15 AM, Zheng Nanrong died by self-immolation at the age of only 41. The back cover of his magazine forever bears the same sentence: "Fight for one hundred percent freedom of speech!"

On May 19, tens of thousands of people followed his coffin through the rain toward the Presidential Office.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The commander of the special task force that led the raid on the magazine office was Hou Yu-ih. Thirty-five years later, in 2024, he ran for president representing the KMT. The same individual stood on the side of state violence during one of the most harrowing moments of Taiwan's democratization, yet campaigned for the highest power within a democratic system. This fact itself embodies the absurdity and complexity of Taiwan's history.

Zheng Nanrong's death directly drove the 1992 amendment to Article 100 of the Criminal Code and the abolition of the "Seditions Act." From then on, thought was no longer a crime. April 7 was later designated as "Freedom of Speech Day."

## Martial Law: Not a Switch

On July 14, 1987, the 75-year-old Chiang Ching-kuo held a press conference at the Presidential Office, announcing that martial law would be lifted at midnight the following day. Twelve days later, on July 27, he invited twelve local elders to the Presidential Office for tea and said a sentence that has since been repeatedly quoted:

**"I have lived in Taiwan for forty years; I am Taiwanese, and of course, I am also Chinese."**

This sentence was later selectively cited across different political spectrums. The independence camp took only the first half; the unification camp emphasized the second. However, in the context of 1987, its function was to bridge the provincial divide: an _waishengren_ strongman acknowledging he was also Taiwanese, attempting to cool the tensions ahead of the coming political opening.

Chiang Ching-kuo passed away six months later.

But lifting martial law did not equal freedom. The government simultaneously passed the "National Security Act," which included Article 9: during martial law, judgments made by military courts against civilians "may not be appealed or protested to the competent court." In other words, the legal avenues for relief were blocked for victims of [Taiwan White Terror](/en/history/taiwan-white-terror/).

> **"Because of this, some scholars argue that Taiwan's true starting point for democratization was 1992 (the amendment of Article 100 of the Criminal Code and the abolition of the Seditions Act), rather than the lifting of martial law in 1987."**

This is also why some who experienced martial law remember July 15 not with euphoria, but with confusion: you could march now, but who would dare? You could form a party, but if you did, would you be arrested? Democracy is not a light that turns on when you flip a switch; it requires an entire society to relearn "how not to be afraid."

## Six Thousand Wild Lilies vs. Tiananmen

On March 16, 1990, less than nine months before the Tiananmen Square protests, students at National Taiwan University began a sit-in at the Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall plaza. The cause was [Lee Teng-hui](/en/people/lee-teng-hui/)'s bid for re-election as president, while the voting National Assembly remained the "permanent assembly" elected in mainland China in 1947—consisting of over seven hundred elderly representatives who had never faced re-election.

Student banners read: "Citizens, can you endure the oppression of 700 emperors?"

Starting with a dozen people, the crowd grew to nearly six thousand within six days. They held _ye-bai-he_ (wild lilies, symbolizing pure Taiwanese native flowers) and presented four demands: dissolve the National Assembly, abolish the Temporary Provisions, convene a National Affairs Conference, and set a timetable for reform.

On March 21, Lee Teng-hui met with fifty student representatives at the Presidential Office.

It was the mirror image of Tiananmen. Nine months earlier, Beijing's students received tanks; Taipei's students received promises from the president. Lee Teng-hui later stated he had ordered that "students must not be harmed" (according to an ETtoday report from 2015).

The promises were fulfilled one by one: in May 1991, the Temporary Provisions were abolished; in December of the same year, the permanent National Assembly was dissolved; in 1992, the Legislative Yuan underwent full re-election; and on March 23, 1996, Taiwan held its first direct presidential election.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The life trajectories of the Wild Lily student leaders are themselves a history of Taiwanese politics: Fan Yun became a DPP legislator, Lin Chia-lung became Minister of Foreign Affairs, Lo Wen-chia became a key figure in the DPP, and Cheng Wen-tsan became the Mayor of Taoyuan. This movement did not just change the system; it defined the political character of an entire generation.

## Voting Under Missiles

In March 1996, on the eve of Taiwan's first direct presidential election, China conducted missile tests 20 miles off the coast of Keelung and 29 miles off the coast of Kaohsiung, paralyzing 70% of commercial shipping passing through these two ports. Simultaneously, the PLA mobilized 100,000 troops along the coast.

Beijing's intention was clear: to deter Taiwanese people from voting for Lee Teng-hui.

The United States responded by deploying two carrier battle groups, the USS Nimitz and the USS Independence—the largest U.S. military deployment in the Pacific since the Vietnam War.

On March 23, voter turnout was 76.04%. Lee Teng-hui secured 5,813,699 votes, a 54% vote share. China's missiles did not deter voters; instead, they likely drove an additional 5% of the vote toward Lee, turning his relative majority into an absolute majority.

Beijing's carefully orchestrated intimidation produced the very result it least wanted to see.

## Three Alternations: The Character of the Loser

The true test of democracy is not the election itself, but "whether the loser is willing to walk away."

**2000**: Chen Shui-bian was elected with a 39.3% vote share, ending 55 years of KMT rule. This was the first time in Chinese-speaking society that power was transferred peacefully through the ballot box. The KMT split was key: Song Chu-yu left the party to run, taking 37% of the vote, while Lien Chan received only 23%.

**2008**: Ma Ying-jeou defeated Frank Hsieh with 58.45%. The DPP peacefully handed over power, proving that "conceding defeat" was not a KMT monopoly.

**2016**: Tsai Ing-wen defeated Eric Chu with 56.12%. The KMT again peacefully stepped down from power.

**2024**: Lai Ching-te was elected with 40.05%. For the first time, the DPP governed without a legislative majority, while the opposition held control of the legislature. The democratic system underwent another stress test.

Four party alternations over thirty years, each completed peacefully. No military coups, no refusal to transfer power, no violent protests.

## Why Was There No Bloodshed?

Eastern European revolutions overthrew communist parties, the Arab Spring triggered civil wars, and the Myanmar military junta carried out bloody suppressions. Why did Taiwan achieve a "silent revolution"?

Political scientist Samuel Huntington categorized Taiwan in _The Third Wave_ as a "transformation"—an elite-led, top-down democratization, distinct from South Korea's "transplacement" or the Philippines' "replacement."

But there were structural reasons why the elites chose reform:

**The economic miracle created a middle class.** Between 1960 and 1990, Taiwan's GDP per capita soared from \$164 to \$8,111. The middle class had too much to lose; they wanted reform, not revolution.

**Diplomatic isolation forced the regime to seek legitimacy.** Withdrawal from the UN (1971), the severance of ties with the U.S. (1979), and expulsion from the IOC. The KMT could no longer rely on "recovering the mainland" as a justification for rule; it had to build a foundation of public mandate within Taiwan.

**The self-restraint of the opposition.** The Formosa figures and the DPP chose institutional reform at critical moments rather than violent struggle. The People Power Revolution in the Philippines (1986 EDSA Revolution) proved that peaceful power transfers were possible, serving as an important signal to Taiwan's reformers.

## Imperfect Democracy

Freedom House gave Taiwan a score of 93 out of 100 in 2025, ranking it 6th globally and 2nd in Asia (behind Japan's 96). _The Economist_ Democracy Index 2024 ranked it 12th globally; it received full marks for electoral process and pluralism, making it the only "full democracy" in Asia.

But there are cracks behind the numbers:

During the [Taiwan White Terror](/en/history/taiwan-white-terror/), an estimated 140,000 to 200,000 people suffered political persecution, and 3,000 to 4,000 were executed. Transitional justice remains unfinished. It was not until 2019 that the Constitutional Court declared Article 9 of the National Security Act unconstitutional—32 years after the lifting of martial law.

Disinformation is the new battlefield. Both the 2018 local elections and the 2020 presidential election saw large-scale disinformation operations, many of which originated from overseas. The Chinese government continues to attempt to influence Taiwan's policymaking, media, and democratic infrastructure (according to the Freedom House 2025 report).

Blue-Green polarization has degraded the quality of policy debate. Important bills are often shelved or forced through due to partisan stances rather than policy merit.

Taiwan's democracy is imperfect. But it is alive.

## Chen Chu's Final Will

In December 1979, 29-year-old Chen Chu wrote her final will in prison, preparing for execution. In that letter, she said goodbye to the people of Taiwan.

She was not executed. She served six years in prison; after her release, she helped found the DPP, served two terms as the Director of the Taipei City Social Affairs Bureau, became the Chairperson of the Council of Labor Affairs, and served twelve years as the Mayor of Kaohsiung. When re-elected in 2014, she received 990,000 votes, the highest in Taiwan. In 2020, she was nominated as the President of the Control Yuan.

From writing a final will to becoming the President of the Control Yuan took forty-one years.

The same person, the same island. The only difference is: in 1979, thought was a crime; in 2020, the former political prisoner was supervising whether the government was committing crimes.

This is what democratization means. It is not a smooth inspirational story, but a political experiment filled with absurdity, contradiction, and cost. The experiment is still ongoing.
