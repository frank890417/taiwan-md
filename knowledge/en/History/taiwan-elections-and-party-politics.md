# Taiwan Elections and Party Politics

> **30-second overview:** The 1950s "democracy showcase" elections could only elect legislators, not presidents, until 1996 when 14.31 million people cast ballots in the first direct presidential election in Chinese society.[^9] Taiwan accomplished this in under half a century, experiencing three peaceful transfers of power (2000, 2008, 2016), transforming elections from political theater under martial law into a civic ritual held every four years. From the flames of the Zhongli Incident to the three-party contest of 2024, democracy on this island was not designed—it was fought for, one ballot at a time.

On November 19, 1977, at the 213th polling station in Zhongli City, Taoyuan County, a poll watcher was seen smearing ink-stained thumbs onto ballots. After news spread, over ten thousand citizens surrounded the Zhongli Police Department. That night, flames consumed the station building. Two people died: National Central University student Chiang Wen-kuo was shot in the head, and 19-year-old Chang Chih-ping collapsed in the street.[^1]

That fire did not just burn down a building. It sent a signal: Taiwan voters would no longer tolerate stolen ballots. In that election, independent candidate Hsu Hsin-liang won by 230,000 votes over the Kuomintang's Ou Chen-yu.[^2] The Zhongli Incident became the first spark of Taiwan's democratic movement. Votes could be stolen, but public opinion could not.

## The Evolution of a Single Vote

### Windows of Democracy (1950s)

In the 1950s, the Nationalist government needed to prove to the United States that it was the "free China." Local elections served as the "window" through which America could see democratic form, even though the Kuomintang tightly controlled the content. The 1950 Taiwan Provincial Assembly election was the first popular election after the war, but the rules were clear: candidates could be elected as assembly members, but not as provincial governor; they could criticize policies, but not question the leader[^3].

Even under strict control, elections opened cracks in the system. In 1951, Wu Sanxian was elected Taipei City Mayor as an independent candidate with 65.6% of the vote, becoming the first directly elected mayor of the capital of the Republic of China[^4]. The Kuomintang secretly supported him behind the scenes, but the mere fact of a "non-KMT" mayor taking office at Taipei City Government showed other local politicians that change was possible.

### Wars on the Ballot (1970s)

In the 1970s, Taiwan faced consecutive shocks: expulsion from the United Nations (1971) and breaking diplomatic ties with the United States (1979). The legitimacy of the Kuomintang began to waver. The middle class grew stronger, and voices outside the party began to emerge in elections.

In 1975, 67-year-old political figure Guo Yuxin from Yilan ran for the Legislative Yuan. When the vote count was announced, nearly 100,000 ballots in Yilan County alone were invalidated—several times the normal rate. Later, during road construction, a bag of discarded valid ballots was unearthed, every single one marked for Guo Yuxin. The lawyers who took up Guo's election case were Yao Jiayi and Lin Yixiong: four years later, both would become defendants in the February 28th Incident[^5].

Guo Yuxin's votes were stolen. But that lawsuit taught an entire generation of non-KMT figures: the law could be a weapon.

### From Underground to the Grand Hotel (1980s)

The February 28th Incident (1979) dealt a blow to the opposition movement, yet also cultivated the next generation. Defense lawyers for the arrested leaders—Chen Shui-bian, Hsie Chang-ting, and Su Feng-san—became new faces of the democracy movement. The families of those detained—Lu Hsiu-lien, Chen Ju, and Chou Ching-chu—entered politics, becoming the female force of the February 28th generation.

On September 28, 1986, 132 opposition figures gathered in the Menck Hall on the second floor of the Taipei Grand Hotel to announce the founding of the Democratic Progressive Party. This violated the then-current "Regulations Governing Civic Organizations During the Period of Communist Rebellion Suppression," and forming a party was illegal. When the news reached Chiang Ching-kuo, his advisors suggested crackdown. Chiang Ching-kuo said one sentence: "Times are changing, trends are changing, and the environment is also changing."[^6]

Ten days later, Chiang Ching-kuo told the publisher of the Washington Post, Kathleen Neuchter, that Taiwan would lift martial law and open up its politics. The founding of the DPP was not suppressed: the Kuomintang chose tolerance.

Two months later, in the legislative election, the DPP secured 22.2% of the vote and won 12 seats. A party barely sixty days old had already found its footing[^7].

## Electing Their Own President

### Full Legislative Elections (1991-1992)

In 1991, the "tenacious congress" that had lasted forty-three years finally underwent a full election. Those veteran legislators and National Assembly representatives elected in 1947 on the mainland, who had never been re-elected, stepped down. In 1992, the Legislative Yuan was fully elected, and for the first time, the people of Taiwan genuinely elected their own congress[^8].

### 1996: Missiles and Ballots

On March 23, 1996, 14.31 million voters went to the polls to elect Taiwan's — and the Chinese-speaking world's — first directly elected president.

The Chinese Communist Party (CCP) fired missiles toward Taiwan's offshore areas before the election in an attempt to intimidate voters. The tactic backfired: voter turnout surged to 76%. Lee Teng-hui won 5.81 million votes (54%), Peng Ming-min 2.27 million (21.1%), Lin Yanggang 1.6 million (14.9%), and Chen Luanxiang 1.07 million (10%)[^9]. The missiles failed to scare anyone away.

### 2000: A Three-Way Race and the First Peaceful Transition

The 2000 presidential election was the most dramatic night in Taiwan's political history. The Kuomintang (KMT) split, with former Taiwan Provincial Governor Song Chuyu running as a third-party candidate and splitting the pan-blue camp's vote with the KMT's nominee, Lien Chan.

The results: Chen Shui-bian received 4.97 million votes (39.3%), Song Chuyu 4.66 million (36.8%), and Lien Chan 2.92 million (23.1%). The Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) won the presidency with less than 40% of the vote[^10].

The key was not who won, but how the losers responded. The KMT, which had held power for 55 years, did not deploy the military or stage a coup. Both Lien Chan and Song Chuyu conceded defeat. Power was transferred peacefully. For the first time, Taiwan proved its democracy was more than theory.

### 2004: Two Bullets

On March 19, 2004, the day before the election, Chen Shui-bian and Annette Lu were shot while campaigning on the streets of Tainan. One bullet grazed Chen's abdomen; the other struck the protective gear on Lu's knee. Both were hospitalized and discharged the same day[^11].

The next day, Chen defeated Lien Chan by fewer than 30,000 votes. Pan-blue supporters gathered outside the Presidential Office to protest, questioning whether the shooting was staged. The main suspect, Chen Yixiong, was found dead by drowning in Tainan's Anping Port ten days later. The truth remains contested to this day.

This was the moment Taiwan's democracy came closest to fracturing. Nearly half of voters questioned the legitimacy of the result, and street protests continued for weeks. In the end, legal procedures did their job: recounts, litigation, and court rulings. The system held.

## The Fragmentation of Party Politics

Taiwan's party history is not as simple as a "two-party system." It is a history of constant splits, reorganizations, deaths, and rebirths.

Internal factional conflicts within the Kuomintang (KMT) erupted in the 1990s. In 1993, dissatisfied with Lee Teng-hui's "localization" direction, a new KMT faction split off to form the **New Party**. After Song Chu-yu's failed 2000 presidential bid, the **People's Party** was established. In 2001, after Lee Teng-hui was expelled from the KMT and supported the formation of the **Taiwan Solidarity Union**[^12].

The Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) side also experienced cracks. In 2015, the energy of the Sunflower Movement transformed into the **New Power Party**, advocating a more radical Taiwanese-centered line than the DPP. In 2019, Taipei Mayor Ko Wen-je broke out on his own to establish the **Taiwan People's Party**, attempting a "beyond blue and green" third path[^13].

By 2024, Taiwan's party landscape had roughly solidified into three major forces: the DPP (green), the KMT (blue), and the TPP (white). But history tells us that this landscape could split again at any time.

## How Taiwan Votes

### Campaign Rallies: A Festival of Democracy

The most distinctive feature of Taiwan's elections is the campaign rally. Candidates rent a square, set up a stage, entertainers warm up the crowd, and people wave flags while chanting _chliansuan! chliansuan!_ (a pun on "elected" in Taiwanese Hokkien).

Campaign rallies are not just political mobilization but community festivals. Street vendors set up stalls along the way, children ride on their fathers' shoulders, and elderly people bring chairs to claim spots. Elections are one of the few activities where Taiwanese people can simultaneously satisfy political participation, social needs, and entertainment consumption.

### Homecoming Voting: An Annual Migration

Taiwan still does not have an absentee voting system. Voters must cast their ballots in person at their registered residence; there are no mail-in ballots and no early voting[^14].

This means that on every election day, Taiwan experiences a massive "homecoming voting" migration. Kaohsiung residents working in Taipei and Yunlin people working in Hsinchu scramble to buy train tickets home to vote. The high-speed rail adds extra trips, and bus companies deploy additional vehicles. On election day, Taiwan's high-speed rail stations are more crowded than during the Lunar New Year.

Opponents of absentee voting worry that over a million Taiwanese working in the PRC could be influenced by Beijing if they could vote remotely. Supporters argue that not allowing people to vote in their place of residence is an implicit voting barrier. This controversy remains unresolved.

### Vote-Counting Night

Vote counting in Taiwan is a nationwide live broadcast. After polling stations close at 4 p.m., election workers open the ballot boxes on the spot and count each vote one by one. Television crews set up cameras at every polling station for real-time coverage.

No country's vote counting is more transparent than Taiwan's. You can stand outside a polling station and watch each ballot being displayed, called out, and tallied. From the close of polling to the results being largely determined, it typically takes only three to four hours.

## Key Moments in Recent Years

### 2018: The Wave of Han and the Referendum Tsunami

The 2018 local elections were a political earthquake. KMT's Han Kuo-yu, entering as a political novice, swept into Kaohsiung and defeated DPP's Chen Chia-i with 890,000 votes, ending 20 years of DPP rule in Kaohsiung[^15]. Across Taiwan, the KMT won 15 mayoral races, while the DPP was left with only 6. Tsai Ing-wen resigned as party chair.

The concurrent citizen referendum was equally stunning. Ten referendum questions covered energy, food safety, same-sex marriage, and gender equality education. Voters faced ten referendum ballots at once, with long lines at polling stations. The "marriage limited to one man and one woman" referendum passed, creating a direct clash between public opinion and the law on Taiwan's path to becoming the first Asian country to legalize same-sex marriage[^16].

### 2020: 8.17 Million Votes

Two years later, the 2020 presidential election completely reversed course. The Hong Kong anti-extradition movement made Taiwanese society's fear of "one country, two systems" concrete. Tsai Ing-wen, rebounding from her crushing defeat in 2018, secured **8,171,860 votes**—the highest vote count for any candidate in Taiwan's electoral history. Han Kuo-yu received 5.52 million votes, with a voter turnout of 74.9%[^17].

The number 817 later became a political symbol, embraced by supporters as a sign of democratic will and by opponents as evidence of political polarization.

### 2024: A Three-Way Race Redux

The 2024 presidential election recreated the three-way contest of 2000. Lai Ching-te (DPP) won with 5.58 million votes (40%), Hou Yu-ih (KMT) secured 4.67 million votes (33.5%), and Ko Wen-cheng (TPP) received 3.69 million votes (26.5%)[^18].

This marked the first time in Taiwan's direct election history that a candidate with less than 50% of the vote was elected, and the first time a party won three consecutive presidential elections. Meanwhile, the DPP lost its majority in the Legislative Yuan—executive and legislative powers now rest in different camps, ushering Taiwan into a new era of divided government.

## Democracy in Evolution

In 2005, the National Assembly voted itself out of existence — the body elected in Nanjing in 1947, which had completed its final task in Taiwan and passed into history. The same constitutional amendment reduced the Legislative Yuan from 225 seats to 113, changed the term from three years to four, and switched to a single-member district two-vote system. Future constitutional amendments must now pass via citizen referendum, with a threshold so high it is nearly impossible to cross[^19].

Taiwan's electoral system continues to evolve. The 2022 citizen referendum proposal lowered the voting age from 20 to 18, but since it was a constitutional amendment requiring over 9.61 million affirmative votes, it ultimately garnered only 5.64 million "yes" votes and failed to pass. Political corruption, disinformation, and electoral reform each remain unfinished business.

From the flames of the 1977 Zhongli Incident to 1.4 million citizens quietly queuing to vote in 2024 — in less than half a century, Taiwan transformed elections from a right that required burning to protect into a habit that runs automatically without anyone needing to be reminded.

Not every election is flawless. But each time, the losers step aside and the winners take office. On this Earth, that outcome is far from self-evident.

## 2024 and the Political Landscape

The January 2024 elections did not merely decide who would become president — they redrew the map of power for the next four years. Lai Ching-te and Joseph Wu entered the Presidential Office with a 40.1% vote share, but the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) secured only 51 seats in the Legislative Yuan, the Kuomintang (KMT) won 52, and the Taiwan People's Party (TPP) took 8. No party held a majority. Taiwan entered its first truly strict "minority government" in constitutional history — the ruling party simultaneously lost its majority in the legislature and the speakership.[^20]

### Minority Government: Guerrilla Tactics and Procedural Disputes

Beginning in May 2024, the KMT and TPP — with a combined 60 seats — pushed forward a "Congressional Reform Act," granting the Legislative Yuan expanded investigative and hearing powers. The DPP accused the blue-white camp of using its numerical advantage to "pass second readings without committee review." The Constitutional Court subsequently ruled in Constitutional Interpretation No. 9 of 113 that several provisions were unconstitutional.[^21]

Over the following eighteen months, similar procedural disputes played out repeatedly. Budget reviews, amendments to the _Act Governing the Allocation of National Subsidies_, a proposal to amend Article 29 of the _Act Governing Relations Between Residents of the Taiwan Area and the Mainland Area_, and revisions to the _Election and Recall Act_ — each vote was accompanied by sit-ins at the speaker's podium, physical confrontations, and late-night voting sessions. The blue-white cooperation demonstrated the strength of its majority, yet internal coordination between the two parties was not always seamless. The TPP maintained reservations on certain controversial bills, while some KMT legislators openly expressed dissent from their party caucus decisions. The stability of the majority coalition required reconfirmation before every vote.

### The 2025 Great Recall

When institutional checks and balances proved ineffective, civil society activated the final tool granted by the Constitution: the recall right. Starting in February 2025, over 1.3 million citizens participated in signature drives targeting 31 KMT legislators and Hsinchu Mayor Huang Kuo-chang (Ho Yuxin).[^22]

On July 26, the first wave of 25 recall votes took place; on August 23, a second wave of 7 votes followed. All failed. In some districts, the proportion of "yes" votes did exceed the 25% threshold of the total electorate in the original constituency (e.g., Wang Hong-wei, Xu Qiaoxi, Fu Kuang-chi), but "no" votes were generally higher — even surpassing the original winning vote counts of the respective legislators.[^23]

While the recall results did not alter the composition of the Legislative Yuan, they left behind several long-term observations. The mobilization energy displayed by civil society during the signature-gathering phase was unprecedented — and whether this energy will extend to the 2026 local elections remains uncertain. The positioning of third forces (TPP, New Power Party, Taiwan Foundation for Democracy) during the recall process was relatively ambiguous; most TPP supporters cast "no" votes or abstained, posing a test for whether the blue-white camp can sustain a third-force trajectory in 2026. Another point of tension lies in the relationship between grassroots mobilization and civic will: in certain districts, the number of "yes" votes exceeded the legislators' original winning tallies, yet still fell short against a consolidated "no" campaign. Whether direct democratic tools can function effectively in a highly polarized environment is a question every election in Taiwan after 2025 will confront.[^24]

For the complete timeline and context, see [Great Recall](/en/history/great-recall-movement-2024).

### Blue-White Cooperation and the 2026 Institutional Test

On March 18, 2026, the KMT Central Standing Committee and the TPP Central Executive Committee simultaneously passed the "2026 Joint Governance and Local Election Cooperation Agreement." Spearheaded by newly elected KMT Chairwoman Cheng Liwen (October 2025) and TPP Chair Huang Kuo-chang, the agreement outlined five core principles: shared policy vision, mayoral nomination cooperation mechanisms, integrated execution methods, joint campaigning support, and post-election collaborative governance. Mayoral candidate integration followed a process of "nominate first, then integrate, ultimately endorsing one candidate jointly" — with public opinion polls used to determine final selections when necessary, with question formats compared against those of the main opposing candidates.[^25]

The three pilot cities selected were New Taipei City, Chiayi City, and Yilan County. These three areas share common characteristics: long-standing competition between blue and green forces, a degree of electoral foundation for third parties, and mayors whose terms were set to expire.[^26]

This agreement represents a new model in Taiwan's party cooperation history. In 2020, Tsai Ing-wen and Joseph Wu ran on a joint ticket within the same party; in 2024, Ko Wen-je and Wu Hsin-ying were nominated internally by the TPP. The 2026 blue-white cooperation marks the first instance of two independent parties engaging in institutional-level integration at the local election tier — through shared polling, joint nominations, and collaborative governance. It differs from the "blue-white cooperation" that broke down at the end of 2023 (the presidential ticket alliance between Hou You-yi and Ko Wen-je): in a presidential race, there is only one position, making integration a zero-sum game; in local elections, there are 22 cities and counties, allowing for district-by-district negotiation.

The success or failure of this blue-white cooperation will retroactively influence the party landscape of the 2028 presidential election. If integration proceeds smoothly, the cooperation model between third forces and major parties may become a new political structure; if it fails, the TPP may revert to its independent line under Ko Wen-je's leadership. This article does not predict outcomes, but records the moment of March 18, 2026: Taiwan's party politics is experimenting with a new, historically untested form of cooperation.

### Evolution of DPP Factions

From its founding day, the DPP was never a monolithic entity. The 132 founding members who gathered in Yuan Shan in 1986 came from diverse backgrounds — non-party civic alliances, non-party public affairs groups, and local political figures. At its inception, two major factions emerged: the **New Force faction** (originating from the 1983-founded non-party editorial writers' association, formally organized in 1987 by Chiu Yi-jen, Wu Nai-ren, and Lin Chun-sheng) and the **Beautiful Island faction** (evolving from the "Beautiful Island Connection" within the non-party public affairs circle, centered around victims of the Beautiful Island Incident and their defense attorneys).[^27]

By the mid-1990s, the arrival of a new generation brought fresh factional divisions. Chen Shui-bian, Hsieh Chang-ting, and Su Feng-chang — who belonged neither fully to the New Force nor entirely to the Beautiful Island faction — gradually aligned into two new political lines. On September 28, 1992, Zhang Junxiong, Yao Jiayi, Hsieh Chang-ting, and Shi Mingde founded the **Welfare State Connection** (later known as Welfare State), advocating social welfare policies and pragmatic cross-strait approaches; in the late 1990s, the **Justice Connection**, centered around Chen Shui-bian, gradually took shape, leaning toward Taiwanese identity and deep local roots.[^28]

After Chen Shui-bian's presidential victory in 2000, the Justice Connection briefly became the strongest faction within the party. However, following Chen's departure in 2008 and the ensuing scandals, the Justice Connection declined rapidly. The Beautiful Island faction also fragmented throughout the 2000s into substreams such as "New Century" (led by Zhang Junhong) and "New Power" (Xu Xinxiang's line). The New Force, with its strong organizational discipline and consistent ideological stance, became the most enduring faction within the DPP — the "English faction" during the Tsai Ing-wen era and the "New Force" during the Lai Ching-te era both trace their lineage to this 1980s lineage.[^29]

The connection between factions and regional electoral bases represents another layer of the DPP's political geography. The New Force resonates strongly in southern Taiwan (particularly Yunlin and Chiayi), the Welfare State has deep roots in Kaohsiung, and the Justice Connection once dominated Greater Taipei. These regional factional structures rarely appear in central campaign platforms but influence every local election's nominations, endorsements, and mobilization efforts.[^30]

After Tsai Ing-wen's presidential election in 2016, the DPP's internal factional structure underwent another round of realignment. Tsai's relationship with the New Force evolved from cooperation to tension, and eventually to a substantive alliance, giving rise to the "English faction" — a sub-faction centered around Tsai's core advisors, overlapping ideologically with the New Force but maintaining independent organizational structures. After Lai Ching-te assumed the presidency in 2024, the New Force once again became the strongest organizational force within the party center. From 1987 to 2024, the New Force has endured nearly four decades — one of the longest-surviving factions in Taiwan's party political history.

### Evolution of KMT Factions

The KMT's factional structure existed from its relocation to Taiwan, though the meaning of "faction" varied across different periods. In the early days, the primary division within the party was between the **"mainstream faction" and the "non-mainstream faction"** — the mainstream faction, led by Lee Teng-hui, advocated localization and democratic reform; the non-mainstream faction, led by Hao Pucun, Lin Yanggang, and Li Huan, upheld the Chiang Kai-shek line and emphasized the ROC's constitutional legitimacy. The 1993 split of the New Party from the KMT marked the first major defection by the non-mainstream faction.[^31]

After the Lee Teng-hui era, the KMT's factional narrative gradually shifted from ideological divisions to personal "mountain top" allegiances: the **Lien faction** (Lien Chan, party chair 2000–2005), the **Ma faction** (Ma Ying-jeou, party chair 2005–2007 and 2009–2014), the **Zhu faction** (Zhu Lilun, party chair 2014–2016 and 2021–2025), and the **Han faction** (Han Kuo-yu, Kaohsiung mayor 2018–2020 and presidential candidate). Each faction developed its own grassroots networks, legislative representative connections, and media ecosystems. The Ma-Wang conflict (2013, regarding Wang Jin-ping's involvement in judicial corruption allegations) was the most representative internal dispute of this era.[^32]

The rise of Han Kuo-yu's "Han wave" in 2018 reshaped the KMT's grassroots mobilization logic — the Han faction introduced not a new policy platform but a "commoner / anti-establishment" campaign style. After Han's defeat in the 2020 presidential election, Zhu Lilun retook the party chairmanship in 2021, leading the KMT to a sweeping victory in the 2022 nine-in-one elections and managing the 2024 presidential nomination. On October 18, 2025, Cheng Liwen won the party chairmanship with 50.15% of the vote, succeeding Zhu upon term expiration. Born into the student movement generation and having started her political career in the DPP, Cheng Liwen became the second female party chair elected by direct party member vote since Hong Hsin-ping.[^33]

With Lu Shiue-yan maintaining high popularity in Taichung and Cheng Liwen assuming central leadership, the KMT's factional structure is undergoing another round of reshuffling. Whether Lu will run in the 2028 presidential election, how Cheng Liwen will coordinate with traditional factional power bases, and how the blue-white cooperation will be implemented at the municipal level — these variables will determine the trajectory of the KMT's factional landscape in the coming years. This article does not comment on individual politicians' strategies but records an observation: compared to the DPP, the KMT's factions tend to be more personality-centered, with relatively looser organizational discipline; the DPP's factions are more ideologically centered, with more institutionalized organizational mechanisms.[^34]

One long-underestimated thread in the KMT's factional structure is the localist faction. From Lee Teng-hui's localization drive, Wang Jin-ping's local network in the Legislative Yuan, to the emergence of populist discourse during Han Kuo-yu's 2018 campaign, the KMT's internal divide between "localists" and "mainlander-born" has persisted from the 1990s to today. This divide is directly tied to regional electoral performance — the KMT's long-standing advantage in central Taiwan (especially Taichung and Changhua) and parts of eastern constituencies is deeply connected to the localist faction's grassroots foundation.

### The Position of Third Forces

The TPP finds itself in a particularly delicate position in 2026. In the 2024 presidential election, Ko Wen-je secured 3.69 million votes (26.5%) — the highest single performance for a third-force candidate since Song Chuyu in 2000. However, after Ko was detained in connection with the Jhonghua City case at the end of 2024, the TPP entered a period of simultaneous decline in both organizational capacity and public visibility. Under new party chair Huang Kuo-chang, the party's orientation shifted from "transcending blue-green politics" to "blue-white cooperation."[^35]

The New Power Party lost three legislative seats in the 2020 legislative election and was left with zero party-list seats in 2024 — a clear sign of declining organizational strength. The Taiwan Foundation for Democracy also entered a downturn after Chen Pojui's failed legislative by-election in 2020. The true test for third forces in 2026 will be whether the TPP can convert its organizational capacity into local electoral support through blue-white cooperation while preserving its distinct identity.[^36]

From the founding of the New Party in 1993 to the blue-white cooperation of 2026, Taiwan's third forces have repeatedly followed a similar trajectory: born from dissatisfaction with the two major parties, rapidly rising under charismatic leadership, and gradually fading under the dual pressures of institutional constraints (single-member district two-vote system) and resource limitations (local organizational reach). Whether this cycle will be broken in 2026 is the next critical observation point in Taiwan's party political history.

It is worth noting that the single-member district two-vote system implemented in 2008 structurally compressed the survival space for third forces. Of the 73 district legislators elected under the single-member district system, the second-place candidate receives no seat. Even if a third force achieves a significant proportion of the national vote, it cannot translate into legislative representation unless it wins first place in individual constituencies. This institutional design has not only entrenched the dominance of the two major parties but also ensured that every rise of third forces ultimately faces the choice of "alliance or marginalization."
