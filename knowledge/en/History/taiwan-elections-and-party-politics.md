# Taiwan's Elections and Party Politics

> **30-Second Overview:** In the 1950s, "democratic showcase" elections only allowed for the election of legislators, not the president; by 1996, 14.31 million people cast their votes in the first direct presidential election in Chinese-speaking society.[^9] In less than half a century, Taiwan has undergone three transitions of power (2000, 2008, 2016), transforming elections from political decorations during the martial law era into a nationwide ritual held every four years. From the flames of the Zhongli Incident to the three-party standoff of 2024, democracy on this island was not merely designed; it was fought for, one ballot at a time.

On November 19, 1977, at Polling Station No. 213 in Zhongli City, Taoyuan County, a poll watcher was witnessed using an ink-stained thumb to deface ballots. As news spread, over 10,000 citizens surrounded the Zhongli Police Precinct. After nightfall, flames consumed the precinct building. Two people died: Jiang Wen-kuo, a student at Central University, who was shot in the head, and 19-year-old Chang Chih-ping, who fell in the street.[^1]

That fire did more than burn down a building. It sent a signal: Taiwanese voters would no longer tolerate stolen ballots. In that election, the opposition candidate Hsu Hsin-liang won a landslide victory over the Kuomintang's Ou Hsien-yü by 230,000 votes.[^2] The Zhongli Incident became the first spark of Taiwan's democratic movement. Ballots could be stolen, but the will of the people could not.

## The Evolution of a Ballot

### A Democratic Showcase (1950s)

In the 1950s, the Kuomintang (KMT) government needed to prove to the United States that it was "Free China." Local elections served as that "showcase," allowing the U.S. to see the forms of democracy while the KMT maintained tight control over the substance. The 1950 Taiwan Provincial Assembly election was the first post-war popularly elected election, but the rules were clear: citizens could elect assembly members, but not the Governor of Taiwan Province; they could criticize policies, but not question leadership[^3].

Even under strict control, elections opened cracks in the system. In 1951, Wu San-lien was elected Mayor of Taipei as an independent with a 65.6% vote share, becoming the first popularly elected mayor of the Republic of China's capital[^4]. The KMT supported him behind the scenes, but the mere fact that a "non-KMT" mayor stood at the helm of the Taipei City Government showed other local political figures what was possible.

### War on the Ballot (1970s)

In the 1970s, Taiwan faced successive shocks from being expelled from the United Nations (1971) and breaking diplomatic ties with the United States (1979), causing the KMT's legitimacy to begin to shake. As the middle class grew, _Dangwai_ (outside the party) voices began to emerge in elections.

In 1975, the 67-year-old Yilan politician Kuo Yu-hsin ran for the Legislative Yuan. When the results were announced, nearly 100,000 invalid ballots appeared in Yilan County alone—several times higher than the normal proportion. Later, during road construction, a bag of discarded valid ballots was unearthed; every single one had been marked for Kuo Yu-hsin. The lawyers who fought his election lawsuit—one named Yao Chia-wen and another named Lin Yi-hsiung—would both become defendants in the Formosa Incident four years later[^5].

Kuo Yu-hsin's votes were stolen. But that legal battle taught an entire generation of _Dangwai_ activists: the law could be used as a weapon.

### From the Underground to the Grand Hotel (1980s)

The Formosa Incident (1979) dealt a heavy blow to the _Dangwai_ movement, but it also nurtured the next generation. Defense lawyers for the arrested leaders—including Chen Shui-bian, Tu Cheng-yeh, and Su Tseng-chang—became the new faces of the democratic movement. The family members of those arrested, such as Lu Hsiu-lien, Chen Chu, and Chou Ching-yu, entered politics, becoming the female force of the Formosa generation.

On September 28, 1986, 132 _Dangwai_ activists announced the founding of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) in the Dunmu Hall on the second floor of the Grand Hotel in Taipei. This violated the "Regulations on Associations during the Period of Mobilization for the Suppression of Communist Rebellion" in effect at the time; forming a party was a crime. When news reached Chiang Ching-kuo, his aides suggested suppression. Chiang Ching-kuo said: "The times are changing, the trends are changing, and the environment is also changing."[^6]

Ten days later, Chiang Ching-kuo told _The Washington Post_ publisher Katharine Graham that Taiwan would lift martial law and open up its politics. The founding of the DPP was not suppressed; the KMT chose tolerance.

In the legislative election two months later, the DPP secured a 22.2% vote share and won 12 seats. A political party that was only sixty days old had already found its footing[^7].

## Electing Their Own President

### Full Re-election of the Legislature (1991–1992)

In 1991, the "long-term parliament" that had persisted for forty-three years finally underwent a full re-election. The veteran legislators and National Assembly members, who had been elected on the mainland in 1947 and never re-elected, stepped down. With the full re-election of the Legislative Yuan in 1992, Taiwanese people truly elected their own legislature for the first time[^8].

### 1996: Missiles and Ballots

On March 23, 1996, 14.31 million voters went to the polls to cast ballots in Taiwan's—and the Chinese-speaking world's—first direct presidential election.

Prior to the election, the CCP launched missiles into the waters off Taiwan in an attempt to intimidate voters. The result was counterproductive: voter turnout surged to 76%. Lee Teng-hui secured 5.81 million votes (54%), Peng Ming-min received 2.27 million (21.1%), Lin Yang-gang received 1.6 million (14.9%), and Chen Lü-an received 1.07 million (10%)[^9]. The missiles failed to deter anyone.

### 2000: A Three-Way Race and the First Rotation of Power

The 2000 presidential election was the most dramatic night in Taiwan's political history. The Kuomintang (KMT) split when former Taiwan Provincial Governor James Soong (Song Chu-yu) left the party to run, splitting the Pan-Blue vote with the KMT nominee, Lien Chan.

The results: Chen Shui-bian received 4.97 million votes (39.3%), James Soong received 4.66 million votes (36.8%), and Lien Chan received 2.92 million votes (23.1%). The Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) won power with less than 40% of the vote[^10].

The key was not who won, but how those who lost behaved. The KMT lost 55 years of rule without deploying the military or staging a coup. Lien Chan and James Soong conceded defeat. Power transferred peacefully. Taiwan proved for the first time that its democracy was more than just theory.

### 2004: Two Bullets

On March 19, 2004, the day before the election, Chen Shui-bian and Annette Lu (Lu Hsiu-lien) were shot while campaigning on the streets of Tainan. One bullet grazed Chen Shui-bian's abdomen, and another hit the knee guard worn by Annette Lu. Both were discharged from the hospital the same day[^11].

The following day, Chen Shui-bian defeated Lien Chan by a margin of fewer than 30,000 votes. Pan-Blue supporters gathered in front of the Presidential Office to protest, questioning whether the shooting was staged. The primary suspect, Chen Yi-hsiung, was found drowned in Anping Harbor, Tainan, ten days later. The truth remains a subject of controversy.

This was the moment Taiwanese democracy came closest to breaking. The election results were questioned by nearly half of the voters, and street protests continued for weeks. Ultimately, however, legal procedures did their job: ballot counting, litigation, and court rulings. The system held firm.

## The Fission of the Political Landscape

Taiwan's political party history is not as simple as a "two-party system." It is a history of constant splitting, restructuring, death, and rebirth.

Internal ideological conflicts within the Kuomintang (KMT) erupted in the 1990s. In 1993, the New KMT Alliance, dissatisfied with Lee Teng-hui's "localization" direction, broke away to form the **New Party**. In 2000, after James Soong's defeat in the presidential election, he established the **People First Party**. In 2001, after Lee Teng-hui was expelled from the KMT, he supported the formation of the **Taiwan Solidarity Union**[^12].

There have also been fissures on the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) side. In 2015, the energy from the Sunflower Movement transformed into **New Power Party**, which advocated for a more radical Taiwanese identity platform than the DPP. In 2019, Taipei Mayor Ko Wen-je started his own movement by founding the **Taiwan People's Party**, attempting to forge a "third way" that transcends the Blue and Green camps[^13].

By 2024, Taiwan's political landscape had largely stabilized into three major forces: the DPP (Green), the KMT (Blue), and the TPP (White). However, history tells us that this landscape could undergo fission once again at any time.

## How Taiwanese People Vote

### Campaign Rallies: A Democratic Carnival

The most unique spectacle of elections in Taiwan is the campaign rally. Candidates rent out plazas and set up stages where entertainers perform to warm up the crowd, while supporters wave flags and chant "Dong-san! Dong-san!" (a homophone for the Taiwanese term for "elected").

A rally is more than just political mobilization; it is a community festival. Vendors set up stalls along the road, children sit on their fathers' shoulders, and elderly people bring stools to claim seats. Elections are one of the few activities in Taiwan that simultaneously satisfy political participation, social needs, and entertainment consumption.

### Returning Home to Vote: An Annual Migration

To this day, Taiwan does not have an absentee voting system. Voters must cast their ballots in person at their registered household location; there is no mail-in voting and no early voting[^14].

This means that whenever an election day arrives, a large-scale "returning home to vote" migration occurs in Taiwan. People from Kaohsiung working in Taipei or people from Yunlin working in Hsinchu scramble to buy train tickets to go home and vote. The High Speed Rail adds extra services, and bus companies deploy additional vehicles. On election days, Taiwan High Speed Rail stations are even more crowded than during the Lunar New Year.

Opponents of absentee voting worry that if the more than one million Taiwanese people working in China were allowed to vote remotely, they might be influenced by Beijing. Supporters, however, argue that not allowing people to vote where they reside is a disguised barrier to voting. There is still no consensus on this controversy.

### Election Night

Election counting in Taiwan is a nationwide live broadcast. After polling stations close at 4:00 PM, election officials open the ballot boxes on the spot and count the votes one by one. Television stations set up cameras at every counting station for real-time broadcasting.

No other country's vote counting is more transparent than Taiwan's. You can stand outside a polling station and witness with your own eyes every single ballot being displayed, read aloud, and recorded. From the closing of the polls to when the results are largely determined, it usually takes only three to four hours.

## Key Moments in Recent Years

### 2018: The Han Wave and Referendum Tsunami

The 2018 local elections were a political earthquake. Kuomintang's Han Kuo-yu, appearing as an outsider, descended upon Kaohsiung and defeated the Democratic Progressive Party's Chen Chi-mai with 890,000 votes, ending 20 years of DPP rule in Kaohsiung[^15]. Across Taiwan, the KMT won 15 county and city mayoral seats, while the DPP was left with only 6. Tsai Ing-wen resigned as party chairperson.

The simultaneous referendums were even more shocking. Ten referendum items covered energy, food safety, same-sex marriage, and gender equality education; voters faced ten ballots at once, resulting in long queues at polling stations. Among them, the referendum for "marriage limited to one man and one woman" passed, causing a direct collision between public opinion and the law on Taiwan's path toward becoming Asia's first country to legalize same-sex marriage[^16].

### 2020: The 8.17 Million Votes

Two years later, the tide of the 2020 presidential election completely reversed. The Hong Kong anti-extradition bill movement made society's fears regarding "One Country, Two Systems" concrete. Tsai Ing-wen launched a successful counterattack from the heavy defeat of 2018, securing **8,170,186 votes**—the highest number of votes ever received by any candidate in Taiwan's electoral history. Han Kuo-yu received 5.52 million votes, with a voter turnout of 74.9%[^17].

The number "817" later became a political symbol, used by supporters as an emblem of democratic will and by opponents as evidence of political polarization.

### 2024: A Three-Way Race Replayed

The 2024 presidential election saw a return to the three-way rivalry seen in 2000. Lai Ching-te (DPP) won with 5.58 million votes (40%), followed by Hou Yu-ih (KMT) with 4.67 million votes (33.5%) and Ko Wen-je (Taiwan People's Party) with 3.69 million votes (26.5%)[^18].

This marked the first time in Taiwan's history of direct presidential elections that a candidate was elected with less than 50% of the vote, and also the first time a political party has won three consecutive presidential elections. At the same time, the DPP lost its majority in the Legislative Yuan—with executive and legislative powers split between different camps, Taiwan entered a new era of "divided government."

## An Evolving Democracy

In 2005, the National Assembly voted to abolish itself—this institution, elected in Nanjing in 1947, entered history after completing its final mission in Taiwan. The same constitutional amendment reduced the number of seats in the Legislative Yuan from 225 to 113, changed the term length from three years to four, and shifted the electoral system to a single-member district, two-vote system. Future constitutional amendments must pass via a national referendum, with a threshold so high it is nearly impossible to overcome[^19].

Taiwan's electoral system continues to evolve. A 2022 referendum proposal sought to lower the voting age from 20 to 18, but because this required a constitutional amendment, it needed the consent of more than 9.61 million people—ultimately, only 5.64 million voted in favor, and it failed to pass. Money politics, disinformation, and electoral reform remain unfinished challenges.

From the flames of the 1977 Zhongli Incident to the 14 million people quietly queuing to vote in 2024—in less than half a century, Taiwan has transformed elections from a right that required setting fires to protect into a habit that functions automatically without anyone needing to remind them.

Not every election is perfect. But every time, those who lose depart, and those who win take office. On this planet, that fact is not as self-evident as it may seem.

## Political Landscape After 2024

The January 2024 election results did more than just decide who would be president; they redrew the power map for the next four years. Lai Ching-te and Hsiao Bi-khim entered the Presidential Office with a 40.1% vote share, but the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) secured only 51 seats in the Legislative Yuan, while the Kuomintang (KMT) took 52 and the Taiwan People's Party (TPP) held 8. No single party held a majority. Taiwan entered its first instance in constitutional history of a strict "minority government" (朝小野大)—where the ruling party simultaneously lost both the legislative majority and the speakership[^20].

### Minority Government: Blitzkriegs and Procedural Disputes

Starting in May 2024, the KMT and TPP used their combined majority of 60 seats to push through "Parliamentary Reform Bills," granting the Legislative Yuan greater powers of investigation and subpoena. The DPP accused the Blue-White coalition of using its numerical advantage to "bypass committee scrutiny" by moving bills directly to a second reading. Subsequently, in Constitutional Judgment No. 9 of Year 113, the Constitutional Court declared several provisions unconstitutional[^21].

Over the following eighteen months, similar procedural disputes recurred. Budget reviews, amendments to the _Financial Holding Company Act_, proposals regarding Article 29 of the _Act Governing Relations between the People of the Taiwan Area and the Mainland Area_, and amendments to the \_Public Officials Election and Recall Act"—every vote in the Legislative Yuan was accompanied by occupations of the rostrum, physical confrontations, and late-night voting sessions. While Blue-White cooperation demonstrated the power of a majority, the two parties were not always in sync. The TPP maintained a reserved attitude toward certain controversial bills, and some KMT legislators publicly expressed dissent against party caucus resolutions. The stability of this majority alliance had to be reconfirmed before every single vote.

### The 2025 Mass Recall

When checks and balances within the system failed to function, civil society activated the final tool granted by the Constitution: the power of recall. Starting in February 2025, over 1.3 million people participated in signature drives to launch recalls against 31 KMT legislators and Hsinchu Mayor Kao Hung-an[^22].

On July 26, the first wave of voting for 25 cases, and on August 23, a second wave for 7 cases, all failed to pass. In some constituencies, the "agree" votes did indeed cross the threshold of 25% of the total registered voters (such as in the cases of Wang Hung-wei, Hsu Chiao-hsin, and Fu Kun-chi), but the "disagree" votes were generally higher, sometimes even exceeding the original vote counts that had elected those legislators[^23].

While the recall results did not change the seat count in the Legislative Yuan, they left several traces worth long-term observation. The mobilization energy demonstrated by civil society during the signature phase was unprecedented—whether this energy will extend to the 2026 local elections remains unknown. The position of third-party forces (TPP, New Power Party, and Taiwan Advance Party) during the recall process remained relatively ambiguous; most TPP supporters cast "disagree" votes or abstained, posing a test for whether the "White camp" can sustain its "third way" in 2026. Another tension lies in the relationship between mobilization and civic will: in certain constituencies, the number of "agree" votes exceeded the legislators' original winning votes, yet they still lost to a more integrated "disagree" mobilization. Whether direct democracy tools can meet their objectives in a highly polarized environment is a question Taiwan's politics will face in every election following 2025[^24].

For the full context of these events, see [Mass Recall](/en/history/great-recall-movement-2024).

### Blue-White Cooperation and the 2026 Institutional Test

On March 18, 2026, the KMT Central Standing Committee and the TPP Central Committee passed the "2026 Joint Governance and Local Election Cooperation Agreement" on the same day. The agreement was jointly promoted by the newly elected KMT Chairperson Cheng Li-wen (as of October 2025) and TPP Chairperson Huang Ko-chang. It established five core pillars: a shared policy vision, a mechanism for cooperation in nominating county magistrates and city mayors, methods for integrated execution, a joint campaign support mechanism, and post-election cooperative governance. For mayoral and magistrate integration, the principle was "nominate first, integrate later, then jointly recommend one person"; if necessary, the candidate would be decided via a primary, with question types designed to contrast against major opponents[^25].

The three counties/cities where the agreement was prioritized for launch were New Taipei City, Chiayi City, and Yilan County. The common characteristics of these three areas are: long-term Blue-Green competition, a previous local electoral base for third-party forces, and the expiration of the current incumbents' terms[^26].

This agreement represents a new form in the history of Taiwan's political party cooperation. In 2020, Tsai Ing-wen and Hsiao Bi-khim's "Presidential/Vice Presidential ticket" was a partnership within the same party; in 2024, Ko Wen-je and Wu Hsin-ying were nominees within the TPP. The 2026 Blue-White cooperation, however, is an institutional integration between two independent parties at the local election level—shared polling, shared nominations, and joint governance. It also differs from the "Blue-White cooperation" that collapsed in late 2023 (the Hou-Ko pairing for the presidential election): a presidential election has only one position, making integration a zero-sum game; local elections involve 22 counties and cities, allowing for regional negotiations.

The success or failure of Blue-White cooperation will backflow to affect the party landscape of the 2028 presidential election. If integration goes smoothly, the cooperation model between third forces and major parties may become a new political structure; if it fails, the TPP may return to the independent path seen during Ko Wen-je's era in 2024. This article does not predict the outcome but records this node on March 18, 2026: Taiwan's party politics is attempting a new, historically unproven form of cooperation.

### Evolution of DPP Factions

The DPP has not been a monolith since its founding. The 132 people who founded the party at the Grand Hotel in 1986 came from various backgrounds, including the _Tangwai_ Editorial and Writers Association, the _Tangwai_ Public Affairs Committee, and local political figures. At its inception, there were two major factions: the **New Tide Faction** (which originated from the _Tangwai_ Editorial and Writers Association founded in 1983 and was formally organized in 1987 by figures such as Chiu Yi-jen, Wu Nai-jen, and Lin Tuo-shui) and the **Formosa Faction** (which evolved from the "Formosa Connection" within the _Tangwai_ Public Affairs Committee, centered around the victims of the Formosa Incident and their defense lawyers)[^27].

In the mid-1990s, the emergence of the "New Generation" brought new factional divisions. A group of middle-ground figures—including Chen Shui-bian, Frank Hsieh, and Su Tseng-chang, who belonged neither strictly to New Tide nor fully to Formosa—gradually formed two new paths: on September 28, 1992, Chang Chun-hsiung, Yao Chia-wen, Frank Hsieh, and Shih Ming-teh established the "**Welfare State Connection**" (later known as _Welfare State_), advocating for social welfare policies and a pragmatic cross-strait approach; in the mid-to-late 1990s, the "**Justice Connection**," centered around Chen Shui-bian, gradually took shape, leaning toward Taiwanese identity and deep local roots[^28].

After Chen Shui-bian was elected president in 2000, the Justice Connection briefly became the strongest faction within the party. However, following Chen's departure from office in 2008 and the subsequent legal cases, the Justice Connection rapidly declined. The Formosa Faction also gradually split into branches such as "New Century" (led by Chang Chun-hung) and "New Power" (the Hsieh Shin-liang line) during the 2000s. New Tide, due to its strong organizational discipline and consistent ideological line, became the most enduring faction within the DPP—both the "English-line" (referring to Tsai Ing-wen's era) and the current New Tide dominance under Lai Ching-te are linked to this lineage continuing from the 1980s[^29].

The correlation between factions and regional electoral bases forms another layer of the DPP's political geography. New Tide resonates with the traditional "Southern Hegemony" in southern Taiwan (especially Yunlin, Chiayi, and Tainan); the Welfare State Connection has deep roots in Kaohsiung; and the Justice Connection was once an electoral guarantee in the Greater Taipei area. These regional factional structures do not appear in central election platforms, but they influence nominations, campaigning, and mobilization in every local election[^30].

After Tsai Ing-wen was elected president in 2016, another wave of restructuring occurred within the party's factional hierarchy. The relationship between Tsai Ing-wen and New Tide moved from cooperation to tension, and finally to a substantive alliance, gradually shaping the "English-line"—a sub-faction centered around Tsai Ing-wen's core staff that highly overlaps with New Tide's ideology but remains organizationally independent. After Lai Ching-te took office in 2024, New Tide once again became the strongest organizational force within the party central committee. From 1987 to 2024, New Tide has spanned nearly forty years, making it one of the longest-lived factions in Taiwan's political history.

### Evolution of KMT Factions

The KMT's factional structure has existed since its move to Taiwan, but the meaning of the term "faction" has shifted across different eras. In the early days, the largest divide was between the "**Mainstream Faction vs. Non-mainstream Faction**"—the Mainstream Faction, led by Lee Teng-hui, advocated for localization and democratic reform; the Non-mainstream Faction, centered around Hau Pei-tsun, Lin Yang-kang, and Lee Huan, advocated for continuing the "Two Chiang" line and emphasized the legal legitimacy of the Republic of China. The 1993 departure of the New Kuomintang (NP) marked the first large-scale split of the Non-mainstream Faction[^31].

Following the Lee Teng-hui era, the KMT's factional narrative shifted from ideological divides to "personality-centered" power blocs: the **Lien Faction** (Lien Chan, Party Chair 2000–2005), the **Ma Faction** (Ma Ying-jeou, Party Chair 2005–2007 and 2009–2014), the **Chu Faction** (Eric Chu, Party Chair 2014–2016 and 2021–2025), and the **Han Faction** (Han Kuo-yu, Kaohsiung Mayor and presidential candidate 2018–2020). Each faction possesses its own local organizations, legislative networks, and media ecosystems. The Ma-Wang political struggle (in 2013, regarding Ma Ying-jeou's handling of Wang Jin-pyng's judicial interference allegations) was the most representative internal conflict of this stage[^32].

The rise of Han Kuo-yu's "Han Wave" in 2018 reshuffled the political mobilization logic of the KMT grassroots—the Han Faction brought not a new policy line, but a "populist/anti-establishment" campaign style. After Han Kuo-yu's presidential defeat in 2020, Eric Chu reclaimed the chairmanship in 2021, leading the KMT to a landslide victory in the 2022 nine-in-one elections and managing the 2024 presidential nominations. On October 18, 2025, Cheng Li-wen was elected Party Chair with 50.15% of the vote, succeeding the term-limited Eric Chu. Coming from a generation of student activists and starting her political career in the DPP, Cheng Li-wen is the second female chair in KMT history to be elected through direct party member voting after Hung Hsiu-chu[^33].

With Lu Show-ray maintaining high popularity in Taichung after 2024 and Cheng Li-wen taking charge of the central party, the KMT's factional structure is undergoing a new round of reshuffling. Whether Lu Show-ray runs for president in 2028, how Cheng Li-wen coordinates with traditional power blocs within the party, and how Blue-White cooperation is executed at the county/city level—these variables will determine the direction of the KMT's factional landscape in the coming years. This article does not comment on individual political strategies but records one observation: compared to the DPP, KMT factions tend to be "personality-centered" with relatively loose organizational discipline; whereas DPP factions are closer to being "ideology-centered" with more institutionalized organizational mechanisms[^34].

There is another long-undervalued thread in the KMT's factional structure: the localizationist/indigenous faction. From the localization line of the Lee Teng-hui era and Wang Jin-pyng's local networks in the Legislative Yuan, to the populist discourse that emerged during Han Kuo-yu's 2018 campaign, the divide between "localizationists" and "Mainlanders" within the KMT has persisted from the 1990s to the present. This thread is directly related to regional electoral bases—the KMT's long-term advantage in central Taiwan (especially Taichung and Changhua) and parts of the eastern constituencies is deeply connected to the local foundation of the localizationist faction.

### The Position of Third Forces

The TPP occupies a unique position heading into 2026. In the 2024 presidential election, Ko Wen-je secured 3.69 million votes (26.5%), the highest single vote count for a third-party candidate since James Soong in 2000. However, after Ko Wen-je was detained in late 2024 due to the Jinghua City case, the TPP entered a period of simultaneous decline in both organization and public visibility. Huang Ko-chang took over as Party Chair, shifting the party's direction from "transcending Blue and Green" toward "Blue-White cooperation"[^35].

The New Power Party lost three seats in the 2020 legislative elections and faced a predicament in 2024 with zero at-large seats, indicating a clear decline in organizational energy. The Taiwan Advance Party also entered a low period following the failure of Chen Po-wei's legislative by-election in 2020. The true test for third forces in 2026 is whether the TPP can translate its organizational energy into local electoral success through Blue-White cooperation without losing its own identity[^36].

From the founding of the New Party in 1993 to the Blue-White cooperation of 2026, Taiwan's third forces have repeatedly followed a similar trajectory: born from dissatisfaction with the two major parties, rising rapidly through charismatic leadership, and gradually waning under the dual pressures of the institutional system (the single-member district two-vote system) and resources (local organizations). Whether this cycle will be broken in 2026 is the next observation point in Taiwan's political history.

It is worth noting that the single-member district two-vote system implemented in 2008 has structurally compressed the survival space for third forces. Of the 73 district seats in the Legislative Yuan election, the candidate with the second-highest vote share receives no seat. Even if a third force achieves a certain percentage of the national vote, as long as they cannot secure first place in local districts, their seats will fail to reflect voter support. This institutional design has institutionalized the advantage of the two major parties and forces every rise of a third force to eventually face the choice between "alliance or marginalization."

[^1]: [The Reporter: 40th Anniversary Special on the Zhongli Incident (2017)](https://www.twreporter.org/a/zhongli-incident-40-years) — Citizens surrounded the Zhongli Police Precinct to protest election fraud, resulting in the deaths of Jiang Wen-kuo and Chang Chih-ping.
