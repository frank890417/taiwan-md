# Agricultural Cooperatives Do More Than Sell Fertilizer: How Taiwan's Rural Public Organizations Evolved from Colonial Institutions to Credit Department Reforms

In 1900, Taiwan’s first agricultural cooperative was established in what is now Sanxia, New Taipei City. In 1943, during the late Japanese colonial period, agricultural cooperatives, industrial associations, and other agricultural groups were merged into Agricultural Associations. In 1949, post-war agricultural cooperatives and cooperatives were merged again. [^1] [^2]

In contemporary local life, agricultural cooperatives may simply appear as the building where one buys fertilizer, applies for loans, attends exhibitions, or takes agricultural courses. Only by extending the timeline can one discover that this seemingly familiar interface once served as a conduit for agricultural production, rural finance, local administration, and political power. Its survival to the present day relies on successive regimes and rural policies continuously rearranging service targets, management methods, and resource flows.

> **30-Second Overview:** The history of Taiwan’s agricultural cooperatives encompasses both farmer mutual aid and state governance. Technology, capital, and agricultural policies enter the local level through them, while local elites, elections, and resource competition leave their marks within these organizations. The role of agricultural cooperatives has gradually taken shape at the intersection of these two trajectories.

## The First Agricultural Cooperative in Sanjiaoyong

The Ministry of Agriculture’s historical articles record that Taiwan’s first agricultural cooperative was established in 1900 in a place then called Sanjiaoyong, which corresponds to the area of modern-day Sanxia. Its original purposes included assisting the Japanese colonial administration in collecting land rent, improving arable land, and encouraging pig and fish farming. According to the Agricultural Cooperative Law promulgated by Japan in 1899, existing agricultural groups were unified under the name “Agricultural Cooperative” and granted legal person status. [^1]

From the outset, agricultural cooperatives bore dual responsibilities. While ostensibly aimed at improving rural production, they also assisted in administrative execution and accommodated the interests of the landlord class. Viewing them solely as civil cooperative organizations would overlook their connection to the ruling apparatus. Viewing them solely as administrative organs would obscure their actual operation within the local economy.

This hybridity explains why agricultural cooperatives could take root locally. They possessed agricultural technology and production improvement work, as well as an organizational position that could assist in administrative execution. For the state, agricultural cooperatives were an existing network for entering rural areas. For local elites, they were a public arena for acquiring information, resources, and institutional status.

Airiti’s research on agricultural cooperative archives indicates that Taiwan’s three-tier agricultural cooperative system was established during the Japanese colonial period. The organization and its operations were frequently influenced by the rulers’ policy needs, laws, administrative orders, and funding subsidies. Archives preserved by local agricultural cooperatives also record institutional reforms, legal revisions, and regime changes. [^4]

## In 1943, Agricultural Cooperatives Became Agricultural Associations

In 1943, to strengthen control over Taiwan’s agricultural economic activities, Japan promulgated the Agricultural Group Law, integrating agricultural cooperatives, livestock associations, federations of industrial associations, federations of fruit and vegetable trade associations, and fertilizer distribution cooperatives into a single entity called “Agricultural Associations.” [^1]

This reorganization changed the flow of resources. Production, finance, marketing, materials, and distribution, previously scattered among different groups, were now consolidated into a single agricultural organization. The wartime government could therefore more easily mobilize materials through Agricultural Associations and transmit orders to the countryside.

Airiti’s research on post-war agricultural cooperatives refers to this history as one of the institutional legacies. The research indicates that while the Japanese colonial末期 Agricultural Associations and industrial association systems were officially merged into new organizations, the post-war government did not immediately form a consistent approach after taking over Taiwan. The Taiwan Provincial Administrative Executive Yuan tended to maintain the Agricultural Associations, while the Central Social Affairs Department, which supervised cooperatives, demanded that cooperatives be separated. [^2]

Therefore, the history of agricultural cooperatives cannot be dismissed with the eight characters “established in the Japanese colonial period, continued post-war.” The new government post-war took over a batch of local organizations whose assets, members, operations, and powers overlapped. How agricultural cooperatives and cooperatives were handled ultimately became an issue of the new regime rearranging rural resources.

## Post-War Division and Re-Merger

The Ministry of Agriculture’s historical articles record that after the liberation of Taiwan, Agricultural Associations were reorganized into two organizations: Agricultural Cooperatives and Cooperatives. Agricultural Cooperatives were responsible for agricultural extension and的事业 safeguarding farmers’ rights, while Cooperatives handled economic affairs. In 1949, the two merged again. In 1950, a second merger was completed under the intervention of the Sino-American Joint Commission on Rural Reconstruction (JCRR). In 1953, reorganization was conducted according to the Interim Measures for Improving Agricultural Cooperatives at All Levels in Taiwan Province. The merger and reorganization were completed in 1954. [^1]

The division and merger of agricultural cooperatives and cooperatives involved far more than administrative efficiency. Airiti’s archive research indicates that the issue stemmed partly from differences in the legal systems of old and new regimes, and partly from the control exerted by different central political forces over cooperative affairs. In 1949, Chen Cheng advocated for re-merger. Related disputes continued until the 1953 improvement of agricultural cooperatives, subsiding only after Chiang Kai-shek supported the merger. [^2]

Therefore, the 1950s improvement of agricultural cooperatives should be viewed as a process of institutional construction rather than a single consolidation. Lin Bao’an’s research indicates that the post-war initial agricultural cooperative improvement policy established the basic system whereby agricultural cooperatives would possess multiple functions and rural monopolies, integrating Japanese colonial legacies, the spirit of the Mainland China’s Agricultural Cooperative Law, JCRR’s proposals, and post-war Japanese rural reform experiences into a single institutional framework. [^7]

This system placed agricultural extension, supply and marketing, distribution, and credit businesses in the same local entity. Farmers could access production technology, materials, loans, and sales channels within the same organization. The influence of agricultural cooperatives thus extended beyond the number of members to the businesses and resources they controlled.

> **Curator’s Note:** The key to the improvement of agricultural cooperatives was not that the organization became simple, but that different institutional legacies were placed into the same rural public institution. Subsequent agricultural cooperatives thus carried characteristics of cooperation, administration, and politics simultaneously.

## Why the Credit Department is the Core of Agricultural Cooperatives

The relationship between agricultural cooperatives and their credit departments is one of the most important entry points for understanding post-war agricultural cooperatives. Ding Wenyu’s research divides the development timeline and major events of agricultural cooperatives and credit departments into ten distinct relational stages. The research abstract indicates that the surplus and number of employees of the credit department hold important positions within agricultural cooperatives; the relationship between agricultural cooperatives and credit departments can be viewed as a microcosm of the development of Taiwan’s agricultural cooperatives. [^3]

The position of the credit department within agricultural cooperatives extends far beyond a supplementary business. It provides capital and supports the organizational scale and local service capacity of agricultural cooperatives. When farmers deposit savings or walk into the counter to apply for loans, the agricultural cooperative transforms from an institution promoting new varieties and holding seminars into a public interface encountered when families arrange their livelihoods.

The larger the scale of the credit department, the harder it is to hide risks in local ledgers. The Ministry of Agriculture, reviewing agricultural finance reforms, noted that the government designated banks to take over 36 poorly managed agricultural and fisheries credit departments in 2001 and 2002. After the implementation of the Agricultural Finance Act in 2004, the Agricultural Finance Bureau was responsible for supervision and guidance, handling issues such as the transfer of credit departments to banks, re-establishment, and takeover mergers. [^5]

The difficulty of credit department reform lies in its simultaneous承担 of local financial services, policy-oriented agricultural loans, and farmers’ trust. Supervision cannot pursue zero risk alone, nor can it ignore the convenience for farmers to obtain funds locally. The agricultural finance system faces precisely this tension.

## After 2003, Credit Departments Were Integrated into a Larger Financial System

Before credit department reforms, agricultural cooperative counters faced very specific local needs: farmers needed to buy fertilizer, cover wages during busy farming seasons, find funds for the next season’s crops, or handle old debts left by poor harvests. These needs could not necessarily be processed by standard bank branch procedures. The existence of agricultural cooperative credit departments allowed financial services to operate with the rhythm of local industries.

Problems began here as well. If credit departments relied excessively on a few local industries or networks, lending risks would concentrate. If supervision only looked at reports, it might ignore the crops, land, and family livelihoods behind a single loan. The reforms of the 2000s were precisely about redrawing the line between local characteristics and financial rules.

The official establishment history of the National Agricultural Bank records that the government convened the National Agricultural Finance Conference on November 30, 2002, and completed the legislation of the Agricultural Finance Act on July 10, 2003. On January 30, 2004, the Agricultural Finance Bureau was established, promoting the National Agricultural Bank. On May 26, 2005, the National Agricultural Bank was approved for operation. According to the Agricultural Finance Act, the National Agricultural Bank and the credit departments of agricultural and fisheries cooperatives jointly constitute agricultural financial institutions, with the bank serving as the upper-tier institution for the credit departments of agricultural and fisheries cooperatives. [^6]

This change did not sever the agricultural cooperative credit departments from the agricultural cooperatives; rather, it placed them into a larger agricultural financial supervision network. Sun Tingxuan’s research used agricultural cooperative credit departments as samples to analyze efficiency before and after agricultural finance reform. The abstract indicates that after the 2003 Agricultural Finance Act, the Agricultural Finance Bureau began guiding previously taken-over credit departments. While the reform improved financial supervision capabilities, 13 of the 14 re-established credit departments still had poor efficiency. [^8]

This result illustrates that institutional reform can make financial data clearer and supervision mechanisms more complete, but it does not mean local organizations immediately recover health. The problems of agricultural cooperative credit departments involve personnel, resource allocation, lending, local industries, and management capabilities. Different regions have different industrial structures, and the borrowing demands and risks faced by credit departments will not be identical. Agricultural finance reform is therefore not just a set of supervision rules, but a long-term project that must address differences in local industries.

## Are Agricultural Cooperatives Farmer Organizations or Local Power Arenas?

The multi-functionality of agricultural cooperatives gives them a strong local presence, making “who do agricultural cooperatives actually represent” a long-standing issue. The Ministry of Agriculture’s historical articles indicate that after the completion of the 1954 reorganization, agricultural cooperative businesses developed rapidly, creating a group of rural elites. Agricultural cooperatives became political stages with resources at the local level, with general secretaries consolidating resources through credit departments. [^1]

One cannot simply label all agricultural cooperatives as tools of local factions. Agricultural cooperatives in different regions have different membership structures, industrial conditions, cadre traditions, and financial scales. However, research does remind us that there may be a gap between the legal identity and actual operation of agricultural cooperatives. While nominally representing farmers, their operation is jointly influenced by upper-level policies, subsidies, laws, local elites, and credit department resources. [^4]

The importance of agricultural cooperative archives lies in allowing these abstract statements to be grounded on paper. Membership rosters can show who is included or excluded. Lists of directors and supervisors can track local elites. Business reports can reveal fund flows. Extension records can show what the state hopes farmers will plant and what technologies they will adopt. Agricultural cooperatives also preserve traces of how the local area responded to policies, such as how members applied for materials, how credit departments handled loans, and how cadres translated central regulations into locally executable work. Agricultural cooperatives are not just a set of central regulations; they exist in a vast amount of local documentation.

Membership qualification is another often overlooked entry point. The Ministry of Agriculture’s historical articles record that after the 1953 reorganization, agricultural cooperatives divided members into regular members and sponsoring members, with different voting and eligibility rights for each. This indicates that “farmers being organized” does not mean every person related to agriculture has the same organizational rights. Who can participate in the general assembly and who can become a director or supervisor directly affects how agricultural cooperative resources are distributed. [^1]

The membership system also explains why agricultural cooperatives became local political stages. Their influence comes from credit department funds, membership rosters, representative seats, extension networks, and government-commissioned businesses. When studying agricultural cooperatives, one must not only look at what services they provide for farmers but also ask who has the power to determine the direction of those services.

## Seeing Taiwan’s Rural Institutional Memory Through Agricultural Cooperatives

![The old building of the Tienchung Farmers Association, photographed in 2012, presenting the continuity of local agricultural cooperatives as daily service spaces](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2a/TienchungFarmersAssociation.JPG)

> **Visual Historical Material:** This photo was taken in 2012. The old building of the Tienchung Farmers Association in the image is not a Japanese colonial period photo and cannot alone prove the building’s construction date. Its value lies in presenting how agricultural cooperatives continue to appear in streets, financial services, and administrative services through a local building. The photo author is Shxpeng, licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 3.0 Unported. [^10]

The history of agricultural cooperatives connects several historical threads often kept separate. Japanese colonial agricultural governance, post-war land reform, rural organizations, agricultural finance, local politics, and agricultural modernization meet within this institution. It is not just one chapter among many; it is also a place where one can see how institutions are implemented.

Lin Bao’an’s research suggests that the 1950s improvement of agricultural cooperatives, besides changing the power structure of agricultural cooperatives, also had the significance of maintaining and diffusing the social effects of land reform. [^7] This does not mean agricultural cooperatives equal land reform, nor that all reform achievements were completed by agricultural cooperatives, but that agricultural cooperatives provided the grassroots channels for managing rural areas, disseminating policies, and organizing production after land reform.

After 2003, the Agricultural Finance Act and the National Agricultural Bank connected agricultural cooperative credit departments to the national financial supervision system. Agricultural cooperatives must also face financial risks, supervision standards, and changes in rural population structures, no longer able to operate solely according to the local organizational habits formed in the 1950s. [^5] [^6] [^8]

When discussing agricultural cooperatives today, it is easy to first ask if they still exist and if they are profitable. A harder and more important question is: after the decrease in the agricultural population, rural aging, and the changing landscape of local industries, can agricultural cooperatives still allow farmers to obtain technology, credit, and public information in familiar places? The answer will not be singular. However, this question brings the history of agricultural cooperatives back to rural daily life, allowing us to see how institutions distribute resources and how people remember an organization.

Agricultural cooperatives were not preserved in their original form from the colonial period to the present. In 1900, they became legal entities for governing rural areas. In 1943, they were integrated into Agricultural Associations. Post-war, they divided and merged between agricultural cooperatives and cooperatives, redefined in the 1950s, and later connected to the national agricultural finance system through credit departments. Their continuation relies on constant transformation.

The historical position of agricultural cooperatives lies here. The state’s agricultural policies enter local daily life through them, and local resource competition returns to state institutions through them. Only by clearly seeing this back-and-forth path can one understand how agricultural cooperatives both provide services and bear the pressures of control and competition.
