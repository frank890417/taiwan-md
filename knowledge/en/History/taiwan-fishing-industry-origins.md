# The Fishing Port Is Not Just for Setting Sail: How Taiwan's Fishing Industry Was Remade by Trawler Ships, Fish Markets, and a Cohort of Technocrats

In 1909, the Taiwan Governor-General's Office built the Linghai Maru survey vessel, beginning to use ships as laboratories to investigate coastal fisheries. In 1929, the Keelung jawfish experimental factory, the Tainan brackish-water aquaculture experimental station, and the Hsiaoli freshwater aquaculture experimental station merged to form the Taiwan Governor-General's Office Marine Experiment Station. In 1937, the Japanese colonial government built the Dapeng Bay fishing port through land reclamation. [^1] [^2] [^6]

Viewed together, the history of Taiwan's fishing industry is not merely a curve of output. Ships must know where the fish are; experimental stations must turn the ocean into measurable knowledge; ports must let ships and cargo come ashore; fish markets must send the catch into price, cold storage, distribution, and family dining tables.

The modernization of the fishing industry was never completed by a single new ship setting sail. It required a system capable of connecting sea conditions, fishing gear, ice-making, education, finance, ports, and local transactions. This system brought work and income, and placed coastal residents into larger administrative, capital, and international markets.

> **30-second overview:** The modernization of Taiwan's fishing industry did not begin only on the ships. The sea was broken into data, ports were transformed into equipment, fish entered markets and cold chains, and technology was organized into teachable, investable, and governmentally manageable industries.

## The Sea Ban Left Behind Is Not an Empty Space

The agricultural ministry's compilation of fishing industry history treats pre-1895 as early fisheries. There were already people engaged in fishing in Taiwan during prehistoric times, and during the Ming-Zheng and Qing dynasties, they were affected by maritime prohibitions, coastal migration, fishing taxes, and pirates. Coastal residents lived off the sea, yet often faced pressures from restrictions on navigation, taxation, and warfare. [^1]

This prehistory cannot be treated as "backwardness" before modernization. Maritime prohibition policies were a state's method of managing the ocean. Coastal residents had their own navigation and fishing experience, yet their activity space was often constrained by military and administrative rules. Officials drew prohibitions on maps, while the lives of coastal settlements continued. Small-scale fishing, salt production, coastal exchange, and seasonal navigation still maintained the relationship between local communities and the sea.

Therefore, the marine surveys that appeared after 1895 were both technological progress and a process of power reorganizing the coast. The Governor-General's Office needed to know where the fish were, who was fishing, and what ships could sail farther, because these knowledges could serve fishermen, as well as the colonial treasury, the military, and the empire's food supply.

## The Linghai Maru Turned the Sea Into a Research Object

The starting point of the Japanese colonial period's marine modernization was investigation and experimentation, which later gradually connected to large ports. The Marine Experiment Station's official chronology records that the Linghai Maru was built in 1909, marine experiment stations were established in Lukang and Hsiaoli in 1913, the Tainan Shakeng brackish-water aquaculture experimental station was set up in 1918, and the Lvmar experimental vessel was built in 1921. In 1923, the Keelung Bajiamen established the jawfish experimental factory. [^2]

The issues handled by these institutions were very concrete: where do fish appear, how do fishing boats catch fish, how is jawfish processed, can seawater fish be raised, and under what conditions do freshwater fish grow. Fishing industry knowledge was found in the sea condition records on experimental vessels, the processing methods in factories, and the failure experiences in aquaculture farms; the classifications in the study were merely the surface that remained in the end.

Chen Dezhi's research on the Taiwan Governor-General's Office's marine fisheries investigation and experiment affairs (1895-1945) points out that from 1895 to 1899 it was mainly marine fisheries forecasting surveys, and from 1899 it entered on-site experiments. After 1909, Taiwan joined the international marine fisheries-influenced contact experiments, and the 1917 South Seas fisheries survey opened the beginning of deep-sea fishing. [^3]

There is a noteworthy contrast in this timeline. The colonial government wanted more efficient fisheries, yet researchers had to first acknowledge that the ocean is not a fixed warehouse. Fish schools move, sea conditions change, and seasons and wind directions change fishing results. The more one tries to make fisheries a planned industry, the more one needs to face the uncertainty of the ocean.

> **Curator's note:** Marine experiments did not make the sea obedient, but they began to use a new language to describe the sea's moods.

## Ports Changed the Scale of Fishing Villages

Keelung's port history shows how engineering changed fishing villages. The National Museum of Marine Science and Technology records that Dapeng Bay was originally a local settlement developed by fishermen, and in 1937 the Japanese colonial government built the Dapeng Bay Peninsula port area through land reclamation. After 1979, the modernized Dapeng Bay fishing port was completed, gradually replacing the position of the Zhenbin fishing port. [^6]

The Zhenbin fishing port was created by the Japanese in 1934 and once became one of the largest fishing ports in Taiwan. When ships increased in tonnage and quantity, the original hinterland and waters were no longer sufficient, and the center of the port moved to Dapeng Bay. This shift was not simply a port upgrade, but also involved the rearrangement of fish sheds, processing, transportation, sailors, and local commerce. [^6]

The story of Dapeng Bay reminds us that a fishing port is not an empty plot of land by the sea. It must handle ships coming and going, fish unloading, fuel, ice, ship repair, and human accommodation. The more modern the port, the longer the industrial chain around it, and the harder it is for residents to live by a single fishing method.

Kaohsiung's Hamaxing presents another form of port and market integration. The Kaohsiung City Government News Bureau's compilation of local history points out that after the first phase of the Takao port construction was completed in 1912, the Dagong fish market was established in the Cuocheng area. In 1920, Takao was renamed Kaohsiung, and the fish market was also renamed the Kaohsiung fish market. In 1927, the Kaohsiung Prefecture Hall built the Kaohsiung fishing port with ice-making, freezing, and fish cargo warehouses; in 1929, the fish market moved to the Xinfujing area, becoming Taiwan's first modern fish market. [^7]

The emergence of fish markets changed the path of fish from ship to dining table. The catch had to be graded and priced in the auction hall, fish needed ice to be transported farther, and distributors and retailers turned the port prices into market prices visible to urban residents. Fishermen did not necessarily master more pricing power, but they were no longer facing a transaction that ended upon coming ashore.

This time difference is worth preserving. Historical articles use contemporary photos, not to pretend today's market is the past, but to let readers see that the spaces left by institutions are still in operation. The history of fish markets often does not lie in a single old wall, but in how goods enter the market, who grades them, who calls out prices, and how they finally leave the market.

## Fish Markets Are Exchanges, Also Local Societies

The post-war Dongshi fish market research provides an observation angle not in the capital or major ports. Lu Qiaorou, taking Dongshi as an example, interviewed fishermen, distributors, retailers, and decision-makers, pointing out that the development of fish markets is affected by central fisheries policies, and also changes the local fisheries and marketing methods in return. [^4]

When facing the decline of the fishing industry, one cannot simply wait for the fish and goods to recover. Local markets developed different sales methods, trying to put trading, local feelings, and social interaction together. Fish markets are therefore not only a place to sell fish, but also a space where acquaintances meet, exchange information, discuss prices, and judge local economic conditions. [^4]

The order in the auction hall has a temporary nature. When fish and goods are just landed, they must be processed quickly; buyers look at size, freshness, quantity, and the day's demand; sellers must complete the transaction within a limited time. This rhythm is hard to appear in policy chronologies, yet it is the moment when the fishing industry system truly reaches the local level.

The life of a market is not entirely determined by output. A decrease in output does indeed make the market lose its foundation, but locally the market function can still be retained through processing, tourism, dining, or community activities. This is not packaging decline as success, but acknowledging that the interpersonal relationships accumulated by the market will not disappear together with the last batch of fish and goods.

Kaohsiung fish market also experienced this shift later. After the fishing industry focus shifted to the Zhenbin fishing port, the Gushan fish market gradually became silent, and the old building was later transformed into an agricultural and fisheries specialty exhibition and sales center. The municipal government's article preserved the local memory of the tuna, mullet, and shark fish that were once gathered there, and also recorded the process of the fish market transforming from a trading space to a cultural and consumption space. [^7]

This reuse has advantages and risks. Old buildings being preserved often allows people to re-examine port history, yet if sea views, brick walls, and cultural and creative products become everything, the labor, prices, and fishing village changes involved in the original fish market will be smoothed over. Preservation work still needs to explain who worked in this building, who made money, and who bore the risks.

## Post-war Reception: What Was Received Was Not Just Ships

After 1945, Taiwan's fishing industry faced the rearrangement of systems and personnel. The Marine Experiment Station's chronology of changes records that in 1945 it was renamed the Taiwan Province Marine Experiment Station, in 1947 it was placed under the Taiwan Province Government, in 1948 it was assigned to the Taiwan Agricultural and Forestry Company's Marine Division, and in 1949 it was placed under the Taiwan Province Government's Agricultural and Forestry Department. [^2]

The rapid changes in names indicate that the post-war government was rearranging agencies and assets. But the fishing industry cannot wait until all systems are sorted out before operating. Experimental stations, fishing ports, ships, cold storage equipment, and local technical personnel must continue to work during the reception process.

Cai Shengzhang's research on fishing industry technical personnel from 1945 to 1947 specifically points out that the repatriation of Japanese fishing personnel caused a development gap, and the Taiwanese technical personnel trained during the Japanese colonial period thus became the main force in the local fishing industry's reorganization. After the war, there was both reception of Chinese technical bureaucrats and equipment and conventions left by the Japanese colonial system. Taiwanese, Japanese, and Chinese technical and management personnel formed a complex cooperation in reception, retention, and assignment. [^5]

This human layer is very important. Facilities can be received, but knowledge needs someone to use it. Who is familiar with the experimental vessels, who knows the port equipment, who can read the past marine records, which directly affects whether the post-war fishing industry can restart. Technical personnel are not background in the chronology; they determine whether a system can land locally.

> **Curator's note:** A change of regime can be completed on paper, yet the rebuilding of the fishing industry still relies on people familiar with sea conditions, equipment, and local relationships to take over the work.

## U.S. Aid Pushed the Fishing Industry Into Farther Seas

From 1951 to 1965, U.S. aid became an important resource for the post-war development of the fishing industry. Wu Boqun's research points out that the government used U.S. aid to repair the fishing industry facilities damaged during the war, increase catch, supplement protein sources, and reduce foreign exchange spending on imported salted fish. The U.S. aid association, the agricultural and cooperative association, the economic reconstruction commission, and the fishing industry increase production committee each were responsible for plan coordination and execution. [^8]

U.S. aid also pushed Taiwan's fishing industry into deep-sea fishing. Deep-sea fishing requires large ships, port equipment, cold storage, and higher capital, which private enterprises find hard to bear alone. The government therefore used the state-owned China Fisheries Company to carry out the 350-ton tuna fishing ship demonstration, hoping to first create a working model and then attract private investment. [^8]

This experience should not be simply written as "aid bringing prosperity." U.S. aid hoped the government would not over-direct, preferring to use funds to assist private enterprises, yet the scale of deep-sea fishing forced the government to enter the market first. Thus, on one hand the policy required marketization, on the other hand it had to rely on state-owned enterprises to bear the demonstration and risks. Research also pointed out that Japanese capital control and U.S. aid repayment became subsequent issues. [^8]

Deep-sea fishing is therefore both industrial expansion and risk transfer. Ships sail farther, capital demands increase, sea labor becomes more dangerous, and fishing industry policies are no longer only decided by local ports. Taiwan's fishing industry began to connect with international waters, foreign exchange, fleet management, and later regional fisheries organizations.

## Increased Output Does Not Mean Everyone Lives Better

The agricultural ministry's century-long fishing industry compilation points out that after the war, coastal fisheries reached 43,900 tons in 1952, and inshore fisheries reached 370,906 tons in 1980. The government started to promote inshore and nearshore fisheries through the four-year economic construction plan from 1953, and later implemented ship-building restrictions, ship reduction, and fishing moratorium policies. [^1]

These numbers show that the industry did expand, yet they cannot alone answer "who benefits." The growth of inshore fisheries may bring opportunities for shipowners, processing industries, port workers, and distributors, yet may also intensify resource competition. The deep-sea transformation increases output value, yet takes laborers to longer and more dangerous journeys. Aquaculture creates new markets, yet also faces issues of epidemics, water and soil resources, and pollution. [^1]

If only successful output is left behind, fishermen's lives will be compressed into statistical columns. Only by putting the Dongshi fish market, Dapeng Bay fish sheds, Gushan auctions, and marine experiment stations back into the article can we see how people work, trade, wait for policies, and seek new survival when the industry declines. The unloading time of fish markets, ice supply, auction order, and transportation routes to the city are often closer to the reality fishermen face every day than a table of annual output. When ports expand, who can obtain ship positions and loans, who must turn to processing or retail, also affects whether a family can continue to stay by the sea.

## The Cost of Marine Modernization Eventually Returns to Shore

The Japanese colonial period's marine experiments left institutions and knowledge for later fishing industries, and U.S. aid accelerated the development of fleets, ports, and deep-sea fishing. Both of these histories cannot be described simply as "progress." The colonial government's surveys served the imperial economy, and the post-war U.S. aid plans brought the fishing industry into larger capital and international markets. Every expansion simultaneously changed the work methods and risk distribution of coastal residents. When research institutions list a certain fish as a focus, when ports invest in large equipment, the local level must follow the new seasons, ship types, and trading rhythms. Fishermen receive new tools, yet often also bear new loans, oil, and market price risks.

The Marine Experiment Station still exists today, and the port spaces of Dapeng Bay, Zhenbin, and Gushan still retain traces of the past. Yet just because research institutions, ports, and fish markets are preserved does not mean the original industrial relationships are naturally preserved. What tourists see is the sea view and old buildings, while what local residents remember may be the fish unloading time, the smell of the fish shed, the sailors returning to port, and the market prices.

The most worth preserving in Taiwan's fishing industry history is not the slogan "surrounded by sea so fishing is important," but how the ocean was rearranged by people of different eras. Someone measured it into marine condition data, someone built it into land-reclaimed ports, someone sent the fish and goods into auction halls, someone used technical education to bridge the post-war rupture, and someone continued to seek a reason for a fishing village to remain after the market shrank.

The sea does not stop changing just because policies are written. What truly needs to be remembered is that every industrial transformation involves ships and fish, and also involves who can stay on shore, who must go to sea, and who has the ability to decide where the next fishing industry should go.

There is also a layer often overlooked: the work of women and families on shore. After sailors go to sea, the sorting of fish and goods, retail, bookkeeping, lending, childcare, and waiting for ship schedules are often shouldered by those staying at the port. Official output statistics rarely record these works, yet market research can see their traces from distributors, retailers, and local relationships. Cold storage equipment is not just technology; it changes how far fish and goods can be sent, how much food a family can save, and whether street vendors can avoid the pressure of unsold goods on the day.

Similarly, the roads, ice-making, electricity, and storage brought by port modernization connect fishing villages to urban supply chains, and also make locals more dependent on fuel prices, external buyers, and government construction schedules. When fish markets decline, what is left is not a single building problem, but the loosening of an entire living rhythm. This is also why fishing industry history is worth re-reading beyond output: the catching on the sea is only the beginning, and the truly long-lasting history happens after the ships come ashore.
