# Sounds Have No Address: How Broadcasting in Taiwan Transformed from a State Megaphone to Local Public Space

In June 1925, the Governor-General of Taiwan conducted a ten-day experimental broadcast to commemorate the thirtieth anniversary of Japanese rule. In October 1928, the Taipei Broadcasting Station was formally established. In February 1931, the semi-official and semi-private Taiwan Broadcasting Association began operations.[^1] [^2]

These three years appear as a straight line of technological progress. In reality, broadcasting carried two natures from its inception: it could deliver commands to every household, or it could play a song, a drama, or a local story, allowing the person in front of the radio to fit their life into the state-scheduled program guide.

Therefore, broadcast history is not just asking "which year had which station." What deserves more inquiry is who was allowed to speak, who was designated as the audience, what language could enter the microphone, and whether listeners could ultimately transform a national megaphone into their own voice.

> **30-Second Overview:** The contradiction in Taiwanese broadcasting was not that it was controlled, but that the controllers had to borrow entertainment, music, dialects, and daily life to make propaganda truly stick.

## Before the Radio Reached Every Home

The ten-day test broadcast in 1925 did not directly deliver radios into every home. New Park in Taipei, Taipei Hospital, Taiping Public School in Dazhong, as well as local post offices, public halls, and municipal offices across various locations, were equipped with sound equipment, and even cars carried loudspeakers through the streets. The content at the time included news summaries, speeches, records, nursery rhymes, Western music, traditional Taiwanese music, and weather forecasts.[^3]

This public listening experience is easily overlooked. When broadcasting first appeared, listeners may not have been sitting in their living rooms but gathered in public buildings, schools, or on street corners. The sound first entered a collective group before gradually becoming background noise in private homes.

In 1927, the Governor-General of Taiwan established radio listening regulations; only 128 households were registered with licenses at that time. After the Taipei Broadcasting Station was established in 1928, the radio began to transition from a spectacle into a daily necessity requiring payment, registration, and maintenance.[^3]

The price and technical threshold of radios also determined the earliest audiences. Research by Lu Shao-li points out that during the Japanese colonial period, the radio market was dominated by capital, technology, and regional conditions; merchants were among the groups most easily able to use them, and Taiwanese people's opportunities to listen were not equal to those of Japanese residents in Taiwan.[^1]

Here is a detail that does not fit the imagination of "modernization": broadcasting could cross distances, but it did not automatically cross class lines. The radio waves could not see barriers, but the radio set, electricity, listening fees, and language all were visible.

## The Taiwan Broadcasting Association: Programs Were Not Born Naturally

After official broadcasts began in 1931, the Taiwan Broadcasting Association was responsible for increasing the number of households that listened, repairing radios, and handling listening obstacles. Broadcast development was not just about installing stations; it also involved program production, personnel, funding, fees, and audience management.[^1]

The organization of Taiwanese broadcasting was linked from the start to the governance goals of the colonial government. Research indicates that broadcast programs were influenced by assimilation policies and political power, limiting the space for Tâi-gí (Taiwanese) content. Broadcasting could introduce hygiene, fire prevention, traffic safety, and radio exercises, but it could also package national identity, wartime mobilization, and colonial order into sounds broadcast daily.[^2]

Yet broadcasting was not only commands. In early 1930s Taipei, radios also appeared in coffee shops, stores, and urban entertainment spaces. Political university research reconstructed this experience using newspaper materials and literary works, pointing out that broadcasting brought precise timekeeping, economic news, popular culture, and consumer life into the urban routine. Its immediacy changed how people waited for news and connected Taipei with mainland Japan and even larger global media markets.[^3]

Some listened to economic news in shops; some used broadcasting as a source of music; some only remembered the stories within the programs. Even if program creators had clear educational objectives, listeners might only take part of it back into their lives. The control of broadcasting was never achieved by pressing a switch; it first needed to become worth listening to.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** A nation can schedule a program guide, but it cannot fully dictate what a family discusses next to the radio.

## From Japanese Stations to Post-War _Voice of Taiwan_

After 1945, Taiwanese broadcasting stations inherited the broadcast equipment and networks left by the Japanese colonial period. In June 1946, the _Voice of Taiwan_ (臺灣之聲) was issued, a monthly magazine that continued until December 1950, comprising 54 issues. It published scripts, radio dramas, sheet music, Mandarin language teaching materials, Min-nan lectures, children's stories, family talks, and literary works.[^4]

_Voice of Taiwan_ clearly illustrates the dual mission of post-war broadcasting. The preface claimed to use "ears and eyes" to conduct cultural reconstruction through sound and text. Research from National Taiwan University points out that this project included removing traces of Japanese colonial rule, instilling Chinese culture, disseminating scientific knowledge, and reshaping the post-war populace.[^5]

Yet the magazine did not rely on a single language. By the end of 1947, broadcasting was divided into First, Second, and Third broadcasts; the Second Broadcast used Min-nan exclusively, specifically for local audiences.[^5] Journal data preserved by the National Taiwan Museum of Literature also shows that early _Voice of Taiwan_ published Min-nan lectures, Japanese content, music, novels, and family lessons, with language and editorial policies gradually being influenced by the political environment.[^4]

This change cannot be simplified as "the immediate prohibition of local voices after the war," nor can it be written as "broadcasting was suddenly liberalized." A more accurate description is that broadcasting institutions were forced to deal with audiences speaking different languages, and subsequently, under the pressures of the 228 Incident, martial law, Mandarin language policy, and Cold War mobilization, the available languages and content were gradually reordered.

_Voice of Taiwan_ also leaves another clue: broadcasting was not just a program received by ears but a printed material that could be read, collected, and studied. When the original sound was difficult to preserve, the program guides, radio scripts, sheet music, and magazine advertisements became important entry points for tracing post-war auditory life.[^4] [^5]

## Radios Entered Daily Life, and Also Wartime Systems

In the 1950s, the government took over the stations and listening habits from the Japanese colonial period, integrating broadcasting into the anti-communist resistance against Russia propaganda system. Research from the National Archives points out that after receiving them in 1945, local Taiwanese stations were renamed radio stations; related organizations reorganized into China Broadcasting Co., Ltd. in 1946; and by 1949, Zhongguang (China Broadcasting) was fully established in Taipei, making anti-communism and resistance against Russia its main operational policy.[^6]

This system was not sustained only by political speeches. Programs in the 1950s gradually incorporated radio dramas, news broadcasts, family content, and other life scenarios. Research by Lin Kuo-hsien on _Radio Magazine_ points out that within an environment where party and government departments screened and monopolized information, they began to learn how to place anti-communist knowledge into a more relatable broadcasting world. Preparedness and daily life thus appeared simultaneously on the same radio.[^9]

The number of radios also increased rapidly. Data compiled by the National Archives shows that approximately 50,000 radios were registered in 1952, exceeding one million by 1962. This growth made broadcasting one of Taiwan's most penetrating information and entertainment media before the popularization of television.[^6]

A family might listen to exercise programs in the morning, market news in the afternoon, songs or radio dramas in the evening, interspersed with state directives. This does not mean that family members believed every word broadcast, nor does it mean they could escape the entire system of control. It only means that authoritarianism was not confined to slogans but was also hidden within a daily schedule, sound habits, and domestic space.

## Private Broadcasting Kept Tâi-gí in the Microphone

The history of broadcasting during martial law cannot be written solely as the history of party-run stations. Zhou Fu-yi's research on private broadcasting from 1952 to 1987 points out that there were still more than twenty private radio stations during the martial law period. These stations lacked the resources of party, military, and government-run stations and had to rely on advertisements and entertainment programs to attract listeners, thus using Tâi-gí broadcasting, producing Tâi-gí songs, radio dramas, and _guangbo gezaixi_ (radio opera) within limited spaces.[^7]

The existence of private stations does not equate to free media. Research indicates that some founders of private stations in the 1950s had backgrounds in party, government, or military circles, and these private stations were also subject to administrative orders, post-facto censorship, and security system control.[^7] Yet there was a gap between control and usage. Programs needed listeners for advertisements, which required a market, and the market forced the stations to respond with languages and content that people could actually understand and listen to for long periods.

Therefore, Tâi-gí songs and radio opera were not just cultural preservation projects; they were also a media economy. They kept the stations alive and allowed languages compressed by Mandarin policy to appear in daily sounds. This history cannot be written as radios bravely confronting the state because most stations operated within the scope of control. A description closer to the historical materials is: private broadcasting found a niche for commercial survival within systemic limitations, and Tâi-gí and local culture happened to find an audience in that niche.

From 1968 to 1973, the Chinese Cultural Revival Movement further contracted the space for Tâi-gí in private broadcasting. From 1974 to 1987, pre-censorship and radio/television regulations tightened content. After lifting martial law, related controls were gradually lifted through legal amendments.[^7]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Sometimes, local languages survive not by a public victory, but by a program needing listeners and advertisers to slowly weather the narrow gate of the system.

## Broadcasting Was Also a Battlefield in East Asia

Taiwanese broadcasting cannot be confined to the history of state directives within the island. Kawashima Sana's research calls the period from 1920 to the 1960s the "broadcasting era," pointing out that the Japanese Empire, the Kuomintang (KMT), the Communist Party, the United States, and the Soviet Union all viewed broadcasting as a tool for mobilization, propaganda, and counter-measures. The radio network crossed colonial, war zone, and linguistic boundaries, forming a radio war in East Asia.[^8]

This cross-border perspective corrects a common misconception: broadcasting was valued not just because governments wanted to "educate the people," but also because it could transmit news instantly, broadcast externally, respond to enemy signals, and bring distant conflicts into homes. Radio waves have no borders, but transmitters, language choices, listening equipment, and content censorship all have political boundaries.

Taiwan during the Japanese colonial period exported programs to mainland Japan. From 1934, Taiwan also accessed the imperial sound network through relay and overseas broadcasting. Post-war, broadcasting was placed within the framework of anti-communist psychological warfare and Cold War propaganda. The languages used by different regimes and their enemies were different, but the imagination of broadcasting as "immediate, universal, and capable of penetrating literacy barriers" was quite similar.[^2] [^8]

## 228 Incident, Underground Radio, and Public Voices

The political role of broadcasting became particularly evident during the 228 Incident in 1947. Lecture materials from the Good Citizen Cultural Action indicate that the public first occupied the Taiwanese Broadcasting Station, causing the incident to spread rapidly across various regions through radio. This material comes from activity notes and speaker compilations and is suitable for illustrating the public role of broadcasting in the event, but should not be taken as a singular explanation for the speed of dissemination in all areas.[^10]

During martial law, radios were also subject to management such as serial numbering, inspection, and repair certificates. Radio programs had to face surveillance, and forbidden songs and unscreened scripts became common violations.[^10] For listeners, this control existed alongside radio dramas, folk stories, and songs. The radio made people feel that someone was speaking for the state, yet they also looked forward to whether the next segment would discuss something familiar from their own lives.

After 1990, underground radio appeared in large numbers. Good Citizen Cultural Action recorded that in 1993, listeners began making calls to _Voice of Taiwan_, leading underground stations to become channels for political figures, social movements, and grassroots workers to speak.[^10]

Underground radio was not a natural result of lifting martial law. Even after the lifting of martial law in 1987, there were hurdles for legally applying for a station; restrictions did not disappear overnight. Underground stations formed because the pace of legal loosening, the speed of social demand for speech, and the capacity of existing media to accommodate local voices were different from each other. It re-established broadcasting as a contested public space.

## What Broadcasting Left Was Not Just a Sound

From the 1925 test broadcasts to the underground radio in the 1990s, Taiwanese broadcasting underwent at least several reorganizations. The colonial government incorporated it into education and mass mobilization. Post-war governments took over the stations, transforming broadcasting into a tool for cultural reconstruction, Mandarin language education, and anti-communist propaganda. Private stations preserved Tâi-gí songs, radio dramas, and local programs amidst commercial pressure and political control. Before and after lifting martial law, underground stations brought excluded political opinions back to the microphone.

If one only looks at the system, broadcast history is a series of regulations and station reorganizations. If one only looks at popular culture, one forgets why songs, stories, and dramas had to find time within those systems. Only when these two threads are placed together can the true weight of broadcasting be seen: it delivered state will into daily life, and it left the irregular tones from daily life for later people to hear.

Today's people habitually skip advertisements with headphones, choose programs, and adjust speed. Past radios did not have so many options. It was more like a window that was permanently open; sounds came in from outside, and family members heard the same melodies, news, and silence in the same room. But the window allowed not only the state to see the family, but the family might also learn by the window which voices were worth believing and which needed to be kept at a distance.

What is most worthy of being preserved from Taiwanese broadcasting history is not the conclusion that "broadcasting was once important," but how it continued to be used under controlled conditions. Some used it to transmit state directives, some sold songs and advertisements through it, some taught languages with it, some waited for _guangbo gezaixi_, and some sent their political judgments into public space via call-in. Sounds have no address, yet they always land somewhere.
