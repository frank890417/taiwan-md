In 1982, Li Yuan-chen, Tsao Ai-lan, Cheng Chih-hui, Liu Yu-hsiu, and Yu Mei-nu founded Awakening Magazine in Taipei while martial law remained in force. On 10 January 1987, Awakening joined with Indigenous groups, human rights organizations, and church groups to launch a parade on Huaxi Street protesting human trafficking and expressing concern for child prostitutes. On 30 November 1996, women's movement worker Peng Wan-ru was murdered in Kaohsiung; one month later, women's groups initiated a night march demanding that women's public safety no longer be treated as a risk each individual must bear alone.[^1]

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's women's movement traced a path from Awakening Magazine's pages, to street actions on Huaxi Street, to the labor rights struggle of female attendants at the National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall, to the personal safety advocacy after the Peng Wan-ru case — step by step reaching the institutional turning points of the Gender Equality Education Act and the Domestic Violence Prevention Act. It gradually converted experiences in family, workplace, and campus that were treated as private matters into public issues that could be complained about, investigated, and legislated upon.

These three dates are not a smooth timeline of progress. They resemble three acts of pushing open a door: first using a publication to give women's experiences names, then bringing the exploitation hidden in red-light district alleys onto the streets, finally turning "I dare not walk alone at night" into a personal safety issue the state must address. The most important change wrought by Taiwan's women's movement lies in redefining which experiences can become public issues, and in making the institutional barriers to women's political participation gradually surface.

> **Curator's Note:** The Constitution long ago wrote down legal equality between men and women, but the real women's movement is what made that sentence begin to enter the home, the campus, and the office.

## Why Constitutional Equality Was Not Enough

After the Nationalist Government retreated to Taiwan in 1949, Article 7 of the Constitution provided formal guarantees of legal equality between men and women. Yet a wide gap existed between legal text and daily life. Matters of marital residence, surname, property, and child custody; mandatory resignation upon marriage or pregnancy in the workplace; domestic violence and caregiving in the family — all were still treated as arrangements within the private sphere, not as rights issues requiring state intervention. In her 2000 women's movement lecture outline, Li Yuan-chen placed this gap at the core of the movement's rise.[^2]

In 1971, Lu Hsiu-lien proposed a "New Feminism," calling on women to step out of the kitchen and participate in society. She originally intended to form a Modern Women's Association, but was restricted by martial law-era regulations on civic organizations, and turned instead to publishing books on women's issues, holding lectures and events through Pioneer Press. After the Kaohsiung Incident (Formosa Incident) in 1979, Lu Hsiu-lien was imprisoned, and the New Feminism movement was interrupted. This rupture illustrates that the women's movement and democratization were never two parallel lines; political suppression directly determined whether women could organize, publish, and speak.[^3]

The 1982 Awakening Magazine picked up this break. The magazine placed abortion, marital property, sexual harassment, housewives' social participation, and female workers' conditions on the same agenda.

This combination of issues sparked debate at the time. Some considered gender equality mere complaints of middle-class women; others understood women's rights claims as wives contending for power against husbands. The publication's work was to let originally isolated experiences begin to recognize each other, then organize these experiences into discussable public issues.[^4]

## How a Magazine Reached Huaxi Street

On 10 January 1987, Awakening, the Rainbow Project, the Taiwan Association for Human Rights, Indigenous groups, and church groups launched the "Protest Human Trafficking — Care for Child Prostitutes" parade on Huaxi Street. This was the first time Taiwan's women's movement brought women's issues to the streets on a large scale. The following year on 9 January, the Garden of Hope Foundation, Awakening, and the Rainbow Project again marched through Huaxi Street in winter rain, the procession turning from the Guilin Police Precinct into alleyways to transmit rescue hotline numbers to girls trapped in the sex industry.[^5]

The historical significance of this action cannot be summarized merely as "successfully arousing social concern." The parade simultaneously connected the women's movement with Indigenous peoples, human rights, child trafficking, and church rescue work. It also exposed the limits of contemporary policy: although the government uncovered large numbers of underage prostitutes through the "Clean-Up Project," problems of police protection, lenient penalties, and insufficient placement did not disappear as a result. Women's groups therefore shifted from protest to sheltering, volunteer training, legal advocacy, and long-term accompaniment.

In November 1987, Awakening Magazine reorganized into the Awakening Foundation. The name change did not mean the movement left the streets; rather, it began using three tools simultaneously: magazine and research to build discourse, parades and petitions to create public pressure, and legislative drafts and lobbying to deliver that pressure into the system. This combination of "publication, streets, bills" became a working method Taiwan's women's movement would repeatedly employ.

> **Curator's Note:** After entering the system, the movement still brought the language of the streets into bills, then back into life to check whether the bills actually worked.

## From a Contract, Questioning Who Can Stay in the Workplace

In 1987, female attendants at the National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall faced mandatory resignation upon reaching age 30, marrying, or becoming pregnant under contract terms. In the process of voicing support, Awakening discovered that the Labor Standards Act, Factory Act, and Civil Code of the time could hardly directly address such gendered contracts. The incident exposed a legal void: women could work, but not necessarily continue working after marriage and childbirth.[^6]

After this incident, Awakening proposed a Gender Equality in Employment Act draft in 1990. The business community pushed back, and the bill stalled for years. In 1999, women's groups used petitions and a pregnancy discrimination and sexual harassment complaint hotline to expand public pressure; the bill finally passed its third reading in 2001. The law prohibits employers from differential treatment of job seekers and employees based on gender, requires employers to establish sexual harassment prevention measures, and provide parental leave without pay, family care leave, and childcare measures.[^7]

When the National Dr. Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall revisited this history in 2025 through a forum and documentary, the protagonist of that time, Yang Li-chun, remained at the story's core. She had been forced to resign due to marriage and fought for unpaid severance. Attorney Pan Cheng-fen spoke of her participation in drafting the Gender Equality in Employment Act. These names remind us that the employment equality act came from the accumulation of female attendants, female section chiefs, lawyers, and women's groups. They organized injuries dismissed as "company regulations" one by one into legal issues.

## Marriage Is Not a Legal Safe Haven

In the 1990s, women's groups turned lived experience into demands for revision of the Civil Code's Family Part. Although the 1929 Family Part used modern legal language of gender equality, it retained the power configuration of the patriarchal family. After the first revision in 1996, the women's movement of the late 1980s through the 1990s began more explicitly demanding that surnames, marital property, domicile, and child custody no longer default to the father.

In 1994, Judicial Yuan Constitutional Interpretation No. 365 declared the "paternal authority supremacy" provision in Article 1089 of the Civil Code unconstitutional. Awakening, the Garden of Hope Foundation, and legal workers used drafts, public hearings, petitions, and Legislative Yuan lobbying to turn "who decides the child" from a domestic dispute into a constitutional equality issue. After the 1996 revision, child custody and parental rights determinations in divorce no longer defaulted to the father. The 2007 revision then allowed children to choose their mother's surname.[^9]

The counterintuitive aspect of this legislative path is that the women's movement demanded legal recognition that power relations exist within the family. When a family's domicile, surname, property, and custody are all prioritized by the male party, the family is not a naturally equal private space. Women's groups dismantled the "home" to let law address the inequality within.

## Teng Ju-wen and "Law Enters the Home"

The 1993 Teng Ju-wen husband-killing case brought long-term marital violence to the surface. She had long suffered her husband's violence and sexual violence, finally killing him. The case triggered discussion among women's groups, lawyers, and the public about marital violence, and challenged the saying "even an upright official finds it hard to judge family affairs." The historical evolution of the New Taipei City Domestic Violence and Sexual Assault Prevention Center links this case to the subsequent formation of the Domestic Violence Prevention Act.[^10]

After Peng Wan-ru's murder in 1996, women's personal safety became another issue the state had to answer. On 21 December 1996, women's groups launched the "Women's Fire, Light the Night Road" night march, demanding the government value women's nighttime movement and public safety. In 1997 the Sexual Assault Crime Prevention Act passed, the Ministry of Education established the Gender Equality Education Committee, and the Executive Yuan established the Committee for the Promotion of Women's Rights. In 1998 the Domestic Violence Prevention Act passed, formally bringing domestic violence into the statutory protection, reporting, and administrative service system.[^11]

"Law Enters the Home" marked a turn in institutional concepts. Before this, violence occurring at home was often considered inappropriate for outsider intervention. After the legal framework was established, protection orders, reporting, police, medical, social work, and judiciary began forming an intervention network. The system certainly would not eliminate violence immediately upon the law's passage, but survivors at least gained a new language: that is an injury that can be complained about, protected, and pursued — not to be attributed to her marital failure.

> **Curator's Note:** The deepest transformation of the women's movement is rewriting "family matters" into "matters the state cannot pretend not to see."

## After Yeh Yung-chih, Gender Enters Textbooks

On 20 April 2000, Kaoshu Junior High School student Yeh Yung-chih died in Pingtung. The incident sparked discussion in Taiwan society on gender characteristics, campus safety, and the education system. The Ministry of Education later designated 20 April each year as Gender Equality Education Day, and reviewed how the Yeh Yung-chih incident pushed schools to rethink respect for students of different gender characteristics.[^12]

The legal institutionalization of gender equality education was not driven by a single event alone. In 1988 Awakening had already begun examining gender bias in elementary and junior high textbooks. The Taiwan Women's Studies Association was founded in 1993, continuously critiquing gender division of labor in education and academia. In 1997 the Ministry of Education established the Gender Equality Education Committee. In 2000, the Ministry commissioned Shen Mei-chen, Su Chien-ling, Hsieh Hsiao-chin, and Chen Hui-hsin to draft the Gender Equality Education Act; the Yeh Yung-chih incident then prompted the draft to more clearly incorporate issues of gender characteristics and sexual orientation.[^13]

From 2002, the draft revision group underwent public hearings and multiple meetings; on 4 June 2004 the Legislative Yuan passed the Gender Equality Education Act on third reading, promulgated on 23 June. Chen Hui-hsin had chaired relevant research groups and long engaged in gender and law research. This history turned "whether education should teach gender equality" into an institutional obligation: schools must provide a safe learning environment, curricula and teaching materials must not replicate gender discrimination, and victims must have channels to request investigation and relief.[^14]

The law's execution relies not only on central orders, but also on whether local education agencies, school gender equality committees, and teachers are willing to turn complaint procedures into support students can actually use. For victimized students, the system's existence is only the first step; what follows involves post-reporting confidentiality, investigator professionalism, coordination with parents and schools, and whether the campus can avoid pushing the complainant back into isolation. Gender equality education thus extended from curriculum content to school governance, and the women's movement progressed from demanding legal recognition of problems to further demanding that agencies establish trustworthy handling procedures. These details determine whether the system can function in daily life.

## From Protecting Women to Gender Proportions

The institutional framework for women's political participation also underwent conceptual change. Early reserved seats for women allowed women to enter the electoral system, but by the 1990s women's groups began questioning whether fixed quotas might become a ceiling on women's political participation. Advocacy thus gradually shifted from "one-quarter reserved seats for women" toward a "one-third gender proportion principle," arguing this system simultaneously addresses women's underrepresentation and uneven distribution of political power.[^15]

In 2000, a pledge for one-quarter women in the cabinet was proposed and fulfilled. In the 2004 constitutional amendment, women could not constitute less than one-half of party lists for at-large legislators. This system simultaneously changed how party organizations and state organs understand representation, and made women no longer merely passive additions: they are the population a democratic system must normally reflect.

This system has always been accompanied by controversy. Reserved seats may be treated as a ceiling, or may become a necessary stepping stone in a male-dominated nomination system. Taiwan's women's movement strategy involves re-examining institutional effects after using reserved seats, then proposing next steps of gender proportion, co-governance, and gender mainstreaming.[^16]

## Organizational Differences Are Also the Movement's Strength

Taiwan's women's movement has never been the story of a single organization. Awakening tends to use policy advocacy, legislative drafts, and cross-party lobbying as primary methods. The Homemakers United Foundation starts from housewives and community life, linking environment, consumption, food safety, and care. Analysis by the Taiwan Research Hub at the University of Nottingham notes that the two organizations respectively adopt top-down policy advocacy and bottom-up community mobilization, yet both influence institutions and daily life through education, alliance-building, and long-term organizational work.[^17]

This also explains why the "women's movement" cannot be written merely as a string of bill names. Laws need research, lobbying, and voting, but for bills to enter life they also need community mothers, campus clubs, survivor support systems, women's bookstores, volunteers, and local workers. Different groups' lines are not necessarily consistent, and may even conflict on licensed prostitution, sex work, lesbians, and class issues. These controversies make the women's movement see that "women" internally also have class, ethnic, sexual orientation, and identity differences.

## From Street Advocacy to State Agencies

In 1997, the Ministry of Education established the Gender Equality Education Committee, and the Executive Yuan established the Committee for the Promotion of Women's Rights. The establishment of these two agencies let women's movement figures begin handling education, labor, personal safety, and political participation issues inside the state. Women's groups did not thereby lose their street position, but learned to move between committees, revision meetings, public hearings, and communities. Some draft texts, some explain bills to affected people, some bring cases of institutional failure back into public discussion.[^15]

After the 2000 party rotation, large numbers of women's movement scholars and group members were invited into policy mechanisms like the Committee for the Promotion of Women's Rights. This cooperation gave gender mainstreaming administrative resources, but also raised new questions: when the movement enters the state, who still represents women who have not entered the meeting room? Awakening and the Homemakers United Foundation's organizational experience shows that policy advocacy and community mobilization each have strengths and blind spots. Relying only on law easily overlooks implementation sites; relying only on local action may not change national rules.[^17]

Institutionalization also added a new task: translating legal language into procedures usable by local agencies, schools, enterprises, and families. The Committee for the Promotion of Women's Rights can propose policy recommendations, but that does not mean every county and city can provide the same services. After the Gender Equality Education Act passed, the investigation quality of school committees, teacher training, complainant confidentiality, and cross-agency referral all became sites the women's movement must continue tracking. This makes the movement's effectiveness impossible to measure by bill count alone; one must also look at whether a person encountering discrimination or violence can truly find an entry point.[^13]

Class issues within the women's movement also became clearer at this stage. Early organizations were often driven by highly educated women capable of writing and participating in meetings; the experiences of factory women workers, rural women, Indigenous women, new immigrant women, and women with disabilities did not necessarily enter the agenda on equal terms. This does not mean early advocacy lacked value, but reminds later movements to continuously ask: a seemingly universal women's policy — who defines the problem, and who bears the cost of reform?[^4]

From Lu Hsiu-lien's 1971 New Feminism proposal, to Awakening's 1982 founding, to the 1997 Sexual Assault Crime Prevention Act, 1998 Domestic Violence Prevention Act, 2001 Gender Equality in Employment Act, and 2004 Gender Equality Education Act, Taiwan's women's movement indeed changed laws and public institutions. But every system leaves the next question: can protection orders truly reach survivors, can the employment equality act resist asymmetric care responsibilities, can gender equality education be understood on campus rather than just fulfill hours, can gender proportions reach decision-making cores.

Taiwan's women's movement history therefore cannot end with "from backward to progressive." It is more like a set of experiences constantly renamed: domestic violence from household matter to crime, mandatory resignation upon marriage from company regulation to discrimination, gender roles in textbooks from common sense to examinable content, women's political participation from reserved seats to co-governance system. Each renaming represents someone first enduring the problem in life, then finding a way to make society unable not to hear. This is also why the women's movement continues to exist, and must continue to be recorded and discussed.

> **Curator's Note:** What the women's movement leaves behind is a method for translating silenced experiences into public language, not a completed checklist.
