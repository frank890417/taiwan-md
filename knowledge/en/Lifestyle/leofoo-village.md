> **30-Second Summary:** In 1979, Lifecamp began as a semi-wild animal park in Hsinchu Kanshi. In the 1990s, four exotic themed villages, rollercoasters, and water parks were added, allowing animal viewing, pursuit of weightlessness, and family vacations to share the same schedule. Its most counterintuitive aspect is that the more successfully a park turns life into a journey, the less it can be managed by amusement ride standards alone.

# Lifecamp: An Amusement Park That Grew Rollercoasters After Animals

In 1979, Zhuang Fu purchased 73 hectares of land in Hsinchu Kanshi to open a semi-wild animal park.[^1] In 1990, Lifecamp Development began planning theme parks, and four exotic themed areas were gradually opened starting in June 1994.[^2]

Therefore, the most memorable starting point for Lifecamp is not any particular roller coaster, but the sequence of "animals first, park later." It combined viewing wild animals, walking through exotic settings, riding thrilling attractions, and staying at ecological resorts into a type of day or two-day trip familiar to Taiwanese families.

This combination seems natural, yet it is rare in reality. Amusement rides are measured by speed and screams; zoos must confront care, breeding, space, escape prevention, and educational responsibilities. The story of Lifecamp is precisely the story of these two timetables being forced into one park complex.

## Zhuang Fu’s 73-Hectare Beginning

Both the official materials from Lifecamp and the Ministry of Tourism refer to the Hsinchu Kanshi semi-wild animal park in 1979 as its predecessor. The official introduction stated that it was the only and largest semi-wild animal park nationwide at the time, covering an area of 73 hectares.[^1] [^2]

This number is not just about the park's scale; it is a microcosm of leisure imagination in Taiwan during that era. Family travel at the time often treated "seeing exotic animals" as the destination itself. The land was large enough for animals to be housed in a viewing framework more open than traditional cages, and visitors could gain a sense of foreignness from a suburban trip.

Zhuang Fu, the founder of Lifecamp, originally ran businesses in enterprise and hospitality. The corporate history of Lifecamp Development marks 1989 as an important turning point in creating a large theme park combined with a zoo, indicating that the transformation was not merely adding some rides, but the company reorienting its direction in the leisure industry.[^3]

There is one easily overlooked difference here: Lifecamp did not treat animals as decorations for an amusement park; it treated the zoo itself as the structural core. The themed villages, performances, hotels, and water parks added later all served to extend visitor stay time, making a visit not just a queue for rides, but a designed journey.

## The 1990s: Exoticism Mapped onto a Park Layout

After planning began in 1990, Lifecamp Development commissioned Battaglia Associate Inc. from the United States to create detailed designs, using the American Old West, South Pacific, Arabian Palace, and African Wildlife Kingdom as thematic skeletons.[^2]

These names seem somewhat direct today, but they effectively illustrate the approach of Taiwanese theme parks in the 1990s. Exoticism was not a background poster; it was an entire consumption language encompassing architecture, music, dining, rides, and animal exhibits. Visitors could move from one area to another without leaving the country, switching between several "worlds" in a single afternoon.

The park materials from the Ministry of Tourism position Lifecamp as a comprehensive park combining four themed villages, an open zoo, performances, and amusement rides. The materials also list attractions such as Laughing Eagle, Grand Canyon Rapids, Sea Pirates, Great Wrath God, and Fire Wheel.[^2]

The focus of this design was not how long the list of attractions was, but how visitors' bodies were arranged at different speeds. The steam train and beast areas made people slow down; themed streets made them stop to take photos; while rollercoasters suddenly surrendered their bodies to gravity. Lifecamp placed "viewing" and "weightlessness" on the same itinerary.

## From Zoo to Day Trip

Official materials state that the park has about 70 species and nearly 1,000 animals, featuring an open zoo. These numbers are self-reports from the park and tourism promotion materials, suitable for understanding how the park positioned itself, but not necessarily as independent animal survey statistics.[^1] [^2]

The early concept of "open" had a more concrete meaning: in the 1970s, Lifecamp allowed visitors to drive into the wildlife area themselves. Windows had to be closed, and passengers could not get out; giraffes, baboons, and elephants would appear right next to the car. A 2024 report noted that this self-driving tour was later canceled due to safety issues, only briefly returning as a limited "Zoo Self-Drive" event during its 45th anniversary.[^12] Therefore, driving to Lifecamp today and driving into an animal exhibit area in the past are two different things. The self-driving mentioned in the park's current traffic guide refers to arriving at the park and using the parking lot, not general visitors freely driving through the wildlife area.[^13]

The space within the park therefore had to serve two types of viewing simultaneously. Visitors wanted closeness; animals needed distance. Visitors hoped to see more in limited time, but animal lives would not conform to tourist schedules. Amusement rides could be activated according to a timetable, but animal exhibits could not demand that every animal appear on schedule in the same way.

In 2010, Kanshi Lifecamp Zhuang's leisure hotel opened, which the group officially described as a hotel themed around animals and ecology.[^1] In 2012, Lifecamp invested another 390 million NTD to build a Greek-style water park, stringing together water play, accommodation, animals, and thematic attractions into a longer stay path.[^2]

This expansion was not simply extending the existing park; it transformed Lifecamp from "deciding what to do after entering" into a pre-arranged vacation product. During the day, one could sequentially pass through themed villages, animal exhibits, and rides; in the evening, consumption extended to accommodation and dining; and in the summer, the water park offered another seasonal entry point. This is why Lifecamp's history cannot be described solely by the number of rides; what truly changed was the amount of time visitors stayed, and how the company connected different experiences into one consumption path.[^1] [^2] [^3]

This path also changed the meaning of "visiting Lifecamp." It was no longer just children playing on rides while adults waited nearby; it incorporated transportation, dining, accommodation, photography, animal viewing, and amusement rides into a single family decision. The more complete the park, the easier it was for visitors to stay, and the more types of risks the company had to manage.

## When Entertainment Becomes an Administrative Issue

In 2021, Lifecamp and Kanshi Lifecamp Zhuang publicly announced obtaining an animal performance permit from Hsinchu County, stating that the review affirmed the area, facilities, activity space, medical equipment, and care staff.[^4] This was a formal statement by the operator about its management capabilities, indicating that obtaining permission was viewed as operational responsibility, not just administrative paperwork.

Film studies also brought attention back to the rides themselves. In February 1999, the "Magic Carpet" in the Arabian Palace area struck an intern who was not standing in the safety zone during descent, and the intern died after being hospitalized. Subsequent reports from NOWnews indicated that the park reviewed the ride's safety after the accident and decided to install safety railings around the Magic Carpet.[^11] This incident is not the same as the later circulating urban legend of Great Wrath God with long hair, but they collectively show how the memory of theme park safety is reassembled among visitors.

However, permission did not end the controversy. In 2022, animal welfare groups pointed out at a press conference commemorating the death of Lifecamp's giraffes that eight giraffes had died within ten years, questioning the captive environment, animal performances, paid feeding, and breeding management. These contents were investigations and accusations from animal welfare groups; the article does not rewrite them as facts recognized by the courts.[^5] [^6]

The same event thus presented two narrative tones. The park's announcement affirmed the review of the area and care, while the animal welfare groups demanded that the government examine death records, exhibition methods, and animal welfare. A reliable approach is not to pre-judge one side, but to place both narratives back into the same systemic issue: who inspects, what is inspected, whether the results are public, and who continuously tracks it.

The giraffe import controversy in 2022 was ultimately not just about "whether to import three more giraffes." An English report from CNA pointed out that animal welfare groups demanded the government prevent Lifecamp from importing three giraffes from Mexico and cited the death of eight giraffes over ten years.[^7] This dispute bound import documents, veterinary care, site design, and animal welfare for scrutiny.

## From Urban Legend to Real Accidents

The video from Zhiqi Qiqi separated the urban legend of "Great Wrath God's long hair being pulled off" from real amusement park accidents. The video itself is a line of inquiry, not a substitute for judicial rulings, regulatory documents, or news reports. What can be directly cited in the article are the news reports of the 1999 Magic Carpet incident and the subsequent installation of safety railings by the park.[^11]

This distinction is important. Urban legends use fear to cast shadows on attractions; real accidents require answers regarding responsibility, operating standards, warning lines, and corrective measures. Mixing the two obscures the events of actual victims with legend, and makes unsubstantiated plots look like historical records.

## Baboon Escape Brings Unseen Boundaries into View

In March 2023, East African baboons escaped from Lifecamp and were later shot dead in Taoyuan. CNA reported that after the incident, animal welfare groups demanded an investigation not only into the baboon area but also into the overall animal welfare and escape management of the entire park.[^8]

The investigation results released by the Control Yuan in October 2023 elevated the event from a wildlife news story to a governance issue. The Control Yuan pointed out that the Taoyuan municipal government failed to appoint an operational commander during the capture, and the Ministry of Agriculture did not provide sufficient contextual explanation, ultimately turning the intended "capture and placement" into "hunting."[^9]

The Control Yuan also noted that the cause and exact number of baboons kept at Lifecamp were still unknown. Although the Hsinchu County government demanded improvements to escape prevention measures, it should continue to track the captive environment of the animals in the park. The report also stated that current regulations lack clear standards for individual identification and "appropriate facilities" in open exhibits with group-kept animals.[^9]

These sentences are heavy, but they cannot just be used to label Lifecamp as "dangerous." They truly reveal the structural difficulties of a mixed-use park: animal exhibition is part of the park's attraction, yet an animal escape immediately becomes a cross-county public safety and wildlife management incident. A gate, a fence section, or a roster within the park can become a problem that requires government intervention after leaving the premises.

The Life Concern Association later compiled the 2022 giraffe controversy and the 2023 baboon incidents, focusing on the gray area between central and local responsibilities, performance permits, animal welfare, and educational use. This is an advocacy group's policy analysis, not an official judgment, but it provides important material for understanding systemic friction.[^10]

## Why Lifecamp Is Still Worth Writing About

If Lifecamp were only written as "a place with rollercoasters," its most Taiwanese aspect would be missed. It connects the imagination of semi-wild animal parks from the late 1970s to the exotic theme parks of the 1990s, and finally to today's questioning of animal welfare, public safety, and corporate responsibility.

If it were only written as an animal welfare controversy, half would be missed. The memories of many Taiwanese families truly left a specific shape here: the mountain roads of Kanshi, the signs from African tribes, the movement of the steam train, the animals seen in the distance while queuing, and the increasingly quiet wait before riding Great Wrath God for the first time.

Lifecamp's difficulty is not which—"animals" or "amusement"—is more important; it chose to let both coexist, so it must manage the gaps between them with higher standards. Visitors buy entertainment, but the park bears more than just entertainment. When animals are placed into a tourism product, care and transparency cannot remain only in the company's self-introduction.

Next time thinking of Lifecamp, perhaps one could first recall that counterintuitive sequence: in 1979, the semi-wild animal park appeared first; the themed villages and large rides did not appear until 1994.[^1] This sequence makes Lifecamp a concrete specimen of Taiwan's leisure industry transformation, and it cannot be glossed over with just the words "old park."

The real question it leaves is: when an amusement park allows people to pursue speed, exotic imagination, and close-range viewing of life simultaneously, can every kind of life not just be a scenic spot in the journey?
