As of October 31, 2026, there are 11,550 people in Taiwan waiting for organ transplants, while the number of donation cases from January to October of the same year is 403. [^1]

These two figures belong to different systems: new people join the waiting list every day, and others leave because they cannot wait any longer. According to the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW), an average of 3.3 people die every day because they cannot receive an organ transplant in time. [^3]

## A Path Started in 1987

Taiwan announced the _Human Organ Transplantation Act_ in 1987, becoming the first country in Asia to legalize organ donation and transplantation; the core of the current act is autonomy, altruism, voluntariness, and non-remuneration. [^2][^4]

Following legal amendments in 2015, the sale of organs was explicitly prohibited in Taiwan. The Department of Medical Affairs at the MOHW explained that Taiwan tracks transplantation history through data cross-referencing between the Department of Medical Affairs and the National Health Insurance Administration: if someone undergoes a transplant abroad and returns to Taiwan using immunosuppressant drugs but has no domestic transplant record, the system will detect it. [^3]

There is also a classification in the system that is easily overlooked. What Taiwan calls _dà'ài_ (great love) donation refers to deceased organ donation, which includes brain death donation and Donation after Circulatory Death (DCD); "relative donation" refers entirely to living donation, and these two represent two different lines in statistics. [^2]

## The Donation Curve: 416, 366, 318

The number of _dà'ài_ donations reached a ten-year high of 416 in 2023. Since then, it has declined for two consecutive years to 366 and 318. [^3]

The trend in 2026 is even steeper. According to data obtained by _The Reporter_, there were only 130 _dà'ài_ donations in the first half of the year, suggesting the full year may hit a nine-year low. The number of new individuals with organ donation notations on their National Health Insurance cards also dropped from 46,000 in 2024 to 42,000 in 2025. [^2]

Looking at this curve alongside the waiting list: MOHW statistics show approximately 12,000 to 13,000 people waiting each year, while between 2016 and 2025, the total annual donations (combining _dà'ài_ and living donors) ranged from roughly 800 to 1,000. [^3]

## 4.87 vs. 11.71

If calculated as a donation rate per million population, Taiwan's rate in 2025 was 4.87, placing it in the lower tier of international rankings. In the same year, South Korea stood at 7.15, Germany at 11.71, Canada at 22.14, and the United States at 47.65; Japan, which shares similar cultural sentiments in Asia, was at 1.27. [^2]

This ranking is often used to argue that "switching to an Opt-Out system would make things better," but the data does not support such a single-cause inference. Singapore, which also uses an Opt-Out system, had only 4.75 in 2025, lower than Taiwan; meanwhile, the United States, which uses an Opt-In (informed consent) system, has long been one of the highest organ donation rate countries globally. [^2]

Li Ming-che (李明哲), Chairman of the Registry, analyzes that the biggest difference between the two systems lies in "who bears the responsibility for advocating organ donation." In an Opt-In system, the medical system actively solicits donations from the public; in an Opt-Out system, the state presumes consent through law. The key is whether the promotion mechanism is complete, not the name of the system. [^2]

## Half of Families Say No

Even when an individual has recorded their organ donation intent on their National Health Insurance card, approximately half of the families still object at the time of death. This is one of the most direct reasons why Taiwan's donation rate is lower than expected by the system. [^2]

The reasons are easy to understand. Taiwan uses an Opt-In system, which requires the individual or their family to express consent at the time of death before organs can be harvested; while medical ethics prioritize the patient's wishes, clinical settings still respect the family. Therefore, if a family disagrees, hospitals will not force the harvesting. [^2]

Family concerns are specific and repetitive: fear that the loved one will be "incomplete," fear that they won't be able to "see" after donating corneas, fear that hospitals will sell the organs, or simply being unable to let go. Li Ming-che noted that in states of extreme grief, relatives find it very difficult to make rational choices; Chiu Chin-fang (邱琴芳), an organ procurement coordinator at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, mentioned the pressure of time—sometimes a patient's condition declines so rapidly that by the time the family wants to donate, nothing can be donated anymore. [^2]

Whether the medical team speaks up has a very direct impact. Li Ming-che cited Registry statistics: if doctors do not solicit donation, about 5% of the public will volunteer; if the medical team actively explains the significance of organ donation, the proportion can rise to 17% or 20%. [^2]

## Kidneys and Livers: Living Donors Support the Other Half

Due to the shortage of _dà'ài_ sources, kidney and liver transplants are increasingly reliant on living donations from relatives. The 2024 figures illustrate this most clearly: there were 240 _dà'ài_ kidney cases and 240 relative kidney cases; there were only 145 _dà'ài_ liver cases, while relative liver cases reached 313. [^2]

The legal frameworks for the two differ. Current regulations limit living kidney donation to blood relatives within the fifth degree of kinship or spouses; living liver donation is expanded to include both blood and in-laws within the fifth degree. The reason for this difference is that there are no machines that can completely replace liver function in cases of liver failure, whereas renal failure can be managed through dialysis to sustain life. [^2]

In May 2020, the Registry convened experts to evaluate expanding living kidney donation, and opinions leaned toward aligning the standards for kidneys and livers. Besides increasing supply, supporters argued this would help with hereditary kidney diseases: blood relatives of families with polycystic kidney disease or Fabry disease may carry the same condition, whereas in-laws might have a better chance of being suitable donors. [^2]

## DCD: The Narrow Gate of a Dozen Cases per Year

Brain death donation is the mainstay of organ donation in Taiwan, but not all terminal patients reach a state of complete brainstem function loss. Most cases involving heart failure or respiratory failure caused by severe lung disease result in death via cardiac arrest, which can only proceed through Donation after Circulatory Death (DCD). [^2]

DCD was implemented in Taiwan in 2017, but by 2024, the national cumulative total was only 82 cases, averaging about a dozen per year. The reason is time: after withdrawing life support, the warm ischemic tolerance for the liver is approximately 30 minutes, for the lungs 60 minutes, and for the kidneys 120 minutes; exceeding these limits means only tissues can be donated. [^2]

In 2025, when a hospital in Yilan performed a DCD, a gap between judicial autopsy procedures and life-support withdrawal protocols triggered public scrutiny. Misinformation subsequently spread on social media, conflating the case with "forced organ harvesting" and even producing content teaching people how to withdraw their organ donation consent. [^2] The consequences of this incident are directly reflected in donation numbers; it is not merely a matter of reputation.

Systemic adjustments are underway. The short-term direction is to simplify practical operations, allowing prosecutors to intervene earlier and issue consent after confirming there is no suspicion of crime; the long-term direction is to rename "heart death" to "circulatory death" and review elevating DCD from operational guidelines to a legal status. [^2]

## Four Lines of Defense and Diluted Funding

Taiwan's procurement system refers to the US model, establishing four Organ Procurement Organization (OPO) regional networks: National Taiwan University Hospital for the North, Taichung Veterans General Hospital for the Center, National Cheng Kung University Hospital for the South, and Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital for the East. [^2]

The US has over 50 OPOs, most of which are non-profit organizations independent of hospitals, authorized by the government to solicit donations from hospitals; Taiwan's OPOs are primarily transplant hospitals, while other hospitals act as cooperating hospitals with relatively less momentum in procurement following policy. Li Ming-che believes this is an area where Taiwan can make adjustments. [^2]

Funding is another reality. After the "Three Laws on Dignified Death" were integrated into the Registry in 2023, about half of the funding shifted toward palliative care and patient autonomy, relatively diluting organ donation operations. Auditor Tian Chiu-chin (田秋堇) also pointed out that since the year the MOHW amended the act in 2015, organ donation promotion expenses have declined annually. [^2]

A veteran coordinator provided a more specific account: in the past, organ donation consent forms were issued by the Registry to hospitals, but each hospital could only apply for 500 forms per year despite an actual need for 1,500 to 2,000; the shortage had to be covered by printing their own, and promotion posters and giveaways were also absorbed by the hospitals themselves. [^2]

## Returning to Those Two Numbers

Putting 11,550 and 403 back into the same table makes the gap clear: those waiting are increasing, while those donating are decreasing. There are specific points of intervention in the four links: whether doctors are willing to speak up, whether families can reach a consensus before death, whether the degree of kinship for living kidney donation can be expanded, and whether judicial and medical procedures for DCD can be aligned. [^1][^2]

None of these are new slogans. Taiwan chose this path in 1987 and added the prohibition on trading in 2015; what is missing now is the actual execution of these well-designed processes for every possible donor.
