In 2024, the inpatient surgical site infection rate was 1.21%.[^1]

The numerator is 12,826 patients with inpatient surgical site infections; the denominator is 1,061,460 inpatient surgery patients.[^1]

Infection determination is based on reported data: if an inpatient surgery patient's secondary diagnosis code is 996.6, 998.1, 998.3, or 998.5 (ICD-9-CM), or the corresponding full ICD-10-CM code, they are counted in the numerator.[^1]

## One Point Two One

The five-year breakdown is as follows: 2020: 1.24%, 2021: 1.26%, 2022: 1.24%, 2023: 1.19%, and 2024: 1.21%.[^1]

```tw-line
Inpatient Surgical Site Infection Rate (%)
2020 | 1.24 | During pandemic
2021 | 1.26 | Five-year high
2022 | 1.24 | —
2023 | 1.19 | Five-year low
2024 | 1.21 | Slight rebound
Source: NHIA "2024 National Health Insurance Monitoring Indicator Monitoring Results Report" Indicator 2.1
```

Overall, the rate remained between 1.19% and 1.26% over the five years, with a fluctuation of less than 0.1 percentage points. The NHIA's commentary is that there has been "no significant change."[^1]

## The Three Pandemic Years

Why did the rates trend upward from 2020 to 2022? According to the NHIA assessment, it may be counterintuitive: it was not that infections increased, but rather that the denominator decreased.[^1]

During the pandemic, non-emergency inpatient surgeries were reduced or postponed, causing the number of inpatient surgery patients to decline. However, the number of infections remained roughly around 12,000, which caused the rate to rise.[^1]

The denominator dropped from 1,020,871 in 2020 to 983,219 in 2021, before rebounding to 1,069,313 in 2023. The numerator remained relatively stable at 12,647, 12,376, 12,474, 12,675, and 12,826.[^1]

In other words, the first half of this curve likely reflects the postponement of surgeries during the pandemic rather than a sudden decline in medical quality.

In 2023, the denominator rebounded to 1,069,313, causing the rate to drop to its five-year low of 1.19%; in 2024, with a slightly smaller denominator and an increased numerator, the rate returned to 1.21%.[^1]

## Large Disparities Between Institutions

While the overall figures are stable, there are significant differences between individual hospitals.

Taking the average of each hospital's infection rate, the values were: 0.77% in 2020, 1.05% in 2021, 1.07% in 2022, 0.77% in 2023, and 0.59% in 2024.[^1]

The standard deviation between institutions was 1.77, 4.10, 4.39, 2.94, and 1.18 percentage points, respectively; the maximum value in 2024 was 14.9%, which is lower than the 50% to 60% seen from 2021 to 2023.[^1]

```tw-stack
2024 Distribution of Surgical Site Infections by Institution
Item | Value | Description
Overall Infection Rate | 1.21% | 12,826 / 1,061,460 people
Institutional Average | 0.59% | Simple average of all hospitals (unweighted)
Zero-Infection Institutions | 232 | 53.1% of the 437 reporting institutions
Source: NHIA "2024 National Health Insurance Monitoring Indicator Monitoring Results Report" Indicator 2.1
```

Among the 437 reporting hospitals, 232 had zero infections for the entire year, accounting for 53.1%.[^1]

The change in the number of zero-infection institutions is also worth noting: from 2020 to 2024, there were 248, 238, 226, 230, and 232 such institutions, while the total number of reporting institutions was 466, 457, 444, 444, and 437, respectively.[^1] The number of zero-infection institutions bottomed out in 2022 and has since rebounded, with its proportion rising from 50.9% to 53.1%.

The duality of these numbers is evident: more than half of the hospitals had zero infections, but a few extreme values pulled the average up. The report also noted that the large standard deviations between 2021 and 2023 were caused by these extreme values, which have converged in 2024.[^1]

## Why This is a Negative Indicator

A wound infection is more than just a number. The report explains: infections prolong hospital stays and may require additional surgeries and treatments, thereby increasing National Health Insurance expenditures.[^1]

Therefore, this indicator is classified as a process indicator for inpatient care safety; the lower the value, the better.

## How to Read This Table

First, the overall 1.21% is a weighted value, reflecting the infection rate of all inpatient surgery patients; the institutional average of 0.59% is the simple average of each hospital's rate, where small hospitals carry the same weight as large ones. These two numbers should not be compared directly.[^1]

Second, a high proportion of zero-infection institutions does not necessarily mean low risk. Statistically, hospitals with smaller denominators are naturally more likely to report zero infections; the true signals lie in the extreme values and the standard deviation.

Third, be cautious when comparing across different hospitals. Generally, the type of surgery, patient complexity, and the completeness of reporting all affect infection rates.

The NHIA recommends continuous observation, particularly regarding whether the disparity between institutions continues to shrink.[^1] The drop from 4.39 to 1.18 represents significant progress.

There is one point prone to misinterpretation: while the number of zero-infection institutions increased and the institutional average decreased, the overall weighted infection rate did not follow suit. The NHIA's analysis explains that although more institutions reported zero infections, the absolute number of patients with inpatient surgical site infections still rose from 12,675 to 12,826, resulting in a slight increase of 0.02 percentage points in the overall rate.[^1]
