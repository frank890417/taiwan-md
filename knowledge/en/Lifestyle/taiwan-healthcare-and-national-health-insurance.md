# Taiwan's Healthcare and National Health Insurance: World's First at 99.6% Coverage Propped Up by 12.61%

> **30-second overview:** The National Health Insurance (NHI) launched on March 1, 1995; Yeh Chin-chuan himself admitted the "actual preparation time was only three days"[^1]. Thirty years later, coverage stands at 99.6% of the eligible population[^2], administrative costs have long stayed below 2%[^3], and the Numbeo Health Care Index has ranked Taiwan first for six consecutive years[^4]. Yet this system rests on three pressure points: a 5.17% premium rate, a nursing turnover rate at a 10-year high of 12.61%[^5], and the political taboo of "household total income" that no one has dared touch for 30 years. From the last-minute scramble of the first six days in 1995 to the 2026 three-shift nurse-to-patient ratio, this article deconstructs three entrenched narratives — "NHI is cheap," "five major voids = low point value," and "IC cards are world's first" — to reveal how a successful institutional design has locked the political pathway for system reform.

## "Preparation Time Was Actually Only Three Days"

At midnight on March 1, 1995, the National Health Insurance (NHI) officially launched. The external narrative was "world-record speed": 96% population coverage, one card for access to 18,000 contracted medical institutions across Taiwan[^6]. The internal version, as recalled by the first Director-General of the Central Health Insurance Agency, Yeh Chin-chuan, in 2021: "Preparation time was actually only three days," "At that time the NHI cards hadn't even been printed yet," "The IT system wasn't secured," "Fire burning our buttocks"[^1].

Over 20 million NHI cards were only rushed to completion a month after launch. On March 1, people used their national ID cards to enter clinics.

Pulling back the timeline, this system had actually been in the works for 27 years. In 1968, the Ministry of the Interior first drafted a universal health insurance plan. In 1986, the Executive Yuan approved 2000 as the target launch year. In 1988, the Council for Economic Planning and Development (CEPD) established a dedicated planning task force[^7]. On February 28, 1989, Premier Yu Kuo-hwa announced in the Legislative Yuan that the launch would be moved up to 1995. In July 1990, planning work was transferred from the CEPD to the Department of Health. After Hau Pei-tsun took office, he instructed moving it up further to 1994. In February 1991, the Department of Health established the National Health Insurance Planning Task Force. In December 1993, the Central Health Insurance Agency Preparatory Office was inaugurated, with Yeh Chin-chuan appointed as its director[^7].

The legislative process was not smooth. On July 16, 1994, DPP legislator Shen Fu-hsiung went on a hunger strike to protest the NHI Act[^8]. On July 19, the National Health Insurance Act passed its third reading. On November 12, labor groups took to the streets for the "Autumn Struggle," chanting "Three No-Insurances"[^7]. On December 23, 1994, the Organic Act of the Central Health Insurance Agency was passed. On January 1, 1995, the Central Health Insurance Agency was established. On February 25, 1995, Premier Lien Chan ordered "launch on schedule on March 1," and Yeh's "only three days of preparation time" is counted from this point[^1].

The system's framework was chosen in 1989 by a task force led by Harvard public health scholar William Hsiao, which compared models from six countries — the U.S., U.K., Germany, France, Canada, and Japan — and selected the Canadian-style "single-payer" model[^9]. Hsiao's reason for choosing the Canadian model was brief: "the quality of services was very high"[^10].

The five key attributes of single-payer were later broken down by academia as: the entire population placed in a single risk pool, a uniform benefit package, sufficient medical resources, a single purchaser setting the rules of the game, and global budgeting to control medical inflation[^11]. These five elements are the structural reason why Taiwan's NHI has sustained the world's highest coverage rate 30 years later. They are also the fundamental binding constraint behind why every reform over 30 years has been "move one and you move them all."

First-year satisfaction data is often obscured by "mythologizing": in 1995, first-year coverage surged to 96%, but contemporary polls showed satisfaction at only 39% and dissatisfaction at 47%[^12]. "Universal support" is a retrospective narrative. At the time, Taiwanese society had doubts about this new payment obligation that seemed to drop from the sky.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: "Single-payer" translates literally as "單一保險人" (single insurer), but these four characters locked in 30 years of political possibilities. Putting everyone in the same pool means any decision to adjust premium rates, benefits, copayments, or household total income calculation methods has no "pilot first," "trial first," or "phase-in first" exit ramp. Either everyone changes together, or no one changes. This was written into the DNA when Hsiao selected the Canadian model in 1989; 30 years later it is simultaneously the NHI's greatest strength and its greatest political brake.

## Li Yu-chun's Four Characters

The term "five major specialties all empty" (五大皆空) became almost synonymous with Taiwan's medical crisis in the 2020s: internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, and emergency medicine — five major specialties unable to recruit resident physicians. But the coiner of these four characters was not Yeh Chin-chuan, not the Medical Association, not the Ministry of Health and Welfare — it was Yang Ming University public health professor Li Yu-chun, who coined "four major specialties all empty" (四大皆空) (internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics) in the summer of 2009[^13]. Emergency medicine was added later by the media, turning it into "five major specialties all empty."

The timeline goes like this. Professor Chiang Tung-liang recalled in 2018: "In the summer of 2009, Control Yuan member Huang Huang-hsiung began conducting a comprehensive examination of the NHI. By 2011, when the comprehensive examination report was released, the issue of the four major specialties all empty suddenly became urgent"[^13]. Yeh Chin-chuan also wrote a counter-version in 2018: "The so-called 'five major specialties all empty' in the medical community probably first appeared at a certain seminar, when a certain hospital superintendent reported insufficient physician manpower in internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics within the hospital, and called it 'four major specialties all empty'; subsequently, others echoed the phrase, emergency medicine joined the fray, and with media distortion and overgeneralization, it became 'five major specialties all empty'"[^14].

The Control Yuan's correction case of July 17, 2012 (ROC year 101) was the first time this term appeared in an official document. Correction committee members Huang Huang-hsiung, Shen Mei-chen, and Liu Hsing-shan wrote bluntly: "The four major specialties face 'low pay, heavy workload, close to prison'"; "Medical disputes and the payment system have become two major 'tight-fillet spells' strangling the development of the five major specialties"; "Most hospitals have insufficient resident physician manpower in the four major specialties, with some working over 300 hours a month; in hospitals completely lacking resident physicians, attending physicians in the four major specialties must cover shifts themselves, plus on-call duty, sometimes reaching as high as 400 hours a month"[^15].

Yeh Chin-chuan has always had reservations about "five major specialties all empty." His 2018 article included a second sentence: "It is not that all five specialties are universally short of people; the total number of physicians is sufficient"[^14]. This counter-argument is almost unheard in the mainstream narrative, but it is crucial for understanding the subsequent inference that "low point value = five major specialties all empty." If the total number of physicians is sufficient, then "can't recruit" must be examined through distribution, work environment, medical dispute burden, and shift systems. Something explainable in a single word — "money" — cannot be something unsolved for 30 years.

## 5.17% Appears Twice

The political history of NHI premium rates can almost be told in a single table. At its launch in 1995, the rate was 4.25%. In September 2002 it was raised to 4.55%. In April 2010, during Yang Chih-liang's tenure, it was raised to 5.17% — this hike was so controversial that Yang resigned on January 26, 2011, right after the second-generation NHI passed its third reading[^16]. When the second-generation NHI took effect in January 2013, the base rate dropped back to 4.91% and a 2% "supplementary premium" was added. From 2016 to 2020 it fell to 4.69% plus a 1.91% supplementary premium. In January 2021, during Chen Shih-chung's tenure, the base rate was raised again to **5.17%**, with the supplementary premium simultaneously rising to 2.11%[^17].

5.17% has appeared twice. The first time was in 2010 when Yang Chih-liang pushed it through and then resigned. The second was in 2021 under Chen Shih-chung. Eleven years apart, the system went full circle and returned to the same number. This equilibrium is a structure bound by the dual constraints of "single payer + politically sensitive rates": every adjustment exacts a political cost; whenever that cost hits its limit, the rate is pushed back down, only to be raised again once finances can no longer hold.

The second-generation NHI's ultimate choice of the "supplementary premium" path was itself a compromise forced into a corner. The original version was "household total income" — levying premiums based on a household's total annual income, theoretically the fairest approach. But the 2010 legislative process saw a reversal. After the third reading in 2011, Yang Chih-liang bluntly stated: "The supplementary premium is a mistake," and "From start to finish, the second-generation NHI was meant to adopt household total income as the basis for premiums, but initially the Ministry of Finance said household total income couldn't be done, and then Finance Committee legislators actually overturned the Social Welfare and Environmental Hygiene Committee's resolution, proposing the supplementary premium idea in just three days"[^18].

Thus the supplementary premium that launched in 2013 targeted six income categories calculated per-payment: large bonuses, part-time wages, professional practice income, dividends, interest, and rent[^19]. This design had two loopholes from day one: per-payment calculation makes it easy to split payments to avoid the premium. Income outside the six categories (e.g., overseas income, crypto assets) was not captured. Fifteen years later, in November 2025, the NHIA planned to shift from "per-payment" to "annual settlement" — any cumulative amount exceeding NT$20,000 in a year would be taxed, the withholding cap raised from NT$10 million to NT$50 million, estimated to affect 4.8 million people and bring in NT$10 billion to NT$20 billion[^20]. But this reform was abruptly halted by the Executive Yuan in November 2025 with a "pause in planning"[^20], sparking public resentment among stock investors and retirees.

The four words "household total income" have gone untouched for 30 years. Hsiao Ching-lun's 1989 single-payer prototype already pointed toward comprehensive-income-tax-style premium calculation. Yang Chih-liang pushed it through in 2010 and stepped down directly afterward. Even the moderate "annual settlement" version was braked by the Executive Yuan in 2025. Technically feasible, politically impossible. Any reform that tries to pool different income sources into a single base will find opposition in the Legislative Yuan.

> ⚠️ **Controversial view**: The supplementary premium has long been criticized by the left as a "regressive tax." Salaried workers face mandatory monthly withholding, while capital-gain earners are only tapped when receiving dividends or rent, and even then there's a withholding cap. Yet the 2010 legislative process was exactly the opposite: left-wing labor groups opposed "household total income" (fearing labor households would be jointly assessed and burdened more), while the right-wing Ministry of Finance opposed "household total income" (fearing insufficient administrative capacity), resulting in the Social Welfare and Environmental Hygiene Committee's proposal being overturned by the Finance Committee in three days[^18]. Who are the "fat sheep that slipped through the net"? Three rounds of narrative have rotated over 30 years, and there is still no consensus.

## 39.8% Use, 54.8% Pay

The 2020 DGBAS National Conditions Statistical Bulletin No. 231 published a precise figure rarely cited: the population aged 65 and over consumed 39.8% of NHI resources. Ages 15–64 consumed 54.8%. Ages 0–14 consumed 5.4%[^21]. Compared to 2015, the 65+ share rose 4.2 percentage points. Per-capita NHI expenditure for the 65+ group exceeded NT$70,000; for those under 65 it was about NT$10,000 — a 7-fold gap[^21].

This 7-fold gap, multiplied by Taiwan's accelerating aging curve, is the biggest financial clock the NHI has faced in 30 years. Yeh Chin-chuan put it bluntly in 2020: "The current system can last at most five more years"[^22]. Counting five years from 2020 brings us to 2025. In November 2025 the NHI Committee approved a 2026 total budget of NT$988.335 billion, a 5.5% increase, with the safety reserve at two months and the current 5.17% premium rate unchanged[^23]. On the surface it held, but the way it held was hair-raising.

The first framing correction is the common claim that "the NHI is about to go bankrupt." The NHI operates on a pay-as-you-go basis; there is no concept of "bankruptcy." The precise question is whether the "safety reserve level" falls below the statutory floor[^24]. In June 2025 the NHIA estimated the safety reserve at "0.96 months"; by November it had become "two months." The difference came from a government injection of NT$3 billion plus a reallocation of NT$18.1 billion from the general budget[^23]. NHI Committee Executive Secretary Chou Shu-wan's November 19, 2025 statement was technical: "The 2026 NHI total budget safety reserve stands at two months, not below the waterline; the premium rate remains at the current 5.17% without adjustment"[^23].

A more hidden prop is the "guaranteed point value of NT$0.95" policy. Taiwan's NHI uses a "global budget + floating point value" settlement — the points claimed by medical institutions are divided by the fixed global budget, yielding a floating conversion to New Taiwan dollars. Point values falling to NT$0.7 or NT$0.8 in some counties and cities have drawn frequent complaints of "lose three cents on every dollar earned"[^25]. To placate the medical community, the government pledged from 2024 to guarantee a point value of NT$0.95, but _The Reporter_ exposed that this policy is estimated to add NT$70 billion in expenditure, with the Executive Yuan allocating NT$33.6 billion in subsidies (NT$18.1 billion reallocated from the general budget + NT$15.5 billion from the Revenue Surplus Fund)[^25]. Cheng Shou-hsia (Dean of NTU College of Public Health, first Director-General of the NHIA after its restructuring) summed it up in one sentence: "Guaranteeing the point value is tantamount to abolishing the global budget; the math works, but it doesn't solve the substantive problem"[^25].

The copayment reform of July 1, 2023: the outpatient drug copayment ceiling at medical centers and regional hospitals was raised to NT$300. Emergency copayments were set at NT$750 for medical centers and NT$400 for regional hospitals[^26]. After the new system took effect, 8.45 million people were affected, generating NT$3.273 billion in additional revenue in one year. But PTS News' one-year assessment was ironic: per-person annual outpatient visits reached 14.3, a four-year high. Average per-person emergency visits increased by 0.1 compared to the previous year[^26]. NHIA Director-General Shih Chung-liang emphasized the rise in chronic diseases at the primary care level plus an injection of NT$5.27 billion, but Medical Reform Foundation Executive Director Lin Ya-hui's critique hit the nerve: "The NHI is not commercial insurance; it emphasizes the spirit of shared risk and ability-to-pay"[^26]. Treating copayments as a "demand suppression tool" runs into a reaction from the spirit of the NHI Act itself.

## Low Point Value Is a Symptomatic Fix, Not a Root Solution

The mainstream narrative attributes the "Five Major Voids" to a single variable: "NHI reimbursement is too low." The extended inference: "Low point value → low physician salaries → cannot recruit staff." This inference has become near-consensus in medical politics from 2024 to 2026, but academia and the NHIA itself have five counter-arguments that, combined, dismantle this "single attribution."

The first is Cheng Shou-hsia's "Argument for Abolishing the Global Budget": "Guaranteeing the point value is equivalent to abolishing the global budget," "The math works, but it doesn't solve the substantive problem" [^25]. The original intent of the NHI's global budget system was to serve as a "brake on medical inflation"; guaranteeing a point value of 0.95 is equivalent to releasing that brake — money is injected, but medical utilization rises in tandem, and the following year the point value falls back again, creating a perpetual-motion subsidy.

The second is from Kao Jo-hsiang (Secretary-General of the Taiwan Medical Workers Union): "The Trickle-Down Effect Does Not Exist at All" [^25]. The Taiwan Medical Workers Union analyzed the financial reports of 13 private hospitals and found that "personnel cost ratio and profit margin are weakly correlated." Hospital surpluses are mostly spent on construction, equipment, and expansion, and do not automatically trickle down to frontline medical staff salaries. In other words, the transmission chain "government subsidizes NHI point value → hospitals make money → medical staff get raises" is not supported by the financial reports of these 13 hospitals.

The third is Lee Yu-chun's own counter-argument. The coiner of the term "Five Major Voids" herself told _The Reporter_ in 2024: "The point value is a symptomatic fix, not a root solution" [^25]. This is an ironic link: she coined the term to sound the alarm back then, but 15 years later she came out saying "It's not a money problem."

The fourth is from Shih Chung-liang (Director-General of the NHIA): "I hope the target point value can reach 0.95, but not through a guarantee mechanism, otherwise medical services will be distorted," and "Physician salaries are determined by hospital management; operators bear significant responsibility" [^25]. The NHIA Director-General personally threw the ball back to hospital operators: the point value the government provides is an overall budget; how it is distributed to physicians, nurses, and technicians is a hospital management issue, not an NHI issue.

The fifth is Yeh Chin-chuan's 2018 statement: "The overall number of physicians is sufficient" [^14]. If the total number is sufficient, then "cannot recruit" is a distribution problem, not a supply problem. What needs solving is "why emergency physicians prefer self-pay clinics" and "why OB/GYN physicians prefer to switch to aesthetic medicine," not "why overall physician salaries are low."

Piecing together these five counter-arguments yields an uncomfortable conclusion: the framing "Low point value = Five Major Voids" is simple, politically correct, and easy to mobilize, but falls apart under scrutiny. Real solutions involve medical dispute resolution, resident physician work-hour caps, internal hospital allocation, specialty recruitment systems, and recognition of foreign medical licenses — each one ten times harder than "adjusting the premium rate, adjusting the point value."

> 💡 **Did you know**: On average, each person in Taiwan makes 18 outpatient visits per year, more than twice the OECD country average [^27]. For the same cold symptoms, a patient might visit 3 clinics and receive 3 prescriptions. This is the "doctor shopping" long discussed in academia. One of the costs of cheap NHI is the structural amplification of medical utilization; the amplified volume in turn pushes down the point value. The chain "Cheap → More Use → Point Value Falls → Medical Staff Salaries Cannot Be Sustained" feeds back on itself and accelerates, not merely a linear transmission.

## 12.61% Turnover Rate, 60% Practice Rate

The precise scope of the nursing shortage must be read this way: a nursing license does not equal active practice. In 2023, there were approximately 310,000 licensed nurses, but the practice rate was only 60% — about 186,000 actually working in clinical settings[^28]. One-third of newly hired nurses leave within three months[^28]. Annual turnover rate (per hospital reports): 11.12% in 2019, 10.13% in 2021, 11.73% in 2022, **12.61% in 2023** — a 10-year high. The vacancy rate that same year was 9.05%, also a 10-year high[^29].

The aging population curve magnifies the problem. The average age of practicing nurses was 36.33 in 2019; by June 2024 it had surpassed 39[^28]. The share under age 40 fell from over 70% in 2017 to below 50% in 2023. Attrition destinations (analysis by Han Hsing-wen / National Taipei University of Business of 13 private hospital financial reports): 30% to NHI clinics, 25% to self-pay clinics, 10% to long-term care, 15% to other industries, 20% left the workforce, emigrated, or retired[^28].

Global demand is expanding in tandem. A shortage of 4.5 million nurses is projected worldwide by 2030. Taiwan had 186,000 practicing nurses in 2023; 2030 demand is estimated at 241,000–260,000, a gap of 55,000–74,000[^28]. Registered nurses in Australia earn AUD 1.44–2.2 million (NT$1.44–2.2 million) annually, 2–3 times Taiwan's level. Lin Hsiu-chu (Vice Chair, National Union of Nurses' Associations) said Australian scholars told her directly: "Sorry, but we've been aggressively recruiting Taiwanese nurses for several years now"[^28].

Bed closures are the most visible indicator. Taiwan's 22 medical centers report that NHI beds account for 92% of total beds. Post-pandemic conservative estimates put the bed-closure rate above 30%[^28]. Kao Ching-chiu (Deputy Superintendent, Wan Fang Hospital; former Chair, National Union of Nurses' Associations) put it most bluntly: "This is the most severe loss in 30 years," and "Which hospital would proactively report bed closures to the local health bureau? Hospitals don't talk about closing beds; they only talk about 'load reduction'"[^28].

The political tug-of-war over bed closures erupted in June 2024. Tsai Shu-feng (Director-General, MOHW Department of Nursing) publicly asked in June 2024: "Which hospitals are the ones closing beds?"[^28]. This was interpreted as "MOHW refuses to acknowledge bed closures," and on June 5, Minister Chiu Tai-yuan apologized for the remark at the Legislative Yuan[^30]. That same month, Chao Lin-yu (Chief of Spine Surgery, Chia Chi Hospital) told _The Reporter_ in an exclusive interview: "No surgeries to perform — does a surgeon still have a career?" and "We lack nurse anesthetists; surgeons are limited to just one operating day per week"[^28]. Lo Hsiang-yun (Director of Emergency Medicine, Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital) addressed a patient's family with what became the most quoted line of the 30-year nursing shortage: "You can yell at me, you can be rude to me, but not to the nurses!"[^28]

_The mobile nursing station at Chiayi Christian Hospital in 2007. The three-shift nurse-to-patient ratio reform, advanced to 2026, is the largest structural adjustment to nursing working conditions in 30 years._

Three-shift nurse-to-patient ratio reform: On March 1, 2024, MOHW announced an administrative order (day shift 1:6/1:7/1:10, evening shift 1:9/1:11/1:13, night shift 1:11/1:13/1:15). Penalties: district hospitals NT$250,000–500,000, regional hospitals NT$500,000–1,000,000, medical centers NT$1,000,000–2,000,000[^31]. This is the largest structural adjustment to nursing working conditions in 30 years, but academia generally assesses that "improved hours ≠ automatic return" — with Australia and Canada offering their current packages, and Taiwan's medical salary structure unchanged, the three-shift ratio can only stem the acceleration of attrition, not necessarily reverse it.

## The 99.6% Blind Spot

The "99.6% coverage rate" figure is often abbreviated as "universal." Precisely speaking, it is "99.6% of the eligible population"[^2]. Beyond the definition of eligibility, three categories of people fall outside: missing migrant workers, unregistered "hei-hu" infants born without household registration, and overseas residents who have suspended their coverage.

After losing NHI eligibility, missing migrant workers often incur unpaid medical bills when facing emergencies — a long-unresolved gap in Taiwan's migrant worker healthcare policy[^32].

A January 2025 policy report from the Global Taiwan Institute noted that the overseas nationals' "suspend-and-resume" mechanism has long been treated as exploitation — people live abroad without paying premiums, then resume coverage only when returning to Taiwan for medical care[^33].

The December 23, 2024 reform finally abolished suspend-and-resume. But how to calculate the spillover costs after abolition, and whether to apply it retroactively, remain political taboos.

The NHI database problem runs deeper. From its 1995 launch through 2024, Taiwan's NHI database has accumulated 70 billion medical records and 3.4 billion medical images, supporting over 8,414 peer-reviewed publications[^34].

The NHIA and NTU Hospital signed an MOU to jointly build the "world's first imaging-based cardiovascular risk database"[^34]. In academia, this is one of Taiwan's greatest soft power assets.

But on August 12, 2022, the Constitutional Court's Judgment No. 13 of 2022 ruled the world's largest NHI database unconstitutional on three grounds: lack of opt-out rights, insufficiently clear regulations, and absence of an independent oversight mechanism[^35]. A three-year deadline was set. On December 2, 2025, the "National Health Insurance Data Management Act" passed its third reading, granting opt-out rights[^36] — the single largest remediation in 30 years of NHI data governance.

Read these two things together: 99.6% is an achievement of coverage breadth. The database's unconstitutionality is the side effect of coverage applied "too thoroughly." One price of universal enrollment is that everyone's medical records go into the same pool; 30 years later, big data pulls them back out for research, and the public discovers "I never agreed." The Constitutional Court's ruling essentially returns "data rights" to individuals. This is a major shock to academic research, but a correction for fundamental rights protection.

Accessibility has another often-overlooked blind spot. The IDS (Mountain and Offshore Islands Medical Benefit Enhancement Project) launched in 1999, covering 49 mountain/offshore islands + 49 non-mountain offshore islands, providing designated outpatient services, 24-hour emergency care, mobile medical services, home visits, and referral transfers[^37]. The NHI covers "enrollment" (99.6%), but not "accessibility." Rural residents have NHI cards, but the nearest hospital may be 50 kilometers away — the card's practical meaning differs from that of urban residents. IDS is a remedial mechanism, not a systemic solution.

Taiwan's NHI IC cards were fully reissued in 2004 and are often claimed to be the "world's first." The accurate version: Slovenia completed nationwide deployment in September 2000, making it the "world's first" country to universalize health insurance smart cards. The HIIS (Slovenia's Health Insurance Institute) and the Hriberšek 2001 paper indexed in PubMed both document this[^38]. Taiwan was the second globally, but the largest in scale and deepest in integration. A difference of three and a half years.

This correction is not to deny Taiwan's achievements, but to correct the narrative inertia of "we are the world's first" — once internalized, this inertia extends into the inference that "therefore data governance is also world-class," and 30 years later one runs into the 2022 Constitutional Court judgment.

> ✦ **Closing warning**: The NHI IC card and NHIRD being at the forefront globally is an achievement accumulated over 30 years; but those who go too fast, too far, too deep are often tripped up by their own achievements. Constitutional Court Judgment No. 13 of 2022 marks the moment when the system has matured to the point where it must go back and catch up on lessons, not a failure of the system.

The "world's first" framing also needs correction in international indices. The Numbeo Health Care Index 2024 ranked Taiwan first for six consecutive years, with a score of 86/100 (South Korea 82.7, Japan 79.3, Netherlands 78.9, France 78.1), covering 4,119 cities and 43,700 respondents[^4]. But this is a user perception index, not an institutional metric; respondents are self-selected and include subjective ratings by healthcare users. The Bloomberg Health-Care Efficiency Index's actual position: Taiwan ranked 14th in 2018 (not #9); rose to the top 4 in 2020 due to COVID-19 pandemic response reshuffling; the index has ceased updates as of 2024[^39].

The academic citations are substantive. William Hsiao (Hsiao Cheng-lun)'s 2003 _Health Affairs_ 22(3):77 paper "Does Universal Health Insurance Make Health Care Unaffordable? Lessons From Taiwan" (not _The Lancet_) is the most-cited academic paper on Taiwan's NHI[^9]. Cheng Tsung-han's 2015 _Health Affairs_ article states: "Taiwan's NHI stands out as a high-performing single-payer national health insurance system that provides universal health coverage to Taiwan's 23.4 million residents based on egalitarian ethical principles"[^40]. Princeton professor Uwe Reinhardt (deceased 2017, one of Taiwan's NHI advisors since 1989) wrote in his own article: "I have not advocated the single-payer model here because our government is too corrupt"[^41], with the system's designer himself publicly opposing U.S. replication.

As for the "Obama learned from Taiwan" narrative, no records of official delegations directly visiting Taiwan to study the system can be found in publicly available information. The accurate version: Taiwan is the case most frequently cited by U.S. single-payer advocates, forming a citation chain composed of academics (Reinhardt, Cheng) and politicians (Bernie Sanders mentioned it multiple times), not "the Obama administration directly learned from it"[^41].

The private insurance market is another piece of counter-evidence. A 2004 survey showed Taiwan's household private health insurance purchase rate at 72.3%; in 1993 (before NHI launch) it was 63.9%[^42]. The hypothesis that "universal health insurance locks out the private insurance market" is reversed — nine years after NHI's launch, household private insurance penetration rose rather than fell, by 8.4 percentage points. Taiwanese people are pragmatic: they know the NHI is good, but also know that needs outside the NHI benefit package — ward upgrades, self-paid therapies, overseas emergency medical care, long-term care, cancer targeted drugs — require private insurance supplementation. This is also the structural reason why the question "Is the NHI enough?" has become increasingly common 30 years later.

## Three Sentences Added Together

Thirty years later, that little card from midnight on March 1, 1995 is still here — only now it lives in an app as a QR code. But the same system rests on three pillars: 99.6% coverage, a 5.17% premium rate, and a 12.61% nurse turnover rate. The first two are Taiwan's pride; the third is a bill no one has dared touch for 30 years.

Yeh Chin-chuan said in 2020: "The current system can last at most five more years"[^22]. Yang Chih-liang said before resigning after the third reading in 2011: "The supplementary premium was a mistake"[^18]. Lee Yu-chun, the coiner of the term "four major voids" himself, said in 2024: "The point value is a symptomatic fix, not a fundamental solution"[^25]. Put together, the three sentences aren't pessimistic; they read more like words that three decades of implementation have finally emboldened someone to say aloud. The world's best NHI demands the world's greatest commitment; but "commitment" has never meant just adding money. It points to household gross income, to internal hospital allocation, to resident physician working hours, to medical dispute liability, to data governance. Each one is ten times harder than adjusting the premium rate.

Hsiao Ching-lun's reason for choosing the Canadian model in 1989 was that "the quality of services was very high"[^10]. Thirty years later, Taiwan's world-leading coverage rate rests atop the lowest reimbursement point value and a 12.61% nurse turnover rate. A system's success is not the antonym of its defects — success itself creates a political path dependence on those defects. Breaking free won't come from another "three days with fire under the seat"; it will come from the first time in 30 years that someone is willing to tackle the taboos of "household gross income," "hospital allocation," and "data exit rights" one by one.

## Supervision Indicators: Ten Improved, Eight Declined

If you want to know how the NHI "actually operates," a more suitable read than total budgets and premium rates is the _National Health Insurance Supervision Indicators Monitoring Results Report_ published annually by the NHIA. It tracks the previous year's performance across 23 indicators in five dimensions: efficiency, medical quality, effectiveness, resource allocation, and finance.[^43]

The 113th-year results show 10 improvements, 8 declines, and 5 stable; among them, more than half of the resource allocation indicators declined.[^43]

Several concrete figures illustrate what these indicators measure:

- Emergency patient retention rate exceeding 24 hours: 3.68%, the highest in three years (111th year 2.56%, 112th year 3.32%).[^43]
- Proportion of terminal cancer patients who had hospice care costs claimed in the last six months of life: maintained at around 60% over the past two years; in the 113th year, average medical costs for those receiving hospice care were 397,273 points, compared to 468,856 points for those not receiving it, a difference of 71,583 points.[^43]
- Average length of stay for acute care beds: 7.3 days (112th year), higher than the OECD average of 6.7 days, but far lower than Japan's 15.7 days.[^43]
- Primary outpatient care rate at regional hospitals and above: 18.37% (113th year), a slight increase from 17.21% in the 111th year, indicating hospitals still handle many minor ailments that should be treated at the primary care level.[^43]

A reminder: these are all claims data, measuring procedures and costs, not patient experiences. Reading them together with satisfaction surveys and complaint data is the only way to avoid mistaking "efficiency" for "quality."
