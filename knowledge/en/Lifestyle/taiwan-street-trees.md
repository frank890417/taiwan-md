# Taiwan's Street Trees: A Road Named After Trees, and a Century-Long Unfinished Decision

> **30-second overview:** The trees on Taiwan's streets are the product of decisions: the orderly planning of Japanese colonial officials, post-war mayors' quick-fix achievements, and red cloth that ordinary people tied on themselves. The reasoning has changed several times over, but the trees still stand there shading us. Yet whether a street tree will topple thirty years later, or crack the sidewalk beneath it, is largely determined on the day it was planted in that two-meter-deep, 1.2-meter-wide pit. Taipei had 86,404 street trees in 1984 and 87,754 in 2020—only about 1,000 more in thirty-six years. The Forestry Bureau's 2022 guidelines already explicitly state that heading (severe pruning) is "prohibited except where public safety requires it," yet in 2026, 6,554 people still petitioned for the same thing—because that guideline has no enforcement teeth.

## No. 292, Qiashu Road Section 2 — A Street Address Named After a Tree

At No. 292, Qiashu Road Section 2 in Changhua City, the address plate doesn't mark a house. It marks a tree.

The age of that Alstonia scholaris is a matter of local dispute — no one can pin it down. But the stories the community has passed down are specific: it would transform into a commoner to hire a theater troupe to celebrate its birthday, a merchant who picked up one of its fallen branches once got lost, even its green leaves could cure illness, and temple lore ties it to the legend of the Jiaqing Emperor's tour of Taiwan[^1]. A 2009 measurement recorded more concrete dimensions: height 12.43 meters, circumference 9 meters, crown projection area 333.45 square meters — the only one of Changhua County's 53 registered heritage trees that grows in Changhua City proper[^2].

The road name on that address plate came from the tree. Qiashu Lane, Qiashu Road — both names derive from it. The road was fitted to the tree, not the tree to the road.

The situation of the several hundred thousand other street trees on Taiwan's streets is the exact opposite: the road is drawn first, the sidewalk width is fixed first, and only the leftover space is allocated to trees.

What does that leftover space look like? In 2013, PTS's _Our Island_ walked Taiwan's streets and recorded the standard: "Most street trees today live in small square pits about 2 meters deep and 1.2 meters on each side"[^3]. Li Youtian, director of the Taiwan Urban Forest Health and Beautification Association, put it more bluntly in 2015: the existing tree pits are "almost only one meter square." He also noted that green building rating standards calculate canopy coverage for shade trees as anywhere from 16 to 36 square meters, yet specify no minimum planting pit area[^4].

Arborist Hsiao Wen-wei's rule of thumb: a tree's root system width is typically twice the crown spread. As for spacing, "the standard interval for street tree planting is one tree every three meters, but mature trees ideally need five to six meters between them"[^5]. That gap doesn't appear automatically as the tree grows. On the day it's planted, three meters apart; thirty years later, still three meters apart — except the tree now needs twice that space.

```tw-stat
A street tree's range of movement is mostly fixed on the day it's planted
2 m deep × 1.2 m wide | Common street tree pit | PTS _Our Island_, 2013 field survey
Twice the crown spread | Root system width a tree should have | Arborist Hsiao Wen-wei's rule of thumb
3 meters | Common planting interval | Same arborist: mature trees need 5 to 6 meters
Sources: PTS _Our Island_, Shih Hsin University _Small World_
```

Singapore's approach offers a useful benchmark: large shade trees need at least 3 meters of linear growing space, and a single planting pit must be at least 4 meters square[^6]. Taiwan's one-meter-square pit on the street is roughly one-tenth the area.

So the question "Will this tree blow over and crush a car on a typhoon night?" — most of the time, half the answer was already written the day it was planted twenty years ago. The other half accumulates from every pruning afterward, every utility line excavation, every complaint about whether to intervene.

So who decides?

## Every Generation Plants Trees for a Different Reason

In 1898, Tashiro Yasusada, working at the Colonial Government's Bureau of Industrial Development, submitted 《Taiwan Road and Street Tree-Planting Theory (with Tree-Planting Opinions for Key Locations)》. Urban researcher Yang Chieh-ying described this document as "marking the beginning of Taiwan's street trees moving toward systematic governance"[^7]. Tashiro later wrote two more books: 《Taiwan Street and Village Tree-Planting Manual》 in 1900, and the two-volume 《Taiwan Street Trees and Municipal Village Tree-Planting Manual》 published by the Taiwan Governor-General's Forestry Bureau in 1920[^8]. These three documents span twenty-two years—the first a proposal, the latter two published works.

A proposal is one thing; institutional implementation is another. Historian Wu Ming-yong's research shows that neither the 1898 regulations for the Taipei nursery nor the 1911 Forestry Experiment Station regulations explicitly listed street tree survey and experimentation items. It wasn't until 1921, when the Central Research Institute's Forestry Division was established, that street tree surveys were first formally listed as one of six departmental duties[^9]. Twenty-three years passed between one person proposing a theory and the state apparatus actually adding it to the official task list.

The first visible results appeared in Taipei's Three-Line Road. The Taipei city wall was demolished in Meiji 37 (1904), and the site was converted into a ring road that was completed in 1909[^10][^11]. This road later became known as "the Paris of the East," though this nickname only circulates among tourist guides, blogs, and encyclopedia entries—it cannot be found in Japanese colonial newspapers or official documents, making it more of a later-generation appellation[^11]. The same planning logic of "wide roads lined with orderly rows of trees" was later applied to today's [Zhongshan North Road](/en/geography/zhongshan-north-road-tiaotong/), formerly known as Chokushi Street.

Trees planted on roads were themselves displays of rule. Yang Chieh-ying wrote clearly about royal palms: they were "not only symbols of colonial rule or Japanese imaginings of tropical culture, but also manifestations of tropical colonial governance grounded in scientific knowledge, demonstrating externally the fruits of civilized, progressive administration"[^7].

It looked like a transplant from Japan proper. Japan's modern street tree development began in Meiji 7 (1874) with black pines and cherry blossoms planted on Tokyo's Ginza Street, twenty-four years before Taiwan[^12]. Yet the two tree species lists barely overlap: Japan's characteristic avenue trees were black pine, cherry, ginkgo, and willow, while Taiwan produced Chinese parasol trees, banyan, royal palms, and royal poinciana. What both shared was "systematic tree-planting through Western-style urban planning"—the administrative logic of the Meiji state—but the trees themselves grew from Taiwan's own soil.

In 1917, coinciding with the opening of Tainan Park, over a hundred royal poinciana saplings were planted on Taisho Street (today's Zhongshan Road), eventually becoming what was called the "Royal Poinciana Flower City"[^13]. Multiple sources agree that royal poinciana was introduced to Taiwan in 1897, though accounts differ on who brought it and from where[^14].

```tw-timeline
1898 | Tashiro Yasusada proposes tree-planting theory | 《Taiwan Road and Street Tree-Planting Theory》 codifies "which trees for which streets"
1909 | Three-Line Road completed | Former Taipei city wall site becomes approximately 40-meter-wide ring road with median plantings
1917 | Over a hundred royal poinciana planted on Tainan's Taisho Street | Coinciding with Tainan Park opening, later becomes the "Royal Poinciana Flower City"
1943 | Black board tree introduced to Taiwan | Forty years later it would be called the "political tree"
1966 | Small-leaf alstonia introduced to Taiwan | About twenty years later, large-scale planting begins islandwide; nurseries use grafting to meet demand
1983 | Taiwan Provincial Government declares greening year | Fast-growing, visibly effective tree species begin mass deployment
Sources: Eye on the City, Wu Ming-yong (2008), National Cultural Memory Bank, Curio Taiwan, Zhang Jinyu (2009), Central News Agency
```

When the government changed, so did the reasons. Ren'ai Road was already drawn as a tree-lined boulevard in Taipei's 1932 urban plan[^15], but after the war its width became a diplomatic facade: it was widened to sixty meters in 1958 as an international reception avenue, and by 1967 the entire length was widened to forty meters, with three sections expanded to one hundred meters, making it Taipei's widest street[^15]. Between the 1950s and 1970s, painter Yan Shui-long, invited by Mayor Kao Yu-shu to serve as municipal advisor, proposed the broad tree-lined boulevards of Dunnan Road and Ren'ai Road, along with the Ren'ai Circle concept[^16].

As for whether this concept was modeled on Paris's Champs-Élysées, accounts vary. Taipei City Government's Public Works Bureau's own press release uses the phrasing "some say it might be"[^15], while the popular history media outlet 《Story》 is more direct: "While we might imagine that Yan Shui-long must have seen the Arc de Triomphe and Champs-Élysées during his brief study in France, whether he used this famous Parisian landmark as a design template lacks reliable documentary evidence."[^16]

Yan Shui-long departed for France in August 1930, returned to Japan in autumn 1932, and had no clearly documented Taiwan activities until 1937[^17]. Having the opportunity to see something and using it as a design template are two different matters of intensity.

By the 1980s, the reason had become speed. In 1983, Taiwan's Provincial Government declared a greening year, and the black board tree, because it grew fast, turned green quickly, and showed visible results, was planted widely throughout streets. The media later gave it a nickname: the "political tree"[^18]. The nickname is memorable, but the dates are easily distorted by it: the black board tree was actually introduced to Taiwan in 1943[^19], forty years before the "political tree" label took shape in 1983. In 1988 it was even voted as Taichung's city tree by residents. Problems likely emerged gradually a decade after planting: surface roots, odor, seed dispersal, and susceptibility to wind damage[^18].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The common narrative is "the black board tree was a mistake," so in recent years it has been banned and removed. Yet in the full nineteen-page peer-reviewed 2009 paper by Jang Chin-yu from Tunghai University's Department of Landscape Architecture, the phrase "political tree" never appears once—the paper's description of the black board tree is neutral botanical description: extremely rapid growth leading to brittle wood and high branch breakage rates after typhoons[^19]. It's easier to place responsibility on the tree species than on "whoever decided to plant a tree that grows to twenty meters in a three-meter planting pit." The same tree in Borneo's rainforest has no wind-throw problems as a giant, and the old black board tree from the Japanese colonial period at Jianguo High School remains structurally intact because of adequate space. The tree hasn't changed; the conditions for planting it have.

Another logic behind tree species selection is the market. Small-leaf alstonia was introduced to Taiwan in 1966 and began to be favored and planted widely islandwide about twenty years later. When Jang Chin-yu conducted surveys in 2006, she found these trees had oddly shaped trunks. After interviewing the director of the Changhua County Horticultural Association, she learned the reason: prices were too high and demand was urgent, so nurseries devised grafting small-leaf alstonia onto alstonia rootstock. "The main reason was to exploit alstonia's rapid growth characteristics, allowing small-leaf alstonia to mature into trees quickly for sale and profit"[^19].

Decades later, the upper small-leaf alstonia grows faster than the lower alstonia, the graft union swells and protrudes, and the lower trunk visibly narrows. Jang Chin-yu worried that strong winds might cause it to topple at the narrowed point[^19]. A 2020 Taipei City field survey put a scale on this: of the city's 685 alstonia trees, 656 were grafted—a ratio of 95%[^20].

Singapore was doing something different in the same period. On May 11, 1967, Lee Kuan Yew proposed the "Garden City," which became a top-down national greening policy[^21]. The term was borrowed from Englishman Ebenezer Howard—he used it in 1898 to describe self-sufficient cooperative towns, and Japan's Home Ministry introduced it in 1907, transforming it into commuter suburbs. One name passed through three hands, and each person redefined it according to their own needs. Taiwan's century of tree species succession is another way of writing the same story.

And it's not just government that decides the trees.

## A Thousand Years of Worship, No Match for a Land Title

The Alstonia scholaris in Changhua survived to today not by luck alone. There is a temple beside it.

Taiwan's folk veneration of old trees has its own vocabulary: _dà-tshhuê-kong_ (great tree deity), _tshhuê-oo-kong_ (tree king deity), _tshhuê-á-kong_ (tree deity), or simply named after the species—_hôo-tshhuê-kong_ (banyan deity), _tshua-tshinn-kong_ (Alstonia scholaris deity). The core is animistic nature worship—trees over a century old and of massive girth are regarded as possessing spirit and life, and thus receive veneration[^22].

The practice is woven into daily life. A red cloth is wound around the trunk, an incense burner placed before it, and the tree gains divine status. Parents bring infants to the old tree deity to "adopt as godchildren," outfitting them with a protective amulet called a _kinn_. Each year they return to the temple to exchange the amulet; when they reach adulthood, a "coming-of-age" ceremony is held. The tree king deity at Tainan's Xigangbao'an Temple traces its origins to the Qing Jiaqing era, and temple records say over two hundred years have accumulated more than one hundred thousand godchildren[^23]. When godchildren return, they bring their childhood baby clothes, which are hung in a "longevity children's clothing box" beside the tree; after the ritual, they change into new adult clothes to take home.

In Puli, Nantou, there stands a thousand-year-old Alstonia scholaris, estimated at over twelve hundred years old—the oldest tree officially recognized by Puli Town Office. The old name of Puli's Tongsheng District was "Alstonia scholaris Foot," a place name derived from this very tree[^24].

It sounds like a complete protection mechanism: folk practice independent of government or environmental groups, organically cultivating a relationship with the tree spanning centuries. Yet this relationship has its limits.

The three-hundred-ping plot of land where Puli's thousand-year Alstonia scholaris stands is privately owned, with the landowner asking approximately thirty million New Taiwan dollars. The Xingnan Temple management committee launched a "sacred tree land-purchase fund," raising money for three years and collecting over three million. A guesthouse operator, unable to bear the tree king's declining health, personally spent over thirty million to buy the adjacent entire townhouse, demolishing concrete and carports to let the tree breathe—yet lacked the means to purchase the three-hundred-ping plot beneath the tree king[^25]. Faith can unite community sentiment and genuinely raise funds. But against the market price of private land, it yields only one-tenth of what is needed.

The old banyan in Taipei's Beitou faced a different wall. This two-hundred-year-old banyan, which locals call _tshhuê-á-kong_, stands seventeen meters tall with a trunk circumference of 595 centimeters and a crown spread of 660 square meters—by crown area alone it exceeds the four-hundred-square-meter threshold for nationally protected trees. The Civil Affairs Bureau even erected a marker calling it a "living heritage site." In 2011, its vigor weakened and disease advanced; after the Cultural Bureau's inspection in April, officials judged the growing base insufficient. The neighborhood chief Ong Tsiann-hue told media at the time: "Since the April inspection, the city government's relevant departments have taken no action whatsoever."[^26]

Protection standards met, faith status established, neighborhood chief willing to speak—all three conditions aligned, yet the administrative process never moved.

```tw-quote
In the 1970s, due to economic development, road construction, and development—even expansion of the small temples they once depended on—many old trees quietly disappeared from our midst
Ong Ka-jun | Council of Agriculture _Agricultural Policy and Information_, 2011[^70]
```

Government decides where trees are planted; faith decides whether trees are remembered. Whoever decides, we live beside these trees every day.

## This Street Changes Its Clothes Four Times a Year

Beyond decisions, there is something the body notices first: seasons. In March, kapok flowers carpet the ground. The orange blossoms fall whole, and stepping on them makes a sound. By April and May, the tree shifts its mode of existence: the fluff begins to fly, clogging air-conditioner filters, blocking drainage holes, triggering allergies. New Taipei and Hsinchu see citizen complaints every year during this season[^27]. The same tree transforms from "most beautiful" to "public grievance" within two months.

May to July belongs to the golden shower tree's rain of gold, with cascades of yellow flowers blanketing the arcade eaves and asphalt[^28]. During the same period, the royal poinciana blooms, coinciding precisely with graduation season. The four characters "royal poinciana in bloom" carry a force for Taiwanese people probably stronger than any other street tree—everyone who went to school in Taiwan has been struck by this phrase once. The royal poincianas on that Tainan street were planted in 1917.

![Royal poincianas at the Taiwan High Speed Rail Tainan Station plaza, with dense summer foliage and elongated seed pods already formed](/article-images/lifestyle/tainan-thsr-flame-tree-2013.webp)
_August 2013, royal poincianas at the Taiwan High Speed Rail Tainan Station plaza. The large-scale planting of royal poincianas in Tainan can be traced back to over a hundred trees planted on Taisho Street in 1917. Photo: Genshi Sei, Wikimedia Commons, CC0 1.0 (full source at end of article)._

August to October is when the Taiwan rain tree takes the stage, yellow flowers giving way to reddish-brown seed capsules—one of Taiwan's few street trees that changes color entirely. Beneath it runs a complete ecological chain: the red-rumped swallow, the Taiwan rain tree, and the Taiwan leaf-footed bug are all interconnected. This bug's breeding season from November to April often brings it near Taiwan rain trees, with Taiwan's earliest reports dating to mid-December 1999 in Taichung City[^29].

(The leaf-footed bug, often mentioned alongside it, is a different insect that primarily damages lychee and longan, treating the Taiwan rain tree as a secondary host—for more on its entanglement with fruit trees, see [Taiwan's Fruit Kingdom](/en/food/taiwan-fruit-kingdom/).)

Once tree groves reach a certain age, the street begins to behave like a forest. The camphor and banyan street trees that matured after the 1990s function ecologically like a low-altitude broadleaf forest cut into strips and laid between concrete—this is partly why the [black-crowned night heron](/en/nature/malayan-night-heron/), originally recorded as "rare," is now increasingly spotted in urban green spaces.

Yet the trees we notice are not the same batch as the trees actually most abundant on the street.

```tw-bars
The most common trees on Taipei's streets are not the ones you look up to see
Banyan | 13.9% | Already exceeds the international recommendation ceiling of 10% for a single species
Beech | 10.8%
Camphor | 9.5%
Liquidambar | 8.1%
Taiwan rain tree | 7.6%
Source: Eye on the City, compiled from Taipei City street tree census data
```

Taipei has a total of 199 street tree species, with only 75 native species, yet native species account for over 60% of total trees[^30]. "Few varieties" and "many numbers" occurring simultaneously is where this data is most easily misread.

As for banyan at 13.9%, it exceeds the first threshold of the frequently cited "10/20/30 rule": no single species exceeds 10%, no single genus exceeds 20%, no single family exceeds 30%. This rule is often credited to American scholar Santamour, though in his 1990 conference paper he himself said he was uncertain who first proposed the 10% rule[^31]. Research analyzing 108 cities worldwide found that no city fully complies with this standard[^32]. Taipei's overage is the norm, not the exception.

Norm does not mean without cost—when a single species is overrepresented, one disease outbreak or one extreme weather event could affect all the trees on an entire street simultaneously, and this too is a consequence of choices made decades ago when the trees were planted[^30].

As for why it's cooler under trees, a National Taiwan University horticulture team measured it in a Taipei park in 2010: the air temperature under the canopy was 0.64 to 2.52 degrees Celsius lower than unshaded ground, and soil surface temperature was 3.28 to 8.07 degrees lower, with canopy leaf density contributing most to the cooling effect[^33]. This was measured in a park, not on a street, but it gives a sense of scale to the everyday experience of "walking in tree shade."

Zhang Xiaofeng's essay "Street Trees" was once a lesson in the state-approved textbooks of Taiwan's national language curriculum, and its opening lines are probably among the passages most frequently assigned for analysis of main ideas to people under middle age in Taiwan: "Our home is on the mountain, in the primeval forest where daylight never reaches. Yet here we stand, standing on the edge of this two-lane road—this is undoubtedly a kind of fall."[^34]

The word "fall" is written heavily. But it is not only the trees that bear the cost.

## Trees Fall Down, Then We Discover No One Was Actually Managing Them

Liu Xiangming, the neighborhood chief of Dalin Li in Tainan, once described a very concrete gap: the city government prunes street trees once every six months, far short of the frequency at which tree leaves fall[^35]. The area he manages is planted with Washington palms, and their massive leaves drop faster than the twice-yearly pruning can keep up with. This is one neighborhood chief speaking to a city—it can't be generalized to Taiwan's entire pruning cycle—but it points to where public grievance truly grows: in the soil of "no one managing it."

Associate Professor Yang Guo-zhen of Providence University describes the other half: "The most beautiful thing about kapok trees is how the branches extend outward, layer upon layer, so stunning when they bloom. Now the branches can't grow, the flowers can't open, the fruit can't develop—people are really quite awful!"[^36] The same tree species: residents complain about too much fluff, scholars complain about humans pruning them into unrecognizable shapes. Both complaints are real.

The root damage part has numbers. In 2009, Tunghai University's Zhang Jinyu and Huang Xiaoju surveyed ten species of street trees in Kaohsiung—2,113 trees total—and classified them by root morphology into four types: buttress roots, stem-base aerial roots, shallow roots, and stem-base spreading roots. The conclusion was direct: every surveyed species with a diameter at breast height exceeding ten centimeters damages the tree pit border, and damage severity increases with diameter[^37]. Sidewalks buckling up has nothing to do with a tree's character; it has everything to do with how small a frame it's been squeezed into.

What about moving the trees? In the 1994 Taiwan Route 3 widening project in Miaoli, nearly two hundred old camphor trees were relocated elsewhere. Within three years, 30 percent had died; after more than a decade, only twenty-two remained alive—an 11 percent survival rate. This figure currently traces back to only a single media investigation[^38].

In 2014, to accommodate the Taipei Dome area expansion and the widening of Guangfu South Road and Zhongxiao East Road, 87 liquidambar, rosewood, and kapok trees on sidewalks and traffic islands needed transplanting[^39]. During the same period, the Songshan Tobacco Factory park area originally had nearly a thousand trees; 780 were transplanted. The contractor promised a survival rate above 80 percent, but public television's follow-up reporting documented "over a hundred dead," including an eighty-year-old camphor tree that died from poor drainage after transplanting[^40].

The gap between plan and reality sometimes shows up the same day. In a 2013 transplanting project at Wenxin Road South Yuan Park in Taichung, MRT Project Director Guo Zhiwen said the transplanting "followed all regulations," but tree-protection volunteers on site witnessed contractors beginning work without any root cutting whatsoever. A volunteer named Angela said two things on the spot: "Too brutal," and "the trees simply cannot survive"[^41].

As for the disasters themselves, the numbers are a mess.

When Taipei Mayor Chiang Wan-an was asked about the typhoon in 2026, he said over 3,000 street trees fell[^71]; poet Wu Sheng's 2024 commentary article cited over 10,000 in Taipei, over 15,000 in New Taipei, and at least 13,000 in Kaohsiung[^72]. An eleven-year-old typhoon, and there's still no agreed-upon answer to "how many trees fell." Many people decide about trees; few bear the consequences. Even the task of recording those consequences has no one to take it on.

City councilor candidate Kao Chia-yu, after a street tree fell and crushed a car, discovered that the fallen tree's root zone contained visible pipes, large stones, concrete backfill, and plastic bags. Her statement was: "While typhoons are natural disasters, if management, construction, or maintenance problems exist ordinarily, responsibility cannot be entirely attributed to natural disaster."[^42]

To ask who should be responsible, the first question is: who manages this tree?

The 2013 《Our Island》 episode concluded: "Currently, street tree management has no unified contact point from central to local government; sometimes a single road's trees belong to different departments, each doing their own thing."[^43] Thirteen years later, in the Golden Coast area of Tainan, roadside trees might belong to the Highway Bureau, those in parks to the Public Works Bureau, those in parking lots to the Transportation Bureau, and those along levees to the Water Resources Bureau[^44]. Taipei divides them into two major categories by land ownership: street trees fall under the Parks Department, while trees on private land, residential yards, and government/school property are the responsibility of the owner[^45]. The rules have always existed; they just naturally grew into a fragmented shape.

Beyond fragmented rules, there's the matter of personnel. Zhuang Jieren, director of the Kaohsiung Forest City Association, noted in 2023 a striking contrast: "Taipei's Public Works Bureau Parks and Street Lights Engineering Management Office has 1,453 staff, but Kaohsiung's Parks Department has only about 100 people," and those hundred-plus must manage over eight hundred parks and green spaces[^46]. These two figures, without converting to "trees per person," would exaggerate the gap if compared directly, but the direction is clear.

Hsinchu's scale is even smaller: the Parks Department has twenty-four field staff organized into eighteen teams, responsible for nearly 130 parks, green spaces, plazas, and public restrooms. One person may sweep four locations a day, totaling 5,566 ping[^47]. This is cleaning and maintenance, not tree pruning, but it's the same stretched workforce.

Zhuang Jieren also said something more concise: "Taiwan's government often removes trees and then 'never plants them back.'"[^48] Taipei's street trees numbered 86,404 in 1984; the 2011–2014 Tree-Lined Avenue Plan once pushed them above 90,000; in 2020 they were 87,754, recorded as the lowest in a decade[^30]. Thirty-six years, a city whose population and built area have changed dramatically, and the total number of street trees has barely moved.

Professional expertise, though, has grown. Taiwan's first ISA Certified Arborist exam in 2014 had seventeen applicants and seven passed; the candidates included five PhDs and ten master's degree holders[^49]. Chen Hongkai, deputy executive director of the Daan Forest Park Friends Foundation, later left a frequently cited remark: "How well one plants a tree, how kindly a city treats a tree, is an indicator of how far that city's civilization has progressed."[^50]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> In the public reporting on street trees over these past dozen-plus years, there are tree doctors, arborists, neighborhood chiefs, city councilors, professors, tree-protection volunteers, and contractor bosses. Not one piece has the person standing on the lift with the chainsaw telling their own story. Reporting's visual habit points the camera at people with titles—personal biography after professional certification is easy to write; the labor perspective of outsourced work crews is not. How a city treats trees may be the same question as how it treats the people who prune them; it's just that the latter hasn't even been asked yet.

The most unresolved cost among these is: who should decide when to prune, how much to prune, and when to remove. And this question hasn't just started being debated this year.

## Rules Written on Paper for Twelve Years, with No Teeth

In late February 2009, before the last old camphor tree at the Songshan Tobacco Factory was moved away, tree-protection activists launched a tree-hugging protest[^51]. In Taichung, Zhang Meihui also spent years petitioning the city government about blackboard trees it wanted to cut down[^52].

On March 28, 2013, the New Taipei City Government planned to relocate thirty-two old banyan trees inside Banqiao Jiangcui Junior High School. Pan Han-chiang climbed into a tree and stayed there for twelve days; on April 8, police forcibly took him to a hospital[^53]. On August 10, 2014, the Tree Party was founded[^54].

This is the third kind of person who "decides about trees"—beyond government and faith, it is citizens who put their bodies in front of machinery. And what they have been demanding has barely changed over more than a decade.

In December 2013, Huang Shuru, deputy director of Taipei's Parks and Street Lights Engineering Management Office, publicly admitted that the current street tree pruning standards were set in 2000 and "indeed some parts have become outdated," promising to complete a revised draft within three months. In the same report, city councilor Li Qingfeng tallied 8,635 trees pruned citywide due to transplantation since 2007, with at least 1,017 confirmed dead. Tree-protection groups demanded replacing low-bid contracting with qualification standards; Hsieh Hongming, president of the landscape engineering trade association, offered an industry-speak line: "Cutting all at once won't save money anyway!" Topping-style pruning only causes trees to sprout more adventitious buds[^55].

The three-month promise stretched past twelve years to 2026.

A national document did appear in between. On January 27, 2022, the then-Forestry Bureau released the 《Ornamental Tree Pruning Operations Guide》, which stated in its first point: given that improper pruning of public trees has become rampant, and "there is no consistent national tree pruning technical standard"[^56].

The guide was clear about "topping"—the official technical term is "stub cutting," "colloquially called topping pruning," and Appendix 1 stated that excessive and high-intensity internodal pruning is "prohibited except where public safety requires it"[^56].

In other words, the ban on topping-style pruning was written in black and white in a central government document four years ago. What were the six thousand-plus people petitioning for in 2026?

On June 27, 2026, a proposal titled "Establish National Standards for Tree Pruning and Felling, Prohibit Topping-Style Pruning and Improper Felling" appeared on the Public Policy Online Participation Platform. The proposal's first sentence read: "In recent years, street trees, park trees, and public-space trees across Taiwan's counties and cities have commonly suffered excessive pruning, topping-style pruning, bare-crown pruning, and large-scale felling."[^57]

The first of eight demands called for "establishing unified national standards for public tree pruning, prohibiting unnecessary topping-style pruning, bare-crown pruning, and heavy pruning," registered on July 9 with 6,554 co-signers, jointly administered by the Ministry of Agriculture and the Ministry of Interior, requiring an official response by September 9, 2026[^57].

In the Ministry of Agriculture's preliminary response, one sentence revealed the crux of the matter: the ministry had already issued the 《Ornamental Tree Pruning Operations Guide》, "for agencies to reference and apply"[^57].

```tw-versus
Guide written in 2022 | What the 2026 petition demanded
Stub cutting "prohibited except where public safety requires it" | Same position against unnecessary topping, plus demand for unified national standards
Ministry of Agriculture positioned it as "for agencies to reference and apply"—no binding force | Demands evaluation by qualified professionals, public information for oversight
No penalties, no audit mechanism | Implicit demand for binding force and enforcement power
Source: Ministry of Agriculture Forestry and Natural Conservation Administration 《Ornamental Tree Pruning Operations Guide》, Public Policy Online Participation Platform proposal text and Ministry response
```

Both documents aimed to prohibit the same thing. This proposal did not seek to reinvent a rule; it sought to give teeth to a rule already written.

And "lack of uniformity" was not an implementation oversight—it was the design of the system itself. Article 38-3 of the final section of Chapter 5 of the Forest Law, "Tree Protection," explicitly states that "local governments may formulate implementation standards according to local conditions"[^58], and the Forestry Bureau's guide also authorized management units to set detailed regulations[^56]. Two documents at different levels both agreed that localities should each set their own.

In July 2026, this erupted in Taipei. Online rumors circulated that the city government had cut over four thousand street trees, with talk of "clearing a path for drones." The Parks Department's July 12 press release provided numbers: 3,164 trees removed last year, over eighty percent (2,630) due to natural death, decay, pests and disease, or natural disaster, plus over 13,225 new trees planted in fiscal years 114–115[^59]. Parks Director Lan Shufan's statement was: should we allow diseased trees to fall just because of malicious drone fantasies by ill-intentioned people; tree pruning is for public safety[^60].

The city government simultaneously said it would gather evidence against those spreading rumors. Democratic Progressive Party Taipei councilor Zhang Wenjie responded: "What a show of official authority," "Is Taipei City Government really going to sue citizens now! Are you telling everyone they can't supervise Taipei City Government anymore?"[^61] Mayor Chiang Wan-an was quoted verbatim in this controversy with only four characters: "Handle it according to law"[^62].

The tree-protection side has names, faces, and over a decade of action history; what the city government can point to is the Parks Director's two sentences plus the mayor's four characters. The distribution of microphones is itself part of this debate.

Sometimes a turning point comes quickly. That same month, the National Health Insurance Administration's Taipei Operations Group, having received public feedback about accessibility needs for people with disabilities and stroller users, announced plans to remove an old Alstonia scholaris tree on Qingdao West Road. Tree-protection action began on July 16, with permit applications for assembly and calls to 1999. Taiwan Tree People Association Secretary-General Pan Han-chiang later described it: "Around 5:30 p.m. that day we got good news—the NHI Administration quickly responded to tree-protection sentiment, corrected course, halted the project, and withdrew the original announcement." The NHI Administration's statement was that it recognized the old tree's value and temporarily canceled the original removal plan[^63].

The most unlikely voice of any faction in this same debate came from tree doctor Zhan Fengchun. She pushed the question back: rather than argue whether a tree should be felled, we should ask why it reached the point of needing removal. Repeated heavy pruning over time can cause branch decay, thick-branch breakage, even damage to the root system supporting the tree; what should really happen is improvement starting from planting space, pruning methods, and long-term care[^64][^65]. This framing can be used by tree advocates to criticize mismanagement, and by the city to explain why removal was unavoidable.

One allegation in the debate remained unresolved. In May 2020, the Taiwan Ecological Society, Kaohsiung Tree Lovers, Taiwan Tree People, and four other environmental groups jointly released an investigation alleging that thick branches pruned from street trees fetched 1,600 to 2,300 yuan per ton, calling on government to regulate them like driftwood and waste metals[^66]. This was a joint allegation by multiple organizations within the same camp; six years later, there was no official investigation result, nor any traceable follow-up.

One thing was absent from this debate: no named individual or group publicly argued "trees don't matter, they should give way to development or traffic." Voices supporting pruning and removal all used the same framework—the city government spoke of typhoon safety, Taiwan Power spoke of power supply safety, residents petitioned about sidewalks being heaved up, councilors spoke of holding management accountable for negligence. Even those requesting tree removal framed it as protecting people. The real divide between the two sides was not "do trees matter," but "whose safety judgment counts."

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The crown spread of that tree deity in Beitou exceeded the national threshold, had a plaque from the Civil Affairs Bureau, had the neighborhood chief speak up for it, and after the site inspection there was "absolutely no action whatsoever." Taiwan's tree pruning guide says "topping pruning prohibited except where public safety requires it," and the Ministry of Agriculture itself positioned it as "for agencies to reference and apply." These two things seem to belong to one folklore and one administrative law, but they are two faces of the same pathology: the rule exists, the status exists, the discourse exists—there is just no one responsible for making it happen. For over a hundred years, each generation has made decisions about trees; the one baton no one has truly caught is "keep caring for it after you plant it."

The path ahead is not unpaved. Environment Minister Peng Qiming's April 2026 target was for people to walk in tree shade from the MRT station all the way to the office, and the Environment Ministry planned cross-agency collaboration and cooperation with the Meteorological Agency to establish a street tree fall-risk warning system[^67]. National Cheng Kung University Architecture Professor Lin Tzyping discussed the relationship between artificial shade umbrellas and tree planting: artificial shade is a short-term response to care for citizens while trees are still growing, but in the long term, planting trees is the fundamental way to improve urban microclimate[^68].

Trees need at least ten years to grow enough to provide shade. This is the one adaptation on the [Taiwan Climate Crisis and Net-Zero Transition](/en/nature/taiwan-climate-change-net-zero-transition/) line that cannot wait.

In ten years we will know whether this baton was caught. But some batons have already been caught.

## Green Tunnels Grow Only on Roads That Weren't Widened

In Jiji, Nantou, the camphor trees lining Taiwan Route 16 were planted in 1940 and have grown into a green tunnel about 4.5 kilometers long. When you drive through it, light flickers past the windshield in regular intervals, and the temperature inside the car drops on its own—this is something you have to experience with your body passing through, not just by looking.

On Taiwan Route 3 in Gukeng, Yunlin, there are about two kilometers of old mango trees roughly fifty years old, already designated for protection. The mango tree green tunnel in Sinhua, Tainan originally had 541 trees; thanks to local opposition and tree-protection groups, over eighty percent were preserved[^69]. In Taitung, there is also a two-kilometer-long Alstonia scholaris shelter road.

These roads share a peculiar trait: they exist because certain things **did not** happen. No widening project passed through. No construction required drawing a centerline. No one said in some meeting, "We need to widen this road." They are shapes accumulated from a series of decisions that were never made.

The Jiji camphor trees planted in 1940 still use the core tree species established during the Japanese colonial period—the same species that was once a resource fought over by empires in the mountains (see [19th-Century Camphor Wars](/en/history/19th-century-camphor-wars/)), but on streets was only asked to provide shade. The old mango trees in Gukeng and Sinhua are remnants of the old practice of using fruit trees as street trees. They were not luckier than other trees; they simply happened to grow on road segments that no one had decided to remake.

The act of "catching" or "preserving" has two intensities. The stronger kind actively takes responsibility and sees it through: guidelines must have teeth, labor must keep pace, standards must be enforced—this has not yet happened overall. The weaker kind only requires that no one actively destroys it, and this has actually happened on a few roads.

"No one stepped forward to say we need to widen this road" is itself a decision, only it is so quiet that it will never be counted as an achievement, nor will it appear in any site inspection record. Its only evidence is the cool breeze when you roll down your car window.

One road in Changhua acknowledged the name of a single tree; the Jiji road acknowledged the height of an entire row of trees. Between them lie more than a hundred years, multiple rounds of changing rationales, a guideline written "absolutely prohibited" but only for reference, and over six thousand signatures still waiting for a response.

The next tree to be planted at your front door is right now being decided by someone deciding how wide the planting pit should be.
