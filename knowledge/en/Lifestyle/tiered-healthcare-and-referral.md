In 2024, the proportion of outpatient visits in Western medicine primary care was 92.01%, accounting for 64.09% of total claims; hospital departments fell to 35.91%.[^1]

Another set of figures from the same year shows an opposite trend: the primary care rate for hospitals at the regional hospital level or above was 18.37%, higher than the 17.21% recorded in 2022; medical centers also rose from 14.40% to 15.56%.[^1]

Primary care is getting stronger, yet hospitals are still treating minor illnesses. Both of these facts hold true simultaneously.

## Two Curves

First, look at primary care. The proportion of outpatient visits in Western medicine primary care was 89.49% in 2022, 91.79% in 2023, and 92.01% in 2024; the share of total claims rose from 61.50% to 63.99%, and then to 64.09%.[^1]

Meanwhile, the share of claims for hospital departments fell from 38.50% to 36.01%, and then to 35.91%.[^1]

```tw-bars
Proportion of Outpatient Visits and Share of Claims in Western Medicine (2024) (%)
Primary Care | Number of Visits | 92.01 | Increasing annually
Hospitals | Number of Visits | 62.20 | Duplicate counts occur if one person visits multiple levels
Primary Care | Share of Claims | 64.09 | 61.50% in 2022
Hospitals | Share of Claims | 35.91 | 38.50% in 2022
Source: NHIA "2024 National Health Insurance Supervisory Indicator Monitoring Report," Indicator 1.5
```

The denominator for the proportion of outpatient visits is the total number of Western medicine outpatient visits. If the same person visits both a clinic and a hospital, they are counted in both places; therefore, the sum of these two figures exceeds 100%. The share of claims does not have this issue, as it sums to 100%.[^1]

## But Hospitals Are Still Treating Minor Illnesses

Primary care refers to illnesses suitable for management at primary care clinics or regional hospitals, such as influenza, intestinal infections, acute bronchitis, skin and subcutaneous tissue infections, chondrocalcinosis, joint disorders, urinary tract infections, etc.[^1]

This classification stems from research by Professor Wu Hsiao-chi of Yang-Ming University in 2003, which was later revised with the assistance of family medicine and primary care specialists; it is not an internationally standardized definition.[^1]

The primary care rate for hospitals at the regional level or above has shown a slight upward trend over three years: 17.21%, 18.01%, and 18.37%. Looking at the breakdown for 2024, medical centers stood at 15.56%, regional hospitals at 20.18%, and district hospitals at 30.51%, with the overall hospital average at 21.57%.[^1]

```tw-line
Primary Care Rate for Hospitals at Regional Level or Above (%)
2022 | 17.21 | Medical Center: 14.40
2023 | 18.01 | Medical Center: 15.13
2024 | 18.37 | Medical Center: 15.56
Source: NHIA "2024 National Health Insurance Supervisory Indicator Monitoring Report," Indicator 1.4
```

The lower the hospital tier, the higher the primary care rate, which aligns with the system's design: district hospitals are intended to handle more general illnesses. The real concern is medical centers—NHIA committee members have long focused on the phenomenon of "bloated outpatient departments" or medical centers "treating minor illnesses."[^1]

## Why Is This Happening?

The National Health Insurance Administration (NHIA) attributes the rise since 2020 to the pandemic, suggesting that patients may have visited hospitals because they could not determine for themselves whether they were infected, thereby increasing the primary care rate at hospitals of regional level or above.[^1]

Another clue is the reduction in outpatient volume. Based on the share of claims within the scope of "outpatient volume reduction" implemented in 2017, the first quarter of 2025 was 57.4%, a decrease of 0.6 percentage points from 58% in the same period last year; the second quarter was 57.8%, also a decrease of 0.3 percentage points from 58.1% in the same period last year.[^1]

Under this metric, the share of claims only decreased by 0.3 to 0.6 percentage points within a year.

## Policy Tools: Alliances, Referral Points, and Co-management

The NHIA's improvement strategies include the "Everyone's Doctor" program, strategic alliances, referral points, and co-management pilots. Below, we discuss the latter three.[^1]

First is vertical integration. As of the end of June 2025, 81 strategic alliances have been formed across Taiwan, involving 6,844 contracted medical institutions. This includes 28 medical centers, 78 regional hospitals, 282 district hospitals, 6,305 primary care clinics, 141 home care agencies, 6 psychiatric rehabilitation institutions, and 4 respiratory care centers.[^1]

Second is referral incentives. Starting September 1, 2025, the payment points for downward referrals (referring a patient to a lower tier) and receiving downward referrals will be increased. Each downward referral will increase by 200 points, reaching 700 points; each received downward referral will increase by 300 points, reaching 500 points.[^1]

Third is the budgetary tool. The "Pilot Program for Individual Hospital Prospective Budgeting and Zonal Co-management" guides hospitals to adjust their ratio of inpatient to outpatient services within a certain total budget cap and uses strategic alliances to refer minor illness or stable chronic patients downward.[^1]

## How to Read These Two Curves

If you only look at the proportion of primary care, you will conclude that "tiered medical care is a success." If you only look at the primary care rate of medical centers, you will conclude that "hospitals are still treating minor illnesses."[^1]

A more honest reading is this: the center of gravity in terms of volume is indeed shifting toward primary care—the rise in the share of claims from 61.50% to 64.09% represents a substantive change. However, minor illnesses have not truly disappeared from large hospitals; instead, the primary care rate at medical centers has actually risen slightly.

The report lists both curves simultaneously, noting that the analysis of the primary care rate is "worth noting" and pointing out that the relationship between the rising proportion of primary care and this slight increase in the primary care rate "warrants further investigation."[^1]

The next point of observation will be whether the adjustment of referral points and the co-management pilot in September 2025 can turn the medical center curve from an upward trend to a flat one. Until then, the figures 92.01% and 18.37% will coexist.
