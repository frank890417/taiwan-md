> **30-Second Overview:** Since the 1950s, "Wāng-á-biāo" (尪仔標) has been a game for children in Taiwan and a yardstick for peer status—the more you collected, the more weight you carried on the playground after class. These cards, rooted in Chinese _yèzi xì_ (leaf games) and inspired by Japanese children's toys, evolved from metal to plastic, with designs ranging from glove puppetry to manga, carrying the collective memory of generations of Taiwanese people. Most uniquely, in Taiwan, even election flyers were repurposed by children into "Wāng-á-biāo," becoming alternative weapons on the childhood battlefield.

In 1950s Taiwan, whenever elections arrived, streets and alleys would be piled high with flyers bearing the portraits of candidates. These political advertisements, viewed as adult matters, found an unexpected second life in the hands of children—they were cleverly folded and cut to become the "Wāng-á-biāo" that children scrambled to collect[^1]. These cards, printed with political figures, became the protagonists of intense "battles" on school grounds after class. This phenomenon of transforming serious politics into childhood play is a unique cultural landscape in Taiwan, allowing Wāng-á-biāo to carry an even richer layer of social memory.

### The Diverse Faces of Wāng-á-biāo: Names, Origins, and Material Evolution

The term "Wāng-á-biāo" originates from Taiwanese Hokkien (_Tâi-gí_), where _wāng-á_ refers to an "image" or "character figure," and _biāo_ denotes a label or card[^2]. However, this toy has many regional aliases across Taiwan, such as _pián-pián_, _phian-phian_, _tàu-phiàn_, _iáng-phiàn_, or _ōe-phiàn_[^3]. In places like Keelung and Chiayi, it is called _āng-á-sián_ or _āng-á_; people in Taoyuan call it _bià-kong_; while in Taichung, it is known as _kiáu-kiáu_. These diverse names reflect the linguistic characteristics and cultural contexts of different regions in Taiwan.

Tracing the history of Wāng-á-biāo, its roots can be found in the _mǎdiào pái_ (horse cards) and _yèzi xì_ (leaf games) of China's Ming Dynasty[^2]. In modern times, it is closely linked to the "small picture cards" included in Western cigarette packs in the late 19th century; these exquisite cards were called _xiāngyān páizi_ (cigarette brands) in Shanghai and evolved into _yánghuà_ (Western-style pictures)[^4]. During the Japanese colonial period (1895–1945), the Japanese game _menko_ was introduced to Taiwan. Its gameplay—flipping cards by striking them against the ground—had a profound influence on the development of Wāng-á-biāo[^5].

Early Taiwanese Wāng-á-biāo were handmade using materials like sugarcane paper or horse manure paper, with patterns pasted on before being cut; this was the primary form before the 1950s[^3]. As times progressed, around the 1960s, the rise of the plastic industry brought a change in materials, moving from celluloid (an early plastic) to cardboard, and eventually, plastic Wāng-á-biāo became widespread and mainstream[^3].

### Epochal Patterns and Clever Back Designs

The patterns on Wāng-á-biāo are rich and diverse, serving as the best medium for reflecting contemporary trends. In addition to glove puppetry figures (such as Shǐ Yàn-wén and Hǎ Mǎi Èr-chǐ), manga characters, and historical heroes, common themes include the twelve zodiac animals, animal series, legendary generals (such as Yue Fei, Zhang Fei, and Cheng Yaojin), and popular television characters of the time[^3][^6]. Among these, the _Guāiguāi_ (乖乖) manga character series is a shared memory for many. The exquisite quality, rarity, and popularity of these patterns often served as the basis for children to judge the value of their cards[^3].

Beyond the front patterns, the back designs of Wāng-á-biāo were also full of ingenuity. Some featured school timetables, allegedly so parents would be more accepting of children bringing them home. Other series featured numbers and suits from Chinese chess, playing cards, or mahjong, allowing them to function as gambling tools—a fact that occasionally sparked moral debates in schools[^2].

### Versatile Gameplay: Childhood Tactics from Fanning to Striking

The ways to play Wāng-á-biāo are highly varied; its strong competitive and collectible nature made it especially popular among boys[^3]. Common methods include:

- **Fanning (_shān pái_)**: Players stack Wāng-á-biāo face down on the ground and take turns fanning them with their palms. If a player can fan an opponent's card so that it flips face up, they win that card[^2]. This method tests hand strength and skill and is a classic image in many people's childhood memories.
- **Flicking (_tán pái_)**: Each player holds one card and takes turns flicking them with their fingers. If a player can land their card on top of an opponent's, they win the opponent's card[^7]. This is a more direct form of combat where victory is often decided in an instant.
- **Striking (_jí pái_)**: This is divided into "scattering" (_jí sàn_) and "flipping" (_jí fān_). In scattering, several cards are stacked, and players take turns striking the middle of the stack with a single card; any individual cards that scatter belong to the striker[^2]. In flipping, players take turns striking an opponent's card to make it flip over to win[^2].
- **Revealing (_xiān pái_ / _rēn tǒng_)**: One person acts as the dealer, shuffles the cards into several stacks, and after players place bets, the winner is determined by comparing numbers[^2].
- **Comparing Suits/Sizes**: Players show their cards simultaneously, determining victory based on pattern content (such as official ranks or martial prowess), "Rock-Paper-Scissors" style patterns, or directly by card numbers/suits. Sometimes, players even had to "debate" historical stories or plots to settle a win[^2].
- **King Card Insertion**: Players stack an equal number of cards and insert a pre-designated "King Card," taking turns striking or pressing the stack; the rules for this are more complex[^8].

To gain an advantage, children even developed various "processing" techniques, such as using Liquid Paper to thicken a card to increase its weight, or using a lighter to slightly singe and curve the card to make it easier to fan or flick[^3]. More common were sticking two cards together to increase thickness, rubbing them on the ground to deform the edges, or even burning the edges to make the card curl upward[^9]. These "clever tricks" were seen as displays of wisdom and tactics in the children's world of that era, though they often sparked disputes among peers regarding "cheating" and "fairness."

### Cultural Significance and Greater China Connections

In an era before video games became widespread, Wāng-á-biāo, along with marbles and bamboo spears, formed a rich gaming world for Taiwanese children[^3]. Owning more Wāng-á-biāo often meant having higher status and prestige among peers[^10]. It also invisibly cultivated strategic thinking, hand-eye coordination, and interpersonal skills. Furthermore, by comparing characters to stories, Wāng-á-biāo became a medium for "Chinese cultural enlightenment," introducing children to historical and episodic novels.

It is worth noting that Wāng-á-biāo has circulated throughout the Greater China region (including mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau, and Singapore) and is not exclusive to Taiwan. In Taiwan, they are primarily circular with a diameter of about 4–5 cm and wavy or serrated edges; in Hong Kong, they are mostly rectangular thick cardboard pieces of about 2 cm[^2]. In recent years, a similar game using cigarette pack covers has emerged among primary school students in mainland China, which has also drawn attention from educational authorities.

Today, although Wāng-á-biāo has gradually faded from the mainstream toy market, it still holds an irreplaceable place in the hearts of many who were in fifth or sixth grade during the 1950s–1970s[^5][^10]. Many collectors still cherish their yellowed cards, using these small pieces of paper to reminisce about their innocent childhoods and even tell stories to younger generations[^5]. Wāng-á-biāo is one of the cultural symbols of Taiwan's social development, recording the shared growth memories of a generation.

### Conclusion

From the _yèzi xì_ of the Ming Dynasty to the _menko_ of the Japanese colonial period, and finally to the localized "Wāng-á-biāo" in Taiwan, this toy has undergone hundreds of years of evolution. In an era before video games, it was social currency on the playground and a carrier of cultural inheritance, recording how Taiwan's language, industry, and pop culture intersected on these tiny slips of paper.
