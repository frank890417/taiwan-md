**30-Second Overview: In 1986, Ren Jiang-da, a Korean-Japanese mixed-heritage youth, borrowed NT$300,000 to take over a failing record shop and began importing "music no one wanted to hear" into Taiwan. Four decades later, the ecosystem that grew from that shop (live houses, independent labels, StreetVoice, the Golden Indie Music Awards) sent a band that sold records in a coffee shop to win Album of the Year at the Golden Melody Awards, and sent an all-English-singing Taipei band to Coachella. Forty years of Taiwan independent music is a relay race with no finish line: every leg runs gasping, every leg feels like it's about to collapse, but the baton never drops.**

## The Dream Bought for Three Hundred Thousand

1986, Taipei, one year before the lifting of martial law. Ren Jiang-da, a Korean-Japanese mixed-heritage young man who grew up across from the Keelung fish market, borrowed NT$300,000 from a friend to take over a small record shop about to close: Crystal Records. He had no business plan, no market analysis — only a group of friends complaining they "couldn't buy foreign records," and an impulse he later summed up in three phrases: "You want to do it, go do it, you'll make it happen." (From _Mirror Media_ 2017 interview)[^1]

This decision looked unremarkable at the time. Taiwan's record market was monopolized by major labels like Rock Records and UFO Records; the concept of "independent" didn't exist in the pop music industry, and young people oscillated between Western charts and Mandarin love ballads.

But Ren did a few things no one else did.

In 1987, he founded _Rock Magazine_ monthly, introducing the concept of underground music to Taiwan. That same year he launched the "Taipei New Music Festival," running it for four editions with crossover lineups including Huang Yun-ling, Tat Ming Pair, and Chao Chuan. In 1991 he released _Voices from the Bottom of Taiwan_ and the _Taiwan Audio Archive_ series, using field recordings to collect Indigenous, _nakasi_, _beiguan_, _koa-á-hì_, and night market hawker calls. In an era when everyone chased Western rock, he bent down to listen to the frequencies of the land beneath his feet.[^2]

> **✦** "I have the desire to let the few, the unseen things be seen." — Ren Jiang-da (from _Mirror Media_ 2017 interview)

Crystal Records became an "alternative cradle," a "springboard for singers." An incomplete list says it all: Wu Bai, Chen Ming-chang, Blacklist Studio, Lei Guang-xia, Chu Yueh-hsin (Pighead Skin), Kinmen King and Li Bing-hui, Turbid Creek Commune, Sweetme, 1976, Chthonic. These names later wrote different chapters of Taiwan pop music history, but almost every album Crystal released lost money. Former Eslite Dunhua Music Hall director Wu Wu-chang's assessment hit the nail on the head: "Crystal was full of passion and ideals, but lacked professional financial management, so it could only become a legend."

In 1993, the company faced an operational crisis; production planner He Dong-hong was in debt for several million NT dollars. Employees went long periods without pay, but almost no one left. Former Crystal employee and singer A-De the Rogue recalled: "All employees were betting on the company with our hearts, wanting to prop it up, very hard but not much complaint."

In 1995, music director He Ying-yi launched the "Crystal Ten-Thousand-Person Support Association," calling for each person to contribute NT$4,000 to purchase equivalent music products, sparking enthusiastic response from political and arts circles — probably the earliest crowdfunding in Taiwan independent music history. But the debt black hole was too deep. On top of that, Ren's second daughter contracted a rare neuroblastoma; treatment costs in the US started at US$300,000. "Did everything that could be done, borrowed money that shouldn't have been borrowed." He turned to loan sharks, interest compounding, and finally couldn't hold on. Medical expenses crushed a record company, and crushed a father. In 1994, his daughter passed away in Taiwan.

Yet in 1998, the Golden Melody Awards unprecedentedly awarded the "Special Contribution Award" to 42-year-old Ren Jiang-da, the youngest recipient in history. Juror Li Chien-fu said: "All mainstream comes from the non-mainstream; he was willing to send charcoal in the snow to non-mainstream creators, and was destined to lose money on almost every record — of course that deserves encouragement." (From _Mirror Media_ 2017 interview)

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Crystal Records' contradiction is the prototype contradiction of Taiwan independent music: the purest idealists are often the worst businesspeople. Ren opened a door for Taiwan, but got caught in the door himself. Crystal closed in 2006 and entered history. But the story didn't end there: in 2021, He Ying-yi announced all Crystal recording materials would be made public domain, the declaration titled "Now We Return Ourselves to Everyone." A record company that couldn't pay salaries ultimately gave all its music to the world for free.[^8]

## The Post-Martial Law Sound Explosion

July 15, 1987, Taiwan lifted 38 years of martial law. For music, this meant one concrete thing: you could finally sing what you wanted, out loud.

Pre-martial-law Taiwan's music environment was closed. Records required censorship review; lyrics couldn't touch politics; even "decadent music" could be banned. Lifting martial law opened the floodgates, and a whole generation's suppressed creative energy burst forth. In 1989, Blacklist Studio released _Crazy Song_. This all-Taigi album used hip-hop rhythms and political satire to shock the entire industry, proving pop music could be both catchy and dangerous. That same year, Affiliated Senior High School students Cai Hai-en, Zhang Ming-chang, and Ying Wei-min (Little Ying) formed a band later called "Turbid Creek Commune." They mixed punk, noise, _nakasi_, and Taigi, documenting working-class life in the rawest way. Their 1995 album _How's Your Anus?_ — the title alone made mainstream media retreat — but underground fans revere it as a classic.

These sounds shared a common trait: they borrowed Western rock forms and filled them with Taiwan's own content. Turbid Creek Commune cursed politicians in Taigi; Chthonic sang the 228 Incident in black metal; Lin Sheng-xiang sang the Meinong anti-dam movement in Hakka. Language itself became a manifesto. After Mandarin monopolized pop music for forty years, singing in Taigi, Hakka, or Indigenous languages was itself political.

Lifting martial law also catalyzed Taiwan's earliest outdoor music festivals. In 1995, two foreigners Jami Marsh and Wade Davis held "Spring Scream" in Kenting — Taiwan's first outdoor rock festival born on a beach. That same year, Yetao Festival launched in Taipei, establishing a multi-stage operation model and beginning to invite international acts like Suede and Moby. These Taiwan festival cultures provided independent bands another growth space beyond live houses — but that's another article's story.

The 1990s Taipei underground scene concentrated at a few geographic nodes: Ximending record shops were intelligence exchanges, the Shida Road area was the agglomeration core, and Gongguan-area cafés were where bands crashed. This was the natural result of poor students and poor musicians gathering — no one designed any "cultural park": rent was cheap there because universities were nearby, because alleys could hide a basement. A veteran fan recalling the Crystal era put it well: "In the pre-internet era, Crystal was like a secret underground society, opening a door to another world for us." (From _Mirror Media_ 2017 interview)

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Post-martial-law Taiwan independent music had a special duality: it was simultaneously "learning outward" and "digging inward." Bands learned Western rock forms (punk, metal, post-rock), but filled them with Taiwan's own stories (White Terror, ethnic identity, class oppression, urban alienation). This "foreign form + local content" hybrid gave Taiwan independent music its own DNA from day one.

## The Revolutionary Base Registered as a "Beverage Shop"

If Crystal Records was independent music's delivery room, live houses were where it learned to walk. And the absurd predicament of Taiwan live houses can be told in one detail: "The Underground," opened in 1996 in a Shida Road basement, registered its business category with the Taipei City Commerce Bureau as "cold/hot beverages, juice, coffee, tobacco and alcohol."

Taiwan law simply has no "live house" category; owners fundamentally cannot register as music venues.

The Underground was co-founded by He Dong-hong and Chen Shu-zhen. He Dong-hong (yes, the same He who owed millions at Crystal Records) left the record industry and chose another way to keep doing what he believed in. This basement fitting only 80–100 people became the heart of Taiwan independent music for the next seventeen years: 1976, Quarterback, Nuomi Tuan, Wonfu, Cosmos People, 831 all played their first, tenth, hundredth shows here.

Same period, Witch Shop incubated Cheer Chen and Chang Hsien (Deserts Chang) in a fifty-person space draped in women's underwear. This venue, opened in 1996 beside the Shida Night Market, didn't restrict genre — folk to heavy metal all had a stage — and with its feminist spirit and zero-threshold openness, defined an entire generation's "wenqing" imagination. Riverside Messages (1995) became a cradle for folk singer-songwriters, providing a relatively quiet, literary performance environment. The Wall, opened in 2003, established the standard model for international independent bands touring Taiwan with its four-hundred-person capacity and professional hardware. The south had its own bases too. Kaohsiung's Kafka by the Sea combined the harbor city's ocean culture and industrial background, becoming a key base for the southern Taiwan independent scene.

Each venue had its personality: Witch Shop was a living room, Riverside Messages a study, The Wall a training hall, The Underground a basement. But together they supported a complete "growing up" path: newcomers started in fifty-person spaces, honed to hundred-person, four-hundred-person, then Legacy's thousand-person scale. Without this path, even the best bands could only stay in place.

However in 2011, the Taichung ALA nightclub fire took nine lives, and county/city governments immediately launched strict inspections on all performance spaces. The problem: regulations lumped live houses together with dance halls and nightclubs — same fire standards, same land-use zoning restrictions — but provided no alternative legal operating path. The Underground, Witch Shop, The Wall received fines in succession. The Underground went dark once in 2012, attempted to amend the _Cultural and Creative Industries Development Act_ through legislator negotiation to give live houses more reasonable regulatory classification. Reopened in August, received two NT$60,000 fines in October: one "violating public safety," one "violating building use classification."[^3]

Mayday bassist Masa said at a support press conference a sentence that condensed the whole industry's helplessness: "As long as there's one road to survive, to walk, don't let those who want to operate have nowhere to turn." Mayday guitarist Monster, 1976, Kao Chiu-chin, Turbid Creek Commune's Little Ying and many other musicians stood up in support. This may have been the moment in Taiwan independent music history when "underground" and "mainstream" were most united.

June 15, 2013, The Underground closed for the second and final time. The official blog farewell was calm and bitter: "The government has taken no action on Live House regulations; the Shida Sanli Self-Help Association continues to apply pressure, viewing us as a source of social chaos. Let us cherish all the memories we created together, thank you all!"

After The Underground closed, noted electronic music creator fish.the launched the "Let's Make a Underground Compilation!" project on Facebook, gathering 40 songs in under a month and a half, paying tribute to this basement as a free online compilation. A venue disappeared, but the people and memories it nurtured became music, continuing to circulate.

One basement's closure exposed the state's systemic indifference to music cultural spaces — a problem still not fully resolved today.

> **🔢 Key Data**
> Taiwan live house regulations only gained preliminary legal recognition after the 2015 amendment to the _Cultural and Creative Industries Development Act_. Before that, not a single live house in Taiwan could obtain fully legal business registration. During The Underground's 17 years from opening to closing, Taiwan independent music went from underground to the Golden Melody Awards stage, but the space carrying all this was, legally speaking, always an illegal structure.

## From "Underground" to "Independent": A Word's Revolution

Around 2000, a quiet language revolution occurred in Taiwan's music scene. The term "underground band" began to be replaced by "independent music."

These two words carry completely different weight. "Underground" implies anti-mainstream, anti-commercial, hiding in unseen places; "independent" emphasizes autonomy: you can be seen, can make money, as long as creative control stays in your own hands.

The key figure driving this shift was Chthonic frontman Lin Chang-tso (Freddy). He argued bands shouldn't stay trapped in basements forever, should proactively move above ground, record themselves, organize their own events, build their own audiences. _Blow Music_ wrote in reviewing this history that Freddy "was one of the representative figures in early Taiwan advocating bands should walk out of the 'underground' and become independent bands." Chthonic itself was the best demonstration: a band using Taiwan historical mythology as material, fusing _erhu_ and _suona_ with black metal, signing with international label Spinefarm Records, playing Europe's Download Festival, proving "independent" and "international" aren't antonyms. Lin Chang-tso later even won a legislative seat, becoming the first person in Taiwan music history to go from mosh pit to parliament hall.[^5]

Scholar He Dong-hong, researching Crystal Records, pointed out a fundamental difference between Taiwan's "independent" concept and the Anglo-American one: in the Anglo-American world, indie is small labels declaring war on big companies; in Taiwan, labels of various scales originally coexisted. Rock Records is both Asia's largest "independent" label and collaborates with multinationals. "Independent" in Taiwan is more an aesthetic attitude and DIY practice, not an industrial structural confrontation.

This explains a phenomenon outsiders often find puzzling. [Mayday](/en/music/mayday-band/) formed at Affiliated Senior High in 1997, built popularity in live houses early on, released debut album _First Creative Album_ in 1999. Then they signed a major label, tours grew larger, filled New York's Madison Square Garden (tickets sold out in 48 hours), CNN called them "the Chinese Beatles." But many Taiwan people still call them "an independent band by origin."

In Taiwan's context, "independent" refers to how you started, not how big you are now. This definitional vagueness is both Taiwan independent music's weakness (lack of clear industrial boundaries) and its secret weapon: it lets "independent" boundaries disappear, becoming a field accommodating all kinds of voices. From Turbid Creek Commune's noise punk to Cheer Chen's xiao qing xin, from Lin Qiang's electronic experiments to EggPlantEgg's Taigi rock, all find a place in this field. Mayday's commercial success didn't "betray" the independent spirit; on the contrary, it proved to later bands one thing: you can go from basement to stadium, and you don't need to apologize for it.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Taiwan's definition of "independent" is vague perhaps precisely because this island is too small. Small enough that everyone knows everyone; small enough that the distance between "mainstream" and "underground" is just one street corner: in New York, you can live your whole life in Brooklyn's independent scene without touching mainstream; in Taipei, you watch a post-rock show at The Wall, walk five minutes and you're at Xinyi District's department stores. Distance's disappearance makes confrontation meaningless; cooperation becomes survival strategy instead.

## StreetVoice, Label Archipelagos, and the Golden Indie Music Awards

In 2006, StreetVoice launched.[^10] This platform letting musicians upload works for free did something Crystal Records couldn't: lowered the publishing threshold to zero.

In Crystal's era, a band needed to be spotted by a record company, enter a studio, press CDs, get distributed to record shops to be heard. After StreetVoice, you only needed a microphone and a computer.

The global music industry was all undergoing the same digital revolution — Taiwan's situation was just one corner. Around 2000, download software like Napster and Kazaa crashed physical CD sales; record companies folded or merged. But for independent music, digitization was liberation: production threshold dropped from million-NT studios to a laptop; distribution threshold dropped from record shop shelves to a link on the internet.

StreetVoice's specialty was playing both platform and curator: regularly hosting events, discovering new talent, connecting labels and musicians, running deep music media like Blow Music. Functionally more like a community activity center. StreetVoice later extended services to China and Hong Kong, becoming one of the most important digital hubs for Sinophone independent music. Taiwan independent music evolved from physical-space agglomerations (live house districts) into digital agglomerations, and StreetVoice was that central station.

Same period, independent labels began specializing and deepening, each like a small island cultivating its own sound ecology.

Wind Music (founded 1998) defined the sound of Taiwan's "wenqing aesthetic." Deserts Chang, Cheer Chen, Waa Wei all started here; its insistence on refined production and fresh style influenced a whole generation's imagination of "independent music." White Rabbit Records deepened experimental music and post-rock, becoming a key pusher of Taiwan noise and avant-garde sound. Kakuto Music, founded by Zhang Si-shi-san, used Taigi and mother-tongue rock to record the island's soundscape. Indigenous singers Chen Chien-nien and Chi Hsiao-chun all found platforms to voice at Kakuto. Yen Records bet on Dwagie and Leo Wang when hip-hop wasn't mainstream yet; later Leo Wang won 2019 Golden Melody Best Mandarin Male Singer, a milestone for hip-hop entering the mainstream hall.

These labels connected into archipelagos through Taiwan festival culture and digital platforms. Megaport Festival, Yetao Festival, Ocean Music Festival became these small islands' United Nations, letting different sounds collide on the same grass. _Blow Music_ articles listed Taiwan's early important independent labels: "Crystal Records, Kakuto Records, to currently active Dianxuan, White Rabbit, Wind Music, Yen Records, re:public, etc., also the behind-the-scenes pushers who tirelessly and quietly hold up a sky for Taiwan's independent music scene."

In 2010, the Ministry of Culture established the "Golden Indie Music Awards" — Taiwan's first national-level award designed specifically for non-mainstream music.[^9] The Golden Melody Awards are everyone's arena; the Golden Indie Music Awards are independent music's home field. Its establishment marked the state formally acknowledging: voices outside the mainstream also deserve to be seen, rewarded, recorded.

From Crystal Records' one-person company to StreetVoice's digital platform, from The Underground's eighty-person basement to the Golden Indie Music Awards ceremony, Taiwan independent music spent twenty-five years going from the law not recognizing its existence to the state giving it awards. This road wasn't a straight line; in between were folded labels, closed venues, career-changing musicians. But the ecosystem survived, and grew denser.

## The Sound Spectrum: From Noise Punk to Xiao Qing Xin

Taiwan independent music's most underestimated characteristic is the astonishing stylistic diversity grown on a small island.

At the metal end, Chthonic and Flesh Juicer represent two completely different heavy paths. Chthonic set Taiwan historical mythology to symphonic black metal; Flesh Juicer fused temple culture, _Ba Jia Jiang_ face paint, and industrial metal — live shows like a heretical ritual. The Chairman used Taigi rock to scream social issues, carrying a grassroots anger and humor.

At the post-rock end, Sweetme is the unsurpassable pioneer. This band, active since the Crystal Records era, used pure instrumental music to construct massive emotional narratives, influencing a whole generation of Taiwan post-rock bands. Aphasia's sound is more introverted, deeper, like mountains in fog. Cicada brought classical chamber music delicacy into the post-rock framework.

The folk line runs longer. Its roots lie in the 1970s campus folk movement, but independent-era folk and campus folk are no longer the same thing. Hu De-fu is the upstream of that river, modern folk movement pioneer, spiritual totem of Indigenous music. Lin Sheng-xiang grafted Hakka _bayin_ and rock together, using music to speak for Meinong's countryside. Deserts Chang (Chang Hsien) became the 2000s "wenqing generation"代名词 with one guitar and a hoarse voice. Crowd Lu's fresh style brought folk into younger audience groups.

And the "xiao qing xin" (Little Fresh) label (the aesthetic on Cheer Chen, Sodagreen, Deserts Chang, etc.) was later exported to China, becoming Chinese listeners' strongest impression of Taiwan independent music. _Taiwan Insight_ articles note "xiao qing xin" has evolved from a music style into an aesthetic and lifestyle label, its influence surpassing music itself. An interesting paradox: Taiwan independent music's "gentlest" face is its most internationally transmissible.[^4]

Electronic music's thread is often overlooked in independent music narratives, but its influence is everywhere. Lin Qiang transitioned from 1990s pop singer to electronic music pioneer. This transition itself is a condensation of Taiwan music history. He went from Taigi pop giant "Walk Forward" to DJing in Berlin underground clubs. Later he scored films for Hou Hsiao-hsien and Jia Zhangke, using electronic sound to redefine what "Taiwan's sound" could be. DJ Mykal, Sonia Calico and others built their own electronic scenes in Taipei. And recent years' Organik Festival held on a secret island proved Taiwan's electronic music community can create international-grade experiences.

> **🔢 Key Data**
> Since 2010, the Golden Indie Music Awards have run for over fifteen years, covering rock, electronic, hip-hop, folk, jazz and more categories. From the 7th edition (2016) where Turbid Creek Commune won Best Album, Best Band, and Jury Award triple crown, to recent years where Kivi, Bad Special and other cross-language cross-genre new-generation creators won, the Golden Indie Music Awards winners list itself is an annual snapshot of Taiwan independent music's diversity.

## No Party for Cao Dong's Coffee Shop and Sunset Rollercoaster's Coachella

In 2016, a band called "No Party for Cao Dong" released debut album _The Ugly Slave_. They didn't use traditional distribution channels, didn't stock record shops, didn't sign a major label — only consigned at coffee shops and independent bookstores. Initial press of two thousand copies couldn't meet demand. Every show sold out in seconds. In 2017, they took three major Golden Melody Awards: Best Band, Best New Artist, Song of the Year. That last award meant an independent band's song was considered the best song in all Taiwan that year. In 2024, second album _Waa_ won Album of the Year and Best Mandarin Album again.[^6]

No Party for Cao Dong's success method is itself a parable. They had no management company, no marketing budget, early MVs even self-shot by band members. They proved in the streaming era a band can completely bypass the traditional industry chain, using the most primitive way (good music plus word of mouth) to conquer an entire island. _Taiwan Insight_ analysis noted No Party for Cao Dong's success "not only demonstrates alternative marketing means but also blurs the boundary between the two ideological categories of independent and mainstream." They simultaneously won Golden Indie Music Awards and Golden Melody Awards, breaking the long-standing imagined boundary of "independent vs mainstream" between the two awards.

Their lyrics are gray, sharp, precisely piercing a whole generation of urban youth's structural anxiety: low wages, high housing prices, generational predicament with no visible future. That line in "Mountains and Seas" — "He understands, he understands, nothing can go back" — became a generational code. Because it finally sang out the frustration no one dared voice in four minutes. 2023's second album _Waa_ continued the same spiritual density, swept the Golden Melody Awards again, proving No Party for Cao Dong's sound is a persistent need: this island's young people need someone to sing their pain.

And while No Party for Cao Dong tore open the island from inside, Sunset Rollercoaster was walking outward.

This band, formed in Taipei in 2009, made a counterintuitive choice: all-English lyrics. Frontman Kuo Kuo's explanation carries a poetic calculation: English lets them "express indirectly," letting listeners discover meaning themselves. The band name came from a Photo Booth photo background on MySpace (roller coaster and sunset). This nonsensical origin perfectly mirrors their musical temperament: not too serious, but serious to the bone.

Sunset Rollercoaster's music fuses city pop, disco, funk, and psychedelic rock; vintage synth sounds carry a subtropical lazy romance unique to Taiwan. After releasing debut _Bossa Nova_ in 2011 they once disbanded. Reformed in 2015, 2016 EP _JINJI KIKKO_ critically acclaimed. Their timeline is an almost perfect upward curve: 2011 Japan Summer Sonic, 2017 NYC Central Park SummerStage, 2018 first Taiwan band to record an Audiotree Live session, 2020 album _Soft Storm_ ranked #4 Asian Album of the Year by NME (partly recorded in LA, collaborating with US legendary musician Ned Doheny). 2023, they stood on the Coachella stage. 2024, collaborative album _AAA_ with Korean band Hyukoh received seven nominations at the 36th Golden Melody Awards.[^7]

A band from Taipei, singing English, playing city pop and psychedelic rock, used a globalized language to tell a story that could only grow in Taiwan.

On the same road, breakthroughs in different directions happened simultaneously. Elephant Gym from Kaohsiung, with math rock's precise structures (irregular time signatures, complex counterpoint, pure instrumental narrative), became Asia's math rock representatives. They're regularly invited to Euro-American festivals. Fire EX. used Taigi punk to sing the Sunflower Movement's sound. "Island's Sunlight" became the unofficial theme song at the 2014 Legislative Yuan occupation site; music and social movement linkage had never been this direct. Kivi is the most unexpected combination: Amis, Taigi, Japanese mixed into rock and folk, two-person lineup with orchestral fullness, won 33rd Golden Melody Best New Artist.

Hip-hop and R&B's rise then broke independent music's long rock-centered landscape: 9m88 shone with jazz and R&B, won 2020 Golden Indie Music Awards Best New Artist. Bad Special gave up med school for music, won 2021 Golden Melody Best New Artist. Dwagie pioneered jazz hip-hop layout long before hip-hop went mainstream; his independent release model built at Yen Records proved rap could also walk the independent path. Miso Group calls themselves "Cyber Taike" with self-made electronic instruments, mixing electronic, temple fair, _Ba Jia Jiang_ into rock, creating a strange species that could only grow in Taiwan.

Taiwan independent music's internationalization is multiple sounds being heard by the world simultaneously, and behind each sound stands a person who chose the hardest road.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The question Taiwan independent music gets asked most internationally is: "Do you sing in Chinese or English?" The answer is "Both, and also Taigi, Hakka, Amis, Japanese." This linguistic diversity is the natural expression of this island's own multicultural genes — no one deliberately designed it. Kivi's Amis rock, Lin Sheng-xiang's Hakka folk, Fire EX.'s Taigi punk, Sunset Rollercoaster's English city pop. Behind every language choice is an answer to "who am I."

> **✦** "The success of this band implies not only the alternative means of marketing music but also the crossover between two ideological categories." — Chen-yu Lin on No Party for Cao Dong, _Taiwan Insight_, 2018

## The Unfinished Story

In 2018, _Taiwan Insight_ academic article called Taipei "the world's newest independent music capital." This title sounds glorious, but the cracks underneath never disappeared.

Market too small is a structural problem. Taiwan's 23 million people — how many full-time independent musicians can it feed? A four-person band earning NT$20,000 per show, playing four shows a month, minus venue fees, transport, gear maintenance, each person's take barely covers rent. The vast majority of independent musicians have another job (teaching guitar, design, coding); music is passion, not profession.

Streaming platforms zeroed the distribution threshold but also drove album income near zero. A song played 1,000 times on Spotify earns the creator roughly one bubble tea's worth. Live house legal status has improved somewhat since The Underground's era, but mid-sized venues are still severely lacking: between The Wall's four hundred and Legacy's thousand, the middle rung for bands to "grow up" is missing. Kaohsiung Music Center (opened 2021) and Taipei Music Center filled the large-venue gap, but independent music needs more small spaces on every street corner — and those spaces are constantly being swallowed by rising rents and urban renewal.

Talent drain is another hidden pain. Excellent musicians get poached by major labels, or simply move to China's larger market. Those who stay face a contradiction: market too small to feed you, but leave and you can't find Taiwan's creative ecology density.

And the most fundamental question: as independent music gets more mainstream, how much meaning does "independent" retain? _Blow Music_ wrote in a 2016 feature that Taiwan has at least five different readings of "independent": "independent" as non-major-label release, "independent" as encouraging DIY spirit, "independent" as revering critical underground spirit, "independent" as local band circle development trajectory, "independent" as inheriting Western music history. These definitions sometimes contradict each other. Perhaps, as that article's conclusion said: "Define your own independent."

The digital era also fundamentally changed independent music's survival mode. During COVID-19, live houses forced to pause, livestreaming became substitute. Streaming platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, KKBOX) made Taiwan music easier than ever for global listeners to discover, but per-play revenue is dismally thin. Musicians must rely on shows, merch, brand collabs, YouTube operations to survive. Studio albums turned from income source into calling cards. This is the shared predicament of global independent music, but for Taiwan with a market size a fraction of the US/UK, the pain is especially sharp.

Government's role is also slowly but surely changing. The Ministry of Culture's Taiwan Beats brand tries to push Taiwan music internationally; subsidy programs support musicians attending SXSW, MIDEM, Music Matters and other international music expos. Kaohsiung Music Center's opening, increased music programming at cultural venues nationwide, all show the public sector starting to view independent music as part of cultural soft power. But whether resources truly reach those who need them most (young bands still rehearsing in garages, still hunting venues for their first show) remains an open question.

Perhaps the answer hides in Ren Jiang-da's story.

In 2017, 61-year-old Ren had left the music industry for years. Crystal Records was an unbearable dream. "You can't understand that pressure; I don't want Crystal to have any spider-silk traces left in my brain." He volunteered as a teacher at the community organization "After School Nest," accompanying disadvantaged children in Guoshun Li, Datong District. Everything was gone, but he was still doing the same thing — letting unseen things be seen.

That day was his last class. He brought a turntable to the classroom, played from The Beatles to Lin Sheng-xiang, from Leonard Cohen to Zhao Yi-hao. Below were twenty-some kids and parents, listening to unfamiliar music with puzzled expressions. He said: "Today's my last class here, let me be willful once!" Then: "You may not understand now, but someday in the future, you'll remember this moment."

A record shop fell. A basement closed. But what grew from there has spread to every corner of this island: every live house still holding on, every college student uploading their first song on StreetVoice, every young person rehearsing in garages in Kaohsiung, Tainan, Chiayi. Those seeds weren't any business model or industrial strategy — just a simple, almost naive belief: this island should have more voices heard.

What Ren said to those children is perhaps the same sentence Taiwan independent music has been saying to this island for forty years: you may not understand now, but someday you'll remember.
