# The Little Tigers: Imported Melodies, Filled with Their Own Youth

> **30-second overview:** The Little Tigers were almost a machine assembled from imported parts: the audition format copied the Japanese boy band, the lead song "Apple Garden" was a Japanese original with Chinese lyrics added, and even the stage performances emulated American styles. But in 1989, their album sold 250,000 copies in less than a month, and Taiwan stubbornly turned this group of imported idols into the most enduring youth memory of an entire generation. Thirty-plus years later, Wu Chih-lung, Su You-peng, and Chen Chih-peng have all shifted their careers to the Chinese mainland, making the question "Are the Little Tigers still Taiwan's?" an unanswerable exam question.

Chen Chih-peng, when asked on the Chinese variety show "Infinite Challenge 3" why he rarely mentions the Little Tigers these days, didn't dodge the question but answered very quietly: "Actually, it's not that I don't want to mention it—I really feel like I'm doing my own thing now, and I don't want to drag others into it." [^2]

The weight of those words can only be understood by rewinding thirty years. Because in 1989 Taiwan, tens of thousands of girls were desperately trying to "pull" these three young men into their lives: buying cassettes, cutting posters, waiting by radio stations, chasing concerts from one city to another. One of the people who most deserved to own this symbol later chose to let it go; and the island that most desperately wanted to grab it is still asking whether it counts as its own.

What this article aims to dissect is this paradox: how could an idol group whose parts were almost entirely imported become a collective memory that Taiwanese people couldn't bear to let go of?

## A Show Needed Three Young Men to Accompany the Little Cats

The story's beginning isn't romantic, and is even somewhat functional. On July 9, 1988, TVBS launched a variety show called "Youth Battle," produced by Chang Hsin-yen, hosted by Cao Lan and Tang Chih-wei [^3]. The show first had a female group called "Little Cats," which started with many members but later shrunk to three girls, one of whom was Hsu Shu-chien, who later became famous under the name Hsu Jo-hsuan [^3].

With the Little Cats, they wanted to find the Little Tigers to pair with them. During the audition process, the show selected three young men to form a male group to sing and dance with the Little Cats. These three faces were a combination pieced together by a television show for visual needs—they were never stars who fell from the sky. Their team cheer was loud and clear: "Little Tigers, Little Tigers, full of spirit" [^3], but behind these four words was a market intuition calculated by both the talent agency and the TV station.

The behind-the-scenes division of labor was equally industrialized. In 1987, Chang Hsin-yen and Miao Hsiu-li founded the "Kally's Dream" talent agency, responsible for artist management; record distribution was handled by the then-powerful Flying Dream Records [^4]. One handled packaging, the other handled distribution—this division of labor itself shows that the Little Tigers were from day one a designed, produced product, not a few friends getting together to play music.

> 💡 **Did you know?** The boy band often mistaken as the Little Tigers' "senior" group, Hong Zi-er, actually came later: Hong Zi-er was launched by Kally's Dream in 1989, making them the "Little Tigers second team." Once the timeline is straightened out, the title of "Taiwan's first (successful) male idol group" truly belongs to the Little Tigers. Before them, the 1985 girl group "City Sweetheart" was Taiwan's first idol girl group, but the male group line was started by the Little Tigers.

## Imported Melodies, Filled with Their Own Lyrics

If you want to find the most blatant evidence of the Little Tigers' "imported" nature, just listen to their most popular song.

![Little Tigers & Merry Party 1989 album "Happy New Year" cover, featuring "Apple Garden" (Universal Music 2025 reissue)](/article-images/music/little-tigers-xinnian-kuaile-album.webp)
_"Happy New Year" album cover (1989-01-10). The "Apple Garden" that Taiwanese girls know best is included here, and its melody comes from the Japanese boy band. Image: Universal Music 2025 reissue. © Universal Music (original release by Flying Dream Records), fair use editorial commentary._

The synthesizer intro of "[Apple Garden](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3u8Po2xioZg)" is something every Taiwanese person who lived through that era can hum along to. But it's actually the melody of the Japanese Johnny's idol group "Shonen T" 1988 11th single "What's Your Name?" [^5]. The original was composed by Maruyama Kōji and Jimmy Johnson; after Flying Dream bought the rights, they had Ting Hsiu-wen write Chinese lyrics [^5]. In other words, the coming-of-age theme song that Taiwanese girls sang along to by their beds, had a Japanese skeleton, with only the Chinese skin grown by Taiwan itself.

And this wasn't an isolated case. "[Red Dragonfly](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IFZ15lArEp0)" covered Long Yuncheng's "Tondo," with lyrics by Li Ziheng; "[Star-Crossed Lovers]" came from Wink's "Sad Tropical Fish"; "[Colorful Sky, Colorful Dreams]" was adapted from British singer Nick Heyward's "You Are My World" [^6]. On a single album, the songs driving the popularity were mostly written by others.

This approach was completely legal: Flying Dream did indeed buy the rights from Japan, it was an official commercial licensing, and then they wrote Chinese lyrics [^6]. But legality and originality are two different things. The fact that a melody remembered by the audience doesn't belong to this island itself makes the common saying "the Little Tigers are Taiwan's idols" hard to stand on.

> 📝 **Curator's note**
> The popular nostalgic narrative says the Little Tigers are "Taiwan's own" youth symbol. But just flip open the liner notes and look at the composer credits, and this claim falls apart: the audition format targeted Shonen T, the lead songs were Japanese melodies, the stage moves referenced American performances, and nearly all the parts were imported. What's truly Taiwan's is the act of "metabolism" itself: an island taking in foreign materials, filling them with its own lyrics, its own memories, its own tears, and finally growing something unrecognizable from its origin. This ability to turn the foreign into the self is actually a repeated reflection of Taiwan's popular culture—and Taiwan itself.

The imports weren't just the lead songs. The entire idol model was borrowed: the three-person lineup, the uniform design, the neat and unified dance steps—almost a direct copy of the Japanese Shonen T (Jinriro Kazuki, Shudo Kiyoshi, Higashiyama Noriyuki). Later, Taiwan's "Guanghua Magazine" English edition looked back and wrote a very blunt review: the Little Tigers were "a copy of Japan's Shonen T in virtually every respect" [^7].

However, stopping at "imitation" would be too simple. Johnny & Associates never had any official authorization or public response to the Little Tigers [^6]. In other words, the Little Tigers were stuck in an ambiguous middle ground: they bought Japan's "songs," but didn't buy Japan's "idol model," the latter being a whole set of things they copied and assembled themselves. What they could afford, they paid for; what they couldn't afford, they imitated. This was a learning method that was practical to the point of being naked.

Of course, not every song was borrowed. "[Wandering Cloud](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZsUx3NkFvA4)" (lyrics by Chen Yichung, music by Chen Chih-yuan), "[Butterfly Flying](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c7hbYLaD-FY)" (lyrics and music by Li Ziheng), "[Happy New Year]" were original songs [^6], proving that Flying Dream's lyric and composition team had their own strength. But when the common divisor of public memory is a cover song "Apple Garden," the word "imported" becomes the unavoidable entrance to understanding the Little Tigers.

## Pikachu, Little Handsome, Good Child: The Three Tigers Were Designed

Even who these three people were was designed.

Wu Chih-lung, Chen Chih-peng, and Su You-peng were respectively nicknamed "Pikachu Tiger," "Little Handsome Tiger," and "Good Child Tiger," and this naming system came from the talent agency [^8]. Each name precisely corresponded to a marketable character setting: Wu Chih-lung had a sports background and could do backflips, so he was the energetic "Pikachu Tiger"; Chen Chih-peng had a dance foundation and his looks were said to resemble Chang Chao-hsiung, so he was the "Little Handsome Tiger"; Su You-peng was a student at Taipei Municipal Jian Guo High School and could study, so he was the obedient and likable "Good Child Tiger" [^8].

> 📝 **Curator's note**
> When you put these three character settings side by side, you'll find they're actually a list of demands: one needs to be handsome, one needs to be energetic, and one needs to be reassuring to parents. Rather than selecting the "best three people," it's more like selecting the "three people most suitable for packaging as a group." Fans thought they fell in love with three different personalities, but behind these three personalities was an unspoken choice: the appearance of the group was not determined by the three people's real personalities, but more like the three templates the market wanted. What was designed wasn't just the songs, but also who to like and why to like them—all pre-planned into the product.

The production effort was concentrated on Flying Dream's team later known as the "Five Chen and Two Li." The entire "Wandering Cloud" album was produced by Chen Hsiu-nan, Flying Dream's deputy general manager and domestic division manager [^9], with arrangements by Chen Chih-yuan, styling by Chen Da-li, lyrics by Chen Yichung, music by Chen Yaochuan, plus Li Shou-quan and Li Ziheng [^9]. These names are almost unrecognizable to the public today, but the melodies and images that made girls scream in 1989 were drawn out line by line in recording studios and planning meetings.

As for "why these three people," there's no complete record. Who made the decision to "do a male idol group," who designed that uniform, who choreographed that dance—these specific decision-makers' names are almost impossible to find in public data [^8]. What's remembered is the three faces on stage, what's forgotten is the entire group of people behind the scenes. This is also the norm of the idol industry: the product shines, the production line is invisible.

> ⚠️ **Controversial view:** There's a view that the Little Tigers became popular because TVBS and Flying Dream monopolized the exposure during prime time—it was made by the channel, not by real popularity. This question can't be said to be entirely wrong; exposure was indeed an accelerant. But it can't be purely channel-driven to achieve the market response of selling nearly 200,000 copies in five days and 250,000 copies in less than a month for the album [^1][^10]. Exposure can make people "see," but it can't force people to "pay money to buy again and again." A more honest way to put it is: Flying Dream provided the firewood, but the fire really burned, relying on the collective hunger of Taiwan society at that time for idols as a new thing.

## From Taipei to Kaohsiung

The designed product met a society that was just preparing to go crazy.

On April 8, 1989, a signing session outside the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall in Taipei completely got out of control. The crowd that poured in was estimated to be anywhere from 20,000 to nearly 90,000 [^11], and the organizers couldn't contain the situation at all. For many people, that was Taiwan's first time seeing what it looked like when people went crazy for three teenagers; it was also the first time an island realized it had this kind of collective, almost religious youth energy.

More symbolically, the birth of the word "chasing stars." It is said that in 1989, the Little Tigers went from Taipei to Kaohsiung on tour, and the crazy fans followed them city by city, so the media coined the word "chasing stars" to describe this phenomenon [^12]. Although the etymology of this word is the media's saying, with a single source, it may not withstand strict verification, but it accurately captured the scene: youth really chases after a car, a train, a dream, running from north to south.

For those who couldn't afford to buy cassettes, there was another version of chasing stars. Radio broadcasts were the ticket for poor kids: waiting by the radio for the DJ to play "Apple Garden," recording that synthesizer intro onto a blank tape, and rewinding it repeatedly. Cassettes and radio were the two parallel channels for that generation to connect with their idols—one needed money, the other only needed patience.

> 💡 **Did you know?** The Little Tigers' popularity wasn't limited to Taiwan. It is said that including pirated versions, their album sales reached 15 million copies, and "Wandering Cloud" alone had 4 million orders in mainland China [^10]. The songs flowed into the entire Chinese-speaking world through both legitimate and pirated cassettes, reaching even the countryside of Southeast Asia. But the farther these songs drifted, the more diluted the "Taiwan" label as their place of origin became. Many overseas listeners hummed "Apple Garden" without knowing the singers were from Taiwan.

## Youth in Its True Colors, Heading Across the Taiwan Strait

In 1991, the Little Tigers took this youth energy across a very sensitive line at the time.

![Little Tigers 1991 album "Love" cover, released the year they crossed the strait to perform in China (Universal Music 2025 reissue)](/article-images/music/little-tigers-ai-album.webp)
_"Love" (1991) is the album released the year the Little Tigers crossed the strait to perform in China, putting social welfare themes into idol dance music. Image: Universal Music 2025 reissue. © Universal Music (original release by Flying Dream Records), fair use editorial commentary._

In September of that year, the three of them went to China. First they participated in three performances of the "Chinese Film Industry's Charity Performance for Disaster Relief" in Beijing (the background was the 1991 East China floods), then launched a tour called "Youth in Its True Colors, Happy Paradise," visiting Shanghai, Wuhan, and other places, holding more than ten shows, and even appearing on the stage of the first Xi'an Ancient Culture and Arts Festival [^13]. In the cross-strait atmosphere at the time, this made the Little Tigers become "one of the earliest Taiwanese artists to hold concerts on the mainland" [^13].

To understand how sensitive this step was at the time, just look at the cross-strait clock of 1991. That year, the Cross-Strait Exchange Foundation was established in March, the government abolished the "Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of Communist Rebellion" in May, and the Straits Association for the Promotion of Reunification wasn't established until December [^14]. The three links across the strait hadn't been opened yet, and artists had to go through Hong Kong to get to the mainland [^14]. In other words, when the Little Tigers stepped onto the mainland stage, they happened to be caught in that window where "the ice was just beginning to melt, but the road wasn't yet open."

> 📝 **Curator's note**
> It's easy to describe this tour as "the lifting of restrictions after the lifting of martial law allowed everything, so idols could cross the strait." But this causal chain is too straight. The lifting of martial law in 1987 did indeed loosen the overall environment, allowing popular culture to thrive and commercial auditions to be established. A master's thesis from Fu Jen Catholic University on the Little Tigers pointed out that "after the lifting of martial law, popular entertainment culture became increasingly active," and Taiwan's idol groups began to emerge in late 1980s [^15]. But the Little Tigers' direct cause was the commercial audition plus the introduction of the Japanese idol model, not the "lifting of martial law" political event itself. The lifting of martial law was just the weather in the background, and the person who pressed play was someone else. Attributing everything to the lifting of martial law would actually obscure the real driving force behind this matter—market and imitation.

It's worth adding: when it came to artists going to the mainland to perform, the Taiwanese government's specific policy controls at the time are not clearly recorded in public data [^14]. So neither "the government banned it" nor "the government allowed it" can be easily stated—this was a period when the system hadn't yet matured and the boundaries were blurry. The Little Tigers were in this blur, singing Taiwan's youth for the first time to the other side of the strait.

## When the Troops Arrived, the Myth Stopped

What stopped the Little Tigers was more like a stack of military orders than the market cooling down.

Many people's memory of "two breakups" is much simpler than reality. The Little Tigers never officially announced their breakup [^16]; they were gradually diluted by reality. At the end of 1991, Chen Chih-peng enlisted for service, moving from the Navy Marine Corps to the Yuju Opera Troupe, lasting about two years [^16]. With one tiger missing, the group's operations were cut off at the first section.

In late 1993, Chen Chih-peng was discharged from the military, and the three of them briefly reunited to release "The Stars Are Still Bright" [^16]—just looking at the name of this album, you can smell a hint of "trying to prove we're still here." But the peak of the idol wave had already passed. After the 1995 "Tiger Roaring Dragon Soaring Mad Dash 95" concert, the group naturally disbanded [^16]; the real final breakpoint was Wu Chih-lung enlisting in 1997 (serving until 1998, retiring with a third-grade disability due to shoulder injury) [^16]. Su You-peng was exempt from military service due to myopia [^16]. There was no announcement, no farewell concert with that kind of ceremonial feeling—the myth just quietly stopped.

It was at this point that Chen Chih-peng's situation was defined. He wasn't the one who was marginalized from the start. Thanks to his resemblance to Chang Chao-hsiung, his early popularity wasn't at the bottom [^17]. His marginalization was the result of serving in 1991, not the starting point: when he returned from the military, the wave that pushed idols to the sky had already receded, and his position couldn't go back [^17]. Projecting the later loss onto the beginning is a common memory displacement.

![Su You-peng 2012 recent photo, the "Good Child Tiger" later became an actor and director, shifting his career to China](/article-images/music/alec-su-2012.webp)
_Su You-peng 2012 recent photo. The "Good Child Tiger" who was studying at Jian Guo Middle School and being an idol at the same time later moved his entire acting career to the other side of the strait. Photo: ASIF Photography / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0._

Su You-peng's reflection on this youth was more complex than the outside world imagined. Years later on the variety show "Breaking Through Thorny Paths," he said: "I don't want to destroy the beautiful picture in everyone's mind, so I feel a lot of pressure." [^18] But in the same sentence, he also said very firmly: "Although we didn't have the slogan of 'team soul' back then, he and Wu Chih-lung and Chen Chih-peng were naturally and sincerely close, truly loving the Little Tigers, loving this honor, and I still am to this day." [^18] The coexistence of pressure and love is more realistic than any single label of "regret" or "no regret."

> 💡 **Did you know?** Su You-peng was a Jian Guo Middle School student. In 1991, he was admitted to National Taiwan University's Department of Mechanical Engineering (fifth choice in the science and engineering group) before dropping out to pursue showbiz [^19]. The tension between his star status and top-tier academics made him later say on "Create 2019": "At that age, I suddenly started bearing a lot of attention, although I didn't have time to settle down." He also admitted that he "couldn't spend his whole life just carrying the Little Tigers' spotlight, wanting to become a capable artist" [^20]. A child who was pushed to the altar at a young age spent most of his life learning how to step down.

## All Three Bodies Crossed the Strait

If you want to find the three people of the Little Tigers today, you need to shift your gaze to the other side of the strait.

![Wu Chih-lung 2012 in Paris street holding a camera, after the Little Tigers disbanded he moved to China and became famous for "Four Gentlemen" in "Step by Step"](/article-images/music/nicky-wu-paris-2012.webp)
_Wu Chih-lung 2012 in Paris. For the Z generation who haven't seen the Little Tigers, he's almost only known as the "Four Gentlemen" in "Step by Step." Photo: LuvElaine / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0._

Wu Chih-lung founded the Beijing-based production company "Straw Bear Entertainment," became famous for his role as the "Four Gentlemen" in "Step by Step," married the female lead Liu Shishi; to the Z generation, he's almost only known as the "Four Gentlemen" [^21]. Su You-peng went through "The Legend of Haolan," transitioned to acting, then became a director, directing "The Left Ear" which grossed over 500 million yuan [^22]. Chen Chih-peng's situation was the most turbulent, admitting tax evasion in 2023, and almost never mentioning the Little Tigers on mainland variety shows [^23]. All three of their career centers have fallen in China.

So the exam question comes up: when the most popular three bodies of a group have all crossed the strait, does this symbol still count as Taiwan's?

Even the attribution of the name became a battlefield. China's Baidu Baike labeled the Little Tigers as "China Taiwan male idol group"—a way of directly writing the sovereignty claim into the entry title; while Taiwan's Wikipedia calls it "Taiwanese pop male music group" [^24]. The same group, two different names, representing two different answers to "who does it belong to."

> 📝 **Curator's note**
> There's a small detail that can explain the problem: when China later appeared with the boy band TFBOYS, Taiwan's Central News Agency reported it as "China's Little Tigers" [^25]. This analogy confirms something in reverse—in that moment, at least in the Taiwanese media's perception, the prototype "Little Tigers" was still Taiwan's. A symbol can be used as a "foreign version" comparison group, the premise is that everyone implicitly recognizes its place of origin. So the question "Is the Little Tigers Taiwan's?" might not lie in where the three people live now, but in the original point that everyone's mind defaults to when they use the word "Little Tigers."

It's worth pondering that the question of "whether the Little Tigers are still Taiwan's after moving westward" has almost never been directly discussed in past reviews and studies. It's a case that's been shelved, with no one willing to make a decision. Perhaps precisely because it's so hard to answer: answering "yes" means facing the fact that the three people's life trajectories have indeed moved elsewhere; answering "no" means having to give up an entire generation of Taiwanese tears that have truly flowed.

So this article doesn't intend to answer it for you. It just wants to put the question in the right place: the Little Tigers used almost entirely imported parts to assemble the most enduring piece of youth in Taiwan's history; and when those three bodies later crossed the strait, the memory didn't follow—it stayed in the cassettes, stayed in the radio recording noise, stayed in the chaos of the crowd outside the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall.

Chen Chih-peng said he "doesn't want to drag others in." But some things, once an island has taken them in and metabolized them into its own flesh and blood, aren't something that the person involved can just let go of by saying so. Three bodies crossed the strait, the memory stayed here.
