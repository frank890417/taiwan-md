# Golden Melody Awards: Taiwan Honors Its Own Music, and the Moments Remembered for a Lifetime Across Thirty-Five Years

> **30-second overview:** The Golden Melody Awards are where Taiwan honors its own music. The first ceremony was held in 1990; by 2026, it had reached its 37th edition. It began with a letter singer Tsai Chin wrote to the head of the Government Information Office. Its judges vote behind closed doors and deliberately ignore sales figures. It has crowned the biggest names in Mandopop, including <a href="/people/周杰倫" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Jay Chou</a>, <a href="/people/張惠妹" target="_blank" rel="noopener">A-Mei</a>, and <a href="/people/蔡依林" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Jolin Tsai</a>, while also giving top honors to people who sing in Taiwanese, Hakka, and Indigenous languages. Over thirty-five years, it has recorded how this island listens to itself.

On June 29, 2024, at Taipei Arena, <a href="/people/草東沒有派對" target="_blank" rel="noopener">No Party for Cao Dong</a> won three major awards in a row: Album of the Year, Best Mandarin Album, and Best Band. It was a rare moment in Golden Melody history: the same band sweeping three major awards in the same year for a second time. Everyone below the stage was waiting for them to come up.

But the person who walked onstage was not a band member. It was their manager. Drummer Tsai Yi-fan, whom everyone called Fan-fan, had died three years earlier at age 26. The manager spoke into the microphone, said what had to be said, and ended with three words: “Thank you, Fan-fan.”

The entire hall went silent for several seconds.

Those few seconds explain what the Golden Melody Awards are. They are not a ranking of “whoever is most popular wins.” They are where Taiwan gives awards to its own music and decides which sounds deserve to be remembered properly. A band’s drummer was gone, but the drums she played remained inside the album The Servile. Three years later, her band stood at the highest level for a second time, and the entire industry was willing to stop for a few seconds of silence for one name. That kind of weight is something accumulated over thirty-five years.

This article is meant to introduce the award properly: how it grew out of a letter, what happens inside that closed-door voting room, which kings and queens of pop it has crowned, how it gives four languages their own stages, and how even its critics have stood on its stage. Thirty-five years is, actually, a very long story.

## A Letter, and an Official Whose Whole Family Were Her Fans

The Golden Melody Awards began with a letter written in 1982 by a folk singer in her twenties.

Tsai Chin was at the height of her popularity that year, and almost everyone could hum “Just Like Your Tenderness.” She felt that Europe and the United States had large pop music awards ceremonies, such as the Grammys and the American Music Awards, while Taiwan, a center of Mandopop, did not even have a national music award. So she sat down, wrote eight pages, and mailed them to James Soong, then head of the Government Information Office, proposing that Taiwan hold an “awards ceremony specifically for Mandarin-language singers.”

She later recalled that the letter was extremely detailed, even listing award categories one by one. Years later, when she talked about the episode on television, host Ma Shih-fang laughed after hearing it: “You even wrote the whole founding blueprint!”

> 💡 **Did you know?** The original copy of Tsai Chin’s letter has never been made public, so no one can verify word for word “which awards she listed in the letter.” What we know is what she later said in person on the PTS program Music All the Way: she had even listed the award categories in advance. A singer in her twenties was serious enough to think through the entire skeleton of the ceremony first. That fact itself feels very Taiwan.

Even better was James Soong’s reaction. According to Tsai, after Soong received the letter and saw the signature, he replied: “Miss Tsai Chin, why did you say in the letter that you’re the Tsai Chin who wears glasses and sings ‘Just Like Your Tenderness’?! You don’t need to introduce yourself. My whole family are your fans!” Two days later, he invited Tsai Chin to the Government Information Office.

_Tsai Chin, “The Forgotten Time.” The person who wrote to James Soong suggesting the Golden Melody Awards was the “Tsai Chin who wore glasses and sang.” One singer’s idea later grew into one of the most important music ceremonies in the Mandarin-speaking world._

There was still some distance between an idea and a national ceremony. The Government Information Office first held three editions of a warm-up event called “Good Songs for Everyone to Sing” in 1986, then formally began planning the Golden Melody Awards at the end of 1988. Registration opened in August 1989, nominees were announced in September, and the ceremony had originally been scheduled earlier, but because Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was closed for renovation, it was postponed all the way to January 6, 1990.

That night, at Taipei’s Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall, Cui Taijing entered in formal dress to host, accompanied by the CTS Orchestra playing the introduction to her signature song “Love God.” Twenty-five judges, 28 record companies, and 410 submissions ultimately produced 11 awards. Best Male Singer went to Yin Zhengyang, Best Female Singer to Jody Chiang, Best New Artist to Sky Wu, and Song of the Year to Chao Chuan’s “I Am Ugly, But I Am Gentle.”

![Taipei Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall, the venue for the first Golden Melody Awards in 1990. Because the hall was closed for renovation that year, the ceremony was postponed until January 6.](/article-images/music/sun-yat-sen-hall-1990-gma-venue.webp)

The first edition was in fact quite plain: no red carpet, none of the large-scale performances that came later, and not even a “Best Vocal Album” award, which would only be added in the second edition. But the skeleton was in place. With one letter, an official whose whole family were fans, and several years of preparation, Taiwan from then on had one night every year devoted to deciding: “This year, which voices deserve to be remembered?”

## The Room Where the Door Closes for Voting

To understand why a Golden Melody Award carries such weight, you first have to look at how it's decided.

The Golden Melody Awards' judging system, put plainly, is a group of people locked in a room, listening seriously to every work, and then voting. It sounds simple; the hard part is that "seriously" and "locked away."

The whole process has three rounds: in the preliminary round, judges listen to songs individually in an online system and score independently; the semi-final and final rounds enter closed-door meetings. The decisive final round is scheduled for the morning of the awards ceremony, where judges vote anonymously by computer to select the winners to be announced that night.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Many people's instinctive reaction to "closed-door judging" is "black box, clique." But the Golden Melody Awards closes the room to block precisely another force — the market. When all judges are isolated, not allowed to see sales figures, not allowed to be lobbied, the only thing they can rely on is their own ears and professional judgment. In other words, that closed door blocks the inertia of "whoever sells best should win." Half of a Golden Melody's credibility comes from this door.

How far does this secrecy go? Blow, which has covered the process, described the final judging scene: judges enter small rooms for isolation, "with dedicated escorts for smoking and bathroom breaks," unable to speak to anyone on the way, voting anonymously by computer until results appear. Even phones are collected uniformly and only returned after the last award is presented.

This isn't legend. At the 37th edition in 2026, winner Chen Hsien-ching laughed on stage saying she had prepared a speech, "but my phone was taken away" — so she had to improvise. Phones moving in and out between judges and winners is itself the best footnote to this secrecy mechanism.

So how are judges selected anyway? The official line looks for "forward-looking vision," affirming works with artistic value that need not follow the commercial market. Dila, founder of Yen She who has served as a judge for three editions, offered a more colloquial criterion. He said when it's hard to choose between two excellent albums, he asks himself one question:

> "Would I want to buy this record?"

The charm of this sentence lies in how it distills an industry award's criterion into a music fan's most honest impulse: would I, myself, pay money for this? Dila also said something for those who feel "not winning means losing": "Being nominated is affirmation, for real."

Of course, this system isn't without critics. Some say it lacks explicit judging criteria, relying too much on individual judges' professional ethics; some say when the same person is nominated in multiple categories, judges tend to spread the awards around. These discussions have always existed: but it's precisely because people care and discuss that what happens in this room is worth taking seriously.

## Golden Melodies for Kings and Queens

The Golden Melody Awards are of course also the coronation stage for Mandopop’s biggest stars. The names you grew up hearing have almost all left peak moments on this stage.

Start with the record-holder. Jay Chou has won 15 Golden Melody Awards, the highest tally among male singers. His true night of apotheosis was the 13th edition in 2002. With Fantasy, he personally took home four awards: Best Pop Vocal Album, Best Composer for “Love Before BC,” and Best Album Producer, not counting Best Lyricist for Vincent Fang’s “William Castle” and Best Arranger for Chung Hsing-min’s “Nunchucks.” One album swept through almost all of that year’s technical awards. For a young artist only a few years into his debut, that night effectively announced the beginning of an era.

_A-Mei, “Listen to the Sea.” She has been nominated for Best Female Singer 14 times, a Golden Melody record, and has won three times: for Truth in 2002, Amit in 2010, and Faces of Paranoia in 2015._

The women’s side is just as rich. A-Mei holds a record that will be hard to break: 14 nominations for Best Female Singer. A three-time winner, she was especially interesting in 2010, when she competed through “Amit,” an alter ego carrying her Puyuma lineage, and won Best Mandarin Female Singer for Amit. One person had become two voices.

For “knowing how to win,” Tanya Chua is another legend. She has won Best Female Singer four times, for Amphibian, Goodbye & Hello, Sing It Out of Love, and Depart, making her the category’s record-holder. And did you know Tanya Chua is Singaporean? The Golden Melody Awards have never been only for singers with Taiwan passports. They honor people who make the best works in Mandarin, and Tanya Chua’s four Best Female Singer wins are the clearest proof.

Some people chose to turn away at the summit. <a href="/people/江蕙" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Jody Chiang</a> is a legend among Taiwanese-language divas. Over her career, she won 13 Golden Melody Awards and took Best Taiwanese Female Singer four years in a row from the 11th to the 14th editions. But after winning again in 2003 for Red Thread, she made a surprising decision: she announced that she would no longer compete for Best Female Singer. Making room on the stage for younger singers was a gesture as moving as going onstage to accept an award.

The stories of the kings and queens could go on: <a href="/music/五月天" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mayday</a> moving from underground venues to stadiums, <a href="/people/蔡依林" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Jolin Tsai</a> with each transformation, <a href="/people/張惠妹" target="_blank" rel="noopener">A-Mei</a> and her nomination record spanning more than two decades. The Golden Melody Awards’ list of winners for Mandopop later became the coordinates of youth for an entire generation.

## Four Languages, Four Stages

If you only remember the pop kings and queens, you miss the most distinctive thing about the Golden Melody Awards: they give four languages their own highest honors. Among music awards around the world, this is almost a singular design.

The story begins with a Taiwanese-language rock song. At the 3rd Golden Melody Awards in 1991, <a href="/people/林強" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Lim Giong</a>’s “Marching Forward” won Best Song of the Year. Before then, Taiwanese-language songs were mostly associated with sorrow and laments. “Marching Forward” used a rock rhythm to sing about a young man from the countryside heading north with a suitcase to make his way, completely overturning the tonal image of Taiwanese-language song. The album sold more than 400,000 copies and is seen as a representative work of the “New Taiwanese Song Movement.” Interestingly, the same year it also won Best Vocal Album Producer, but the recipients were Bobby Chen, <a href="/people/李宗盛" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Jonathan Lee</a>, and others, not Lim Giong himself.

At first, the Golden Melody Awards did not distinguish by language. The first two editions had only “Best Male Vocalist” and “Best Female Vocalist.” Only at the 3rd edition in 1991 were “Mandarin” and “dialect” separated. Then, at the 14th edition in 2003, “dialect” was further divided into separate Taiwanese, Hakka, and Indigenous-language awards. Why subdivide further? Because in the preceding years, every winner of “Best Dialect Vocalist” had been a Taiwanese-language singer, while Hakka and Indigenous-language creators had long been squeezed out. That year’s submissions surged to 128 record companies and more than 4,800 works, including over 100 Hakka or Indigenous albums. The Golden Melody Awards simply opened a separate door for each.

Once those doors opened, some of the most moving moments in Mandopop followed.

At the 11th edition in 2000, a Puyuma police officer from Taitung stood on the highest stage. <a href="/people/陳建年" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Purdur Chen</a> won Best Mandarin Male Vocalist for Ocean. Note: Mandarin Male Vocalist, not Indigenous-language vocalist. He defeated fellow nominees Jacky Cheung, Wang Leehom, David Tao, and Harlem Yu, four kings at the height of their powers. A police officer who worked at a station by day, singing songs he wrote about the sea and the mountains, defeated the hottest figures in the entire Mandopop industry. His grandfather was the Puyuma song master Lu Sen-bao; the grandson caught that musical lineage across thirty years.

If Purdur Chen proved that an Indigenous creator could beat the pop kings, then <a href="/people/阿爆" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ABAO</a> (Aljenljeng) at the 31st edition in 2020 proved that an album sung entirely in an Indigenous language could win the ceremony’s top prize. Her kinakaian: Mother Tongue, an album entirely in Paiwan, took home three awards that night: Best Indigenous Language Album, Song of the Year for “Thank You,” and finally the top Album of the Year award. An album whose lyrics you might not understand at all was chosen by the judges as Taiwan’s best album of the year.

When ABAO accepted Album of the Year, she said something very Golden Melody: “If you don’t know why this album became Album of the Year, please listen to it once. If you don’t like it, please listen a second time!” She also addressed Indigenous children watching on camera: “Do not waste your talents, and do not rely on talent.”

> 💡 **Did you know?** There is almost no other music award in the world that gives separate top honors to four different languages within its own territory. You can make the best album in Taiwanese, Hakka, or an Indigenous language, and be crowned at the same ceremony. For Hakka and Indigenous languages, whose speaker populations are shrinking, the Golden Melody stage offers not only honor, but also a reason for these voices to be heard nationwide and for a new generation to want to learn them.

This is what makes the Golden Melody Awards worth being proud of. They never pretend Taiwan has only one voice. Mandarin, Taiwanese, Hakka, and Indigenous languages: four languages, four stages, lit in turn on the same night.

## Even Its Critics Have Stood on Its Stage

An award large enough has to be able to contain the people who criticize it. One of the most fascinating things about the Golden Melody Awards is that several times, they have handed the microphone to their sharpest critics, and those moments have become among the ceremony’s most memorable images.

At the 18th edition in 2007, Lin Sheng-xiang, formerly of <a href="/music/交工樂隊" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Labor Exchange Band</a>, was nominated for and won both Best Hakka Singer and Best Hakka Album for Planting Trees. Then he did something no one had done in the Golden Melody Awards’ 18 years: he refused the awards on the spot.

He spent only 30 seconds onstage, but those 30 seconds had been planned for two years. He said these were the two awards he “least wanted to receive.” His criticism was of the system itself, not of any person: he felt that classifying music by language put the cart before the horse. “Why can Best Producer cross languages, but albums cannot?” He also said, “For such an important Chinese-language music award to classify by language means other countries simply cannot enter.” He declined the two trophies and donated the NT$250,000 prize money, distributing it to a tree-planting team in Meinong, two local publications, and “rice bomber” Yang Ju-men.

What is worth considering is that the Golden Melody Awards did not change the design simply because they were publicly criticized. They heard it, kept discussing it, and retained language categories. For an awards ceremony to calmly receive the statement “there is something wrong with how you run this award” on its own stage is itself a form of confidence.

How to classify language remains a live debate even today. Scholar Kuo Li-hsin has criticized the division into Mandarin, Taiwanese, Hakka, and Indigenous languages, saying it “appears to protect each language, but in fact consolidates a cultural system in which disadvantaged language communities continue to be marginalized.” In his view, rather than setting up a few “exclusive” Golden Melody categories that push Indigenous and Hakka languages into the margins, what is needed is real educational and promotional investment.

> ⚠️ **Contested view:** Language categories also create an awkwardness: works that mix multiple languages often have nowhere to go. At the 33rd Golden Melody Awards in 2022, Collage’s MEmento·MORI was half Taiwanese and half Amis, with a large amount of Japanese mixed in, but it could not enter any language category because it did not satisfy the requirement of being “mainly sung” in a particular language. In the end, it could only compete for language-neutral annual awards. A work singing in several languages of this land instead became an anomaly within the classification system. The Golden Melody Awards are still trying to solve this problem.

These arguments are not flaws in the Golden Melody Awards. They are evidence that the awards are still alive. A thirty-five-year-old award is still arguing with itself about “how should language be divided?” and “what do we do with multilingual works?” That means it has not hardened. It is still growing together with Taiwan’s music.

## The Golden Melody Awards Also Record Taiwan’s Times

Because the Golden Melody Awards are Taiwan’s largest music stage, some winning moments are not only about music. They also carry the social emotions of their time. For this part, it is enough to record what happened.

At the 26th edition in 2015, <a href="/music/滅火器樂團" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Fire EX.</a>’s “Island’s Sunrise” won Best Song of the Year. The song was born from the 2014 Sunflower Movement: lead singer Sam Yang was invited by Taipei National University of the Arts students to create it, spent two days writing the melody and one day recording it, and first taught it live at the Legislative Yuan on March 27. When accepting the award, Yang said, “Fifteen years as a band, our first Golden Melody Award! ... Dedicated to our beloved Taiwan.” He also shouted, “God bless Taiwan.” This winning segment was skipped in the Tencent broadcast version in China.

The Golden Melody Awards were once also a shared stage for Mandarin-language singers on both sides of the Taiwan Strait. Na Ying became the first singer from China to win Best Female Singer at the 12th edition in 2001 with Sad Romance, and also won Best Lyricist that year. Cui Jian became the first China-born Golden Melody Best Male Singer at the 33rd edition in 2022. But across those 21 years, these two were the only China-born Golden Melody Best Male and Best Female Singer winners. The true golden age in which the Golden Melody Awards and the Chinese market overlapped was actually the 2000s, when Taiwan’s music industry dominated Mandarin record sales in China, not a period when singers from China won Golden Melody Awards in large numbers.

These moments can be placed here without much further judgment. The Golden Melody Awards are Taiwan’s stage. What Taiwan has experienced, cared about, moved closer to, and moved away from over these years naturally leaves traces on that stage. By this point, you probably already have the images in your own mind.

## The Opening on a Snake’s Back, and Two People Who Waited a Long Time

Thirty-some years may sound like long ago, but the Golden Melody Awards have always been growing in the present tense. Just last month, on June 27, 2026, the 37th Golden Melody Awards concluded at Taipei Arena.

That night, A-Lin hosted for the first time. In the opening, she entered standing on the head of a giant python, paying tribute to <a href="/people/蔡依林" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Jolin Tsai</a>’s classic stage performance, with tremendous force. She was also a winner herself: the film theme song she sang, “Happiness Is Singing,” won Song of the Year.

The two biggest winners that night were both people who had waited a long time. Jolin Tsai competed for Best Female Singer for the fifth time with her album Pleasure and finally got her wish, winning for the second time after a 19-year gap. She personally took home three awards: Album of the Year, Best Mandarin Female Singer, and Best Vocal Recording Album, from nine nominations. On the Best Male Singer side, it was another kind of waiting: Chang Chen-yue won Best Mandarin Male Singer for the first time 33 years after his debut, while also taking Best Mandarin Album and Best Composer. One waited 19 years; the other, 33. The Golden Melody Awards are never in a hurry to give prizes to the fastest people.

The stages for all four languages were also lit that night. Best Taiwanese Male Singer went to <a href="/people/蕭煌奇" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ricky Hsiao</a>, Best Taiwanese Female Singer to PiA Wu, Best Hakka Singer to Chen Yi-no, and Best Indigenous Language Singer to Suming <a href="/music/當代原住民創作歌手" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rupi</a>. Best Band went to Taipei’s <a href="/music/落日飛車" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Sunset Rollercoaster</a>, another band that grew out of the independent scene, bypassed the mainstream industry, and reached the highest stage. As for the Best New Artist whose “phone was taken away” and who had to improvise her speech, that was <a href="/music/陳嫺靜" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Chen Hsien-ching</a>, whom we met in the judging section.

## Three Decades: How This Island Listens to Itself

Back to those few seconds of silence.

At the 2024 Taipei Arena, No Party for Cao Dong cemented their legendary status for the second time; their agent accepted on their behalf and said, "Thank you, Fan Fan." Fan Fan is gone, but the drums she played live on in _Ugly Slave_ — every time someone hits play, that drumbeat sounds once more. A departed drummer, remembered by an entire industry because of the Golden Melody Awards; a three-year-old album, heard anew by a whole generation because of it.

This is probably what the Golden Melody Awards do: from that modest first edition in 1990 to the 37th edition today, they answer the same question for Taiwan year after year — what voices on this island this year are worth remembering well.

What it remembers includes Jay Chou's sweep on the night of _Fantasy_, A-Mei's persistence through fourteen nominations, a Taitung policeman singing of the sea, an album entirely in Paiwan, a Hakka singer who refused the trophy, a song born on the streets. Mandarin, Taiwanese, Hakka, Indigenous languages; million-sellers and songs not a single word understood; reigning heavenly kings and quiet songwriters — it places all these voices on the same stage.

After thirty-odd years, the Golden Melody Awards aren't really about giving out awards. They're about recording, year by year, how this island listens to itself — on Taiwan's behalf.
