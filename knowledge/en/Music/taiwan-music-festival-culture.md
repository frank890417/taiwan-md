**Taiwan's music festival culture began in 1995 with a makeshift stage set up by two Americans on Kenting Beach. Over thirty years, it has evolved into distinct species such as Spring Scream, Hohaiyan Rock Festival, and Megaport Festival. By the 2020s, this island—smaller than the Netherlands—hosts over 50 music festivals annually. This scale cannot be explained by market demand alone; it is closer to a collective ritual: a way for an entire generation to find their voice outdoors.**

## Two Foreigners on Kenting Beach

In April 1995, Wade Davis and Jimi Moe, two Americans living in Taiwan, set up a stage at a music venue called "Dream Kenting" in Kenting, inviting about 12 bands to perform. There were no sponsors, no ticketing systems; the bands sold hand-dyed T-shirts from their own stalls. They called the event "Spring Scream."

That year, it had only been eight years since Taiwan's martial law was lifted. The underground music scene was just beginning to emerge: [Loh Tsui Kang Commune](/en/music/loh-tsui-kang-commune/) was roaring in Taiwanese at small venues in Taipei, and the band Fourtitude (四分衛) was rehearsing in front of Witch House. But an outdoor music festival? No one had ever done it. Rock music in Taiwan had not yet moved beyond the concept of indoor venues.

In a 2005 interview with the _Taipei Times_, Davis said, "It's a small island, so it's good to have some new things coming from outside." Looking back thirty years later, this statement underestimates the impact he created. Spring Scream wasn't just about "letting new things in." It proved something Taiwanese people hadn't considered: you could listen to music by the sea, on a grassy field, or under the stars, and it would change your entire perception of music.

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> Spring Scream grew from its first edition with 12 bands and one stage to a peak of 270 bands across eight stages, with events once spanning up to 11 days. Ticket prices rose from free to NT$1,600, but they always maintained one rule: no performance fees were paid to the bands, nor were there registration fees.

By 1999, another co-founder of Spring Scream, Jimi Moe, established a far-reaching threshold: all performing bands had to play original works and could not perform covers. This decision directly transformed Spring Scream from "outdoor karaoke" into a "proving ground for original music." Moe later said, "We've always tried to make it clear that Spring Scream is for the bands." (Source: _Taipei Times_, April 2005 report)

## The Official Beach: Twenty Years of Hohaiyan Rock Festival

Spring Scream proved that music festivals were viable, but it always carried the label of a "party organized by foreigners." What truly brought music festivals into the Taiwanese mainstream was a completely different driver: the government.

On July 15, 2000, the Taipei County Government (now New Taipei City Government) collaborated with the independent music label Chieftain Music (角頭音樂) to hold the first "Gongliao International Hohaiyan Rock Festival" at the Fulong Beach in Gongliao. The key figures behind it were Chieftain Music boss Chang Ssu-shih (張四十三) and the then-director of the Taipei County Government Newsroom, Liao Chih-chien (廖志堅). Their ambition was clear: to create a Taiwanese version of Woodstock.

The first year lasted only one day and drew 8,000 people. While not massive in scale, the sight of a stage built on the sand, blending rock music with the sound of crashing waves, gave everyone present a brand-new sensation.

The real game-changer was the "Hohaiyan Independent Music Awards," which began in 2001. This competition opened registration to independent bands across Taiwan; winners were decided through several preliminary rounds, with the grand prize being NT$200,000. For independent bands at the time, who were often too poor to even rent a rehearsal studio, this money and this stage were lifelines.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The paradox of the Hohaiyan Rock Festival lies in this: it is funded by the government, organized officially, and free to enter—a combination that is almost impossible in the global music festival ecosystem. It is the "most institutional" festival, yet it has incubated the most "anti-institutional" bands.

Sodagreen (蘇打綠), Wang Fu (旺福), 88rising's predecessor/indie acts like 88 Ball (八十八顆芭樂籽), and Matzka. These names that would later shine at the Golden Melody Awards all emerged from the sands of the Hohaiyan festival. The lineup of judges for the second Hohaiyan Awards in 2001 illustrates the weight of this competition: Wu Bai (伍佰), Huang Yun-ling (黃韻玲), and Ma Shih-fang (馬世芳). When the gatekeepers of indie music are willing to sit on a beach as judges, you know the matter is serious.

However, the fate of Hohaiyan Rock Festival was ultimately tied to its origins. Since the government provides the funding, the government can also withdraw it. It was suspended in 2020 due to COVID-19 and remained silent for three consecutive years. In May 2023, the New Taipei City Tourism Bureau once announced its permanent termination, sparking strong backlash from the music community and local residents of Gongliao; Legislator Lai Pin-yu (賴品妤) and local village chiefs publicly called to preserve this cultural asset. A few days later, the city government reversed its stance, saying it would continue—but the golden era, when tens of thousands would take the train to Fulong every summer to watch the sea and listen to music, is gone.

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**
> The controversy over the festival's suspension reveals a structural problem: when a music festival relies on government budgets, it remains merely an "administrative project" that can disappear due to a change in mayor, budget cuts, or a pandemic. The cultural capital cultivated by Hohaiyan over twenty years lacks a corresponding protection mechanism within the bureaucratic system.

## The Southern Hegemon: Megaport Festival and Urban Identity

While Hohaiyan flourished on northern beaches, something completely different was brewing in southern Taiwan.

In October 2006, Lin Chang-tso (Freddy), lead singer of Chthonic (閃靈), and band leader Yeh Hsiang-yi (葉湘怡) brought their curation team, TRA Music, to Kaohsiung's Piers 11 and 12 for the first "Megaport Festival" (大港開唱). The name "Da-gang" (大港) has a Hokkien pronunciation that implies "fullness" or "abundance," an idea conceived by Afasia guitarist Wu Yi-chun (吳逸駿). The English name, MEGAPORT, was chosen by Lin Chang-tso. Having a heavy metal frontman name a music festival speaks volumes about Megaport's DNA.

The fundamental difference between Megaport and Hohaiyan lies in identity. Hohaiyan is the government finding musicians to hold an event; Megaport is musicians deciding to host a festival belonging to the South in Kaohsiung. From its first year, Megaport has insisted that at least half of its lineup comes from southern Taiwan. Its stage names are all steeped in local flavor: Nan-ba-tian (南霸天), Goddess Dragon (女神龍), Sea Dragon King (海龍王), Chut-thau-tian (出頭天), and Ka-mo-mai (卡魔麥)—each carrying the grassroots grit unique to this port city.

Megaport has carried a political gene since day one. The first edition coincided with the Kaohsiung mayoral election. The organizers did something unprecedented: they required the major candidates to publicly propose local music and culture policies and published these in the event's program. The festival suddenly gained public significance; it was not just a performance, but a platform to hold politicians accountable.

Megaport's journey has also been rocky. It was suspended in 2008, revived in 2010 at the Pier-2 Art Center, and paused again for a year in 2013 due to internal team changes. After Lin Chang-tso was elected as a legislator in 2016, he stepped away from management, leaving drummer Wang Tzu-hsiang (汪子驤) and Yeh Hsiang-yi to take over. It was suspended again in 2019. The trigger this time was particularly ironic: then-Kuomintang Mayor Han Kuo-yu criticized Megaport as "substandard culture" after seeing Taipei City Councilor Chiu Wei-chieh (呱吉) using profanity on the Megaport stage.

> **✦** When a city's mayor calls his own city's most important music festival "substandard culture," Megaport's response was to revive in 2021, with pre-sale tickets setting a record for the fastest sell-out in Taiwan's music festival history. In 2022, both early bird and pre-sale tickets sold out within ten minutes.

In the 2020s, Megaport Festival has become more than just a music festival. Spanning between the Kaohsiung Music Center and the Pier-2 Art Center, its stages cross indoor venues, harbor piers, and warehouse clusters, featuring rock, electronic, folk, and hip-hop. The 2026 lineup includes Sunset Rollercoaster (落日飛車), [Fire Ex](/en/music/fire-ex/), Blood Yo-Yo (血肉果汁機), and Finnish singer Käärijä—this breadth is Megaport's attitude: as long as it sounds good, anything can happen by the harbor.

Megaport is also one of the few platforms that has successfully transformed _Tâi-gí_ (Taiwanese) from a nostalgic symbol into a contemporary cultural language. [Fire Ex](/en/music/fire-ex/) sings punk in Taiwanese, Pa-sia Boy (拍謝少年) sings post-rock in Taiwanese, and Me Show Group (美秀集團) sings cyber-rock in Taiwanese. These bands are not "preserving a dialect"; they are using their mother tongue to create something entirely new.

Megaport provides them with the loudest megaphone.

## The High Schooler's Festival and Its Bankruptcy

The stories of Megaport and Hohaiyan are about institutions: governments, labels, and famous musicians. But the most incredible chapter of Taiwan's music festival history was written by a group of high school students.

In 2009, Yen Ting-hsien (顏廷憲), a high school student from Chiayi, along with friends from various school music clubs, organized a showcase called "Rise Up Music Festival" (起來音樂祭). No one took it seriously; it was just a term-end party for high school bands. But Yen Ting-hsien thought differently. Year after year, he expanded its scale, renamed it the "Wake Up Festival" (覺醒音樂祭), began inviting international bands, and moved the venue from schools to the Chiayi Cultural and Creative Industries Park.

By the mid-2010s, Wake Up Festival had become one of Taiwan's most internationally significant festivals, listed by the international music media _beehype_ as a recommended global festival. An event organized by a high schooler from Chiayi stood alongside names like Fuji Rock and Primavera Sound.

Then everything collapsed on September 22, 2019. Wake Up Arts Company filed for bankruptcy, with vendors complaining they hadn't received even ten percent of their payments. _Liberty Times_ reported the details of the outstanding debts, and _United Daily News_ tracked the chaotic aftermath of the bankruptcy. This festival, which rose from a high school club leader, was ultimately brought down by cash flow problems.

The story of Wake Up is the perfect microcosm of Taiwan's music festival culture: on this island, any passionate young person can start a music festival from scratch; the barrier to entry is astonishingly low. But because that barrier is so low, the survival rate is also astonishingly low. Passion can spawn a festival, but only operational capability can make it survive past its tenth year.

## Why This Island Needs So Many Music Festivals

Spring Scream in Kenting, Hohaiyan in Gongliao, Megaport in Kaohsiung, Wake Up in Chiayi, Emergence Festival (浮現祭) in Taichung, Fireball Festival (火球祭) in Kaohsiung, Wanderer Festival (浪人祭) in Tainan, Organik Festival on the outlying islands, and JAM JAM ASIA in Taipei. This doesn't even include the small and medium-sized events in various counties and cities.

For an island of 36,000 square kilometers and a population of 23 million, the density of music festivals is abnormal by global standards. The UK has Glastonbury and Reading; Japan has Fuji Rock and Summer Sonic; but few countries can sustain so many festivals with such diverse characters within such a small area.

Why? It isn't because Taiwanese people have an exceptional love for social gatherings. There are three layers to the reason, each pointing to Taiwan's unique historical and geographical conditions.

The first layer is political. After the lifting of martial law in 1987, Taiwanese society had a surge of energy in cultural expression that had been suppressed for decades needing an outlet. Music festivals—especially outdoor, open, and loud ones—provided the most intuitive form of release. Spring Scream was born eight years after martial law ended, Hohaiyan thirteen years later, and Megaport nineteen years later. Each wave of music festivals stepped to the beat of social liberalization. This island spent half a century suppressing sound, then spent thirty years turning the volume up to the maximum.

The second layer is geographical. Taiwan's long, narrow island terrain means every city wants its own cultural calling card. Kaohsiung refused to be just an industrial port, so it got Megaport; Chiayi refused to be just the gateway to Alishan, so it got Wake Up; Tainan wanted Wanderer Festival, and Taichung wanted Emergence Festival. In Taiwan, music festivals are not just cultural activities; they are tools for local revitalization, using three days of music to drive a year's worth of urban identity.

The third layer is ecological. After Taiwan's indie music scene established a complete network of live houses, independent labels, and digital distribution channels in the 2000s, the threshold for "supporting a band" dropped significantly. When thousands of bands are rehearsing simultaneously on an island, they need more than just live houses; they need larger stages. As the number of bands increases, the need for performance venues increases. Music festivals both create demand and respond to it. Thousands of independent musicians on _StreetVoice_ are waiting to be heard, and every new music festival is a new opportunity.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> What is truly special about Taiwan's music festivals is not the quantity, but the diversity. On the same island, there coexist government-hosted free beach parties (Hohaiyan), harbor rock festivals founded by heavy metal musicians (Megaport), international festivals started by high school club leaders (Wake Up), and hipster gatherings combined with beach cleanups (Wanderer Festival). They are not competitors, but different species within the same ecosystem.

## After Stepping Off the Stage

2019 was a watershed year. That year, Spring Scream held its final edition and never returned after leaving Kenting; Wake Up Festival ended in bankruptcy; Megaport Festival announced a suspension (though it later revived in 2021). Three festivals from different generations said goodbye or were interrupted in the same year, like a period at the end of an era.

The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 dealt another blow. All outdoor activities ground to a halt, and Hohaiyan Rock Festival never returned to Fulong Beach. Paradoxically, however, after the pandemic, Taiwan has seen even more music festivals. Young people who were confined to their homes for two years crave standing outdoors and among crowds more than ever. In 2021, the Pingtung County Government launched "Taiwan Festival" (台灣祭) to carry the torch of the Kenting music scene; Wanderer Festival carved out a niche in Tainan's Anping using a model of beach cleanups combined with performances; and JAM JAM ASIA at the Taipei Music Center focused on transnational Asian exchange, drawing over 40,000 spectators on its first day.

The old dies, and the new grows.

This may be the core characteristic of Taiwan's music festival culture: it does not rely on any single brand to survive, because the impulse to hold a festival does not come from commercial calculation, but from a more fundamental need. Young people want to confirm they are alive—outdoors, in a crowd, amidst loud music.

Perhaps the best evidence of the vitality of Taiwan's music festivals is not Megaport's ten-minute sell-outs or JAM JAM ASIA's crowds of 40,000. It is that every spring, in university campuses across Taiwan, there are still countless students and high schoolers, just like Yen Ting-hsien back then, preparing their very first music festival. Some will fail, some will survive a second year, and a tiny few will grow into the next Megaport or Wake Up. But every festival, regardless of scale, is doing the same thing: letting those on stage be heard, and ensuring those in the crowd do not feel alone.

Thirty years ago, when Wade Davis set up a stage on Kenting Beach, there were perhaps a few hundred people in front of him. He and Jimi Moe likely never imagined that this act would proliferate into a collective ritual of over 50 events per year on a single island. Even less likely did they imagine that while they are no longer present, the seed of "singing your own songs loudly outdoors" has grown into a forest.
