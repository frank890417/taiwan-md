# Endemic Species

> **30-Second Overview:** Despite an area of only 36,000 square kilometers, Taiwan nurtures a high density of endemic species: 29 species of birds, 14 mammals, over 35 amphibians, over 70 freshwater fish, approximately 1,000 vascular plants, and more than 56 endemic butterfly species. The Formosan black bear, the Taiwan salmon trout, and the Mikado pheasant are among the most well-known "star" species. The vertical compression of mountains, valleys, and coasts has created this evolutionary laboratory, though habitat destruction and invasive species remain the most pressing threats. [^4]

## Why It Matters

Taiwan's endemic species are vital subjects for global biodiversity research. These unique life forms, evolved over tens of thousands of years, cannot be recreated once extinct. The existence of endemic species also reflects the structural integrity of Taiwan's ecosystems, as each species plays an irreplaceable ecological role in food chains and habitat maintenance.

## Overview

Located at the edge of the Eurasian continental shelf, Taiwan's complex geological history and diverse ecological environments have fostered a high density of endemic species. From sea level to the 3,952-meter summit of Yushan (Jade Mountain), vertical altitudinal changes create tropical, subtropical, temperate, and cold climate zones, providing habitats for different organisms. Endemic species are distributed across various taxa, including mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, insects, and plants, with varying rates of endemism: the rate of endemism in freshwater fish is extremely high, while mammals—due to a smaller base number of species—have limited absolute numbers but also show significant proportions. [^4]

## Key Facts

Broken down by major taxa: 14 endemic mammal species (such as the Formosan black bear and Formosan macaque), 29 endemic bird species (such as the Mikado pheasant, Blue Whiskered Whydah, and Taiwan blue magpie), over 35 amphibian species (such as the Formosan tree frog and Taipei tree frog), over 70 freshwater fish species (such as the Taiwan salmon trout and high-bodied bitterling), approximately 1,000 vascular plant species (such as _Taiwania cryptomerioides_ and Taiwan red cypress), and 56 butterfly species (such as the Wide-tailed swallowtail). Many endemic species have been listed as endangered or critically endangered and urgently require conservation. [^1][^4]

## In-Depth Content

### Island Biogeography Background

**Geological History:** The island of Taiwan was formed by mountain-building movements approximately 5 million years ago. Following the Japanese period (starting in 1895), natural history surveys began to systematically record Taiwan's species; by 1906, Central Mountain Range endemics like the Mikado pheasant were successively named. **Glacial Land Bridges** repeatedly connected and separated the island from the mainland, creating a unique evolutionary environment. **Strait Isolation** provided geographic isolation via the Taiwan Strait, allowing species to evolve independently and develop differences from their mainland relatives.

**Ecological Diversity:** From tropical coastal forests to alpine tundra, Taiwan covers many ecosystems within short distances, providing various evolutionary niches. **Climate Change** throughout history has prompted species to undergo vertical migration and adaptive differentiation.

### Endemic Mammals

The **Formosan black bear** (_Ursus thibetanus formosanus_) is Taiwan's largest terrestrial mammal, characterized by a white V-shaped mark on its chest; the current population is estimated at 200–600 individuals. The **Formosan macaque** is widely distributed across the island and is the only native primate.

The **Formosan wild boar** is smaller than the mainland Asian wild boar, adapted to mountain environments. The **Formosan serow** is skilled at rock climbing and is found in mid-to-high altitude regions. The **Formosan sika deer** is Taiwan's largest herbivore, with males possessing beautiful antlers.

### Endemic Birds

The **Mikado pheasant** (_Syrmaticus mikado_) is an endemic pheasant species of Taiwan. Males possess magnificent blue-black plumage and white-banded tail feathers; it is a precious representative bird of Taiwan and is featured on the NT$1,000 banknote. [^1] The **Blue Whiskered Whydah** is found at mid-to-low altitudes, with males featuring blue bellies and red wattles. The **Taiwan blue magpie** has sapphire-blue feathers and a long tail, making it one of Taiwan's most beautiful birds.

Other members of the Leiothrichidae family, such as the **Taiwan laughingthrush** and **Taiwan babbler**, demonstrate the diversity of birds in Taiwan's mountains.

### Endemic Fish

The **Taiwan salmon trout** (_Oncorhynchus masou formosanus_) is a precious relic from the Ice Age, surviving only in the upper reaches of the Dajia River, and is referred to as a "national treasure fish." Freshwater fish such as the **Taiwan white-armored fish** and **Taiwan stone loach** demonstrate the uniqueness of the island's river systems.

**Marine endemic species** include various coral reef fish and deep-sea species, reflecting the biogeographical specificity of Taiwan's waters.

### Endemic Plants

**_Taiwania cryptomerioides_** is a world-class precious conifer; trees can reach heights of 70 to 84 meters, making it Taiwan's tallest native conifer species. [^2] **Taiwan red cypress** and **Hinoki cypress** form precious cypress forest ecosystems. Species like **Taiwan camphor** and **_Taiwania_** varieties demonstrate the uniqueness of Taiwan's flora.

**Alpine plants**, such as _Primula yushanica_ and _Juniperus morrisonicola_, have adapted to harsh high-altitude environments. The proportion of endemic **orchid species** is extremely high among Taiwan's Orchidaceae, with many species possessing significant horticultural value.

### Endemic Insects

Taiwan is a paradise for endemic insects. The **Wide-tailed swallowtail** (_Agehana maraho_) is a rare endemic butterfly with a wingspan of about 9 to 10 cm; it is distributed only in the mid-altitude mountains of central and northern Taiwan, using the Taiwan alder as its sole host plant. [^5] Beetles such as the **Taiwan long-armed scarab** and **rainbow stag beetle** demonstrate unique evolutionary adaptations.

**Butterfly Diversity:** Taiwan has recorded approximately 400 butterfly species, over 50 of which are endemic. The number of **endemic moth species** is even larger, with many species yet to be scientifically described.

### Conservation Status and Challenges

**Habitat Destruction:** Human activities such as urban development, agricultural expansion, and road construction threaten endemic habitats. **Climate Change** causes rising temperatures that force alpine species to migrate to higher altitudes, eventually potentially leaving them with no habitat available.

**Invasive Species:** Alien species like _Mikania micrantha_ and golden apple snails compete with native species for resources. **Over-development** of tourism can interfere with wildlife behavior.

### Invasive Species: The Invisible War of Extinction

The scale of the invasive species problem in Taiwan is massive. The Forestry and Nature Conservation Agency manages over 50 invasive plant species and over 30 invasive animal species. [^3] The most severe cases include:

**_Mikania micrantha_** (mile-a-minute weed) is the fastest-spreading invasive plant in Taiwan's mountains, entangling native vegetation at a rate of several meters per week; since the 2000s, it has invaded mid-altitude forest edges. The **Golden apple snail** (_Pomacea canaliculata_) was introduced from Argentina for food in 1979; after being abandoned, it exploded in populations across Taiwan's rice paddies and wetlands, destroying rice crops and consuming native aquatic plants. The **Invasive red fire ant** (_Solenopsis invicta_) was discovered in Taoyuan in 2003 and has since spread to many counties, directly threatening the foraging and nesting behaviors of soil-dwelling animals (including some ground-nesting birds).

The core difficulty in controlling invasive species is that once a population is established, it is difficult to eradicate; they can only be managed, not eliminated, and management costs rise exponentially over time. The Ministry of Agriculture allocates hundreds of millions of NT$ annually for invasive species management, and citizen reporting systems (Invasive Species Reporting Platform) have been integrated into the monitoring network of the Forestry and Nature Conservation Agency. [^3]

### Conservation Efforts

**National Park System:** National parks such as Yushan, Taroko, and Siba protect important endemic habitats. **Protected Area Networks** include nature reserves and wildlife refuges that provide legal protection.

**Restoration Programs:** The _Wildlife Conservation Act_ (1989) laid the legal foundation, leading to subsequent projects like the Taiwan salmon trout restoration and Formosan black bear population monitoring. **Citizen Science** platforms like eBird and iNaturalist promote public participation in biological surveys.

On **August 1, 2023**, the Ministry of Agriculture was officially established. The former Forestry Bureau of the Council of Agriculture was upgraded to the **Forestry and Nature Conservation Agency**, integrating forest resources and biodiversity conservation functions to formally bring endemic species conservation into a more complete legal framework. [^3] In the same year, the former Species Research Institute of the Council of Agriculture was restructured into the **Institute of Biodiversity, Ministry of Agriculture**, specializing in biodiversity survey, monitoring, and research, becoming the core institution for consolidating Taiwan's endemic species data. **International Cooperation** with organizations like IUCN and TRAFFIC enhances conservation technology. **Environmental Education** increases conservation awareness through ecotourism and citizen science.

## Global Significance

The experience of conserving Taiwan's endemic species serves as a reference for island ecosystem conservation worldwide. Taiwan possesses both the vertical compression of mountain-to-lowland terrain and a tropical-to-cold climate gradient, making it an advantageous field site for studying island biogeography and evolutionary biology.
