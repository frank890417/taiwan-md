# Malayan Night Heron: A Birder's Dream Species, the "Clumsy Bird" and "Sweet Potato Bird" of Campus

> **30-Second Overview:** Between 1985 and 1992, the Malayan Night Heron (_Gorsachius melanolophus_) was a "rare resident," accounting for only 25 out of 4,000 bird records in Taiwan. Today, however, it is seen daily in the Coconut Grove of National Taiwan University (NTU) and Da'an Forest Park. Ding Tsung-su, an associate professor in the Department of Forestry at NTU, describes it as a "elusive dream species" for birdwatchers in Southeast Asia; "Since thirty years ago, for some reason, Taiwan's Malayan Night Herons have started to become bold." An explanation closer to the truth points elsewhere: the densifying greenery and pesticide-free lawns of Taiwan's urban campuses have replicated its original low-altitude forest habitat. The bird hasn't changed; the land has.

## A Dream Species in Southeast Asia, a Regular in Taipei's Coconut Grove

In June 1865, in Tamsui, British naturalist Robert Swinhoe collected a specimen of the Malayan Night Heron. His record contained two words: "rare," followed by a vocal characteristic—"calls like a duck"[^1]. One hundred and twenty years later, this assessment still holds. From 1985 to 1992, the Bird Conservation Society of Taiwan database accumulated approximately 4,000 bird records, of which the Malayan Night Heron accounted for only **25 entries**[^2]. Field guides at the time listed it as a "rare resident," primarily inhabiting pristine broadleaf forests at low altitudes.

By the 2010s, the situation for this species had completely reversed. Associate Professor Ding Tsung-su of NTU's Department of Forestry explains that in Southeast Asia, night herons are actually very skittish birds with elusive movements—a "dream species" for birdwatchers. Since thirty years ago, Taiwan's Malayan Night Herons have become bold, even walking brazenly along urban roadsides, much to the amazement of foreign researchers[^3]. "As animals become bolder, humans must be more friendly so that the distance between them can decrease," Ding wrote in the NTU campus newsletter[^3].

The question of "for some reason" is what this article seeks to unpack.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The common popular science explanation online is: "Malayan Night Herons have adapted to the city and become unafraid of humans." While narratively convenient, this reverses cause and effect. The neural reflexes of Ardeidae (heron family) birds would not evolve an entirely new "human-indifference" trait within thirty years; a more accurate explanation is that Taipei's green spaces have quietly transformed since the 1990s into functional replicas of low-altitude forests. The bird hasn't changed; the land has.

## From 25 Records to 9,000

Expansion did not happen uniformly. Sightings began to rise in the 1990s and accelerated after 2000[^4]. National Chung Hsing University first recorded a pair of breeding adults in 1994; the university attributed this to an "environment rich in earthworms" brought about by its "pesticide-free" policy and long-term management as an "organic ecological campus." By 2014, the campus population was estimated to have reached over 30 individuals[^5].

The team that truly brought this population into focus is led by Yuan Hsiao-wei of NTU. Starting in 2010, Yuan began systematic banding on campus, initially establishing a baseline observation sample of 25 birds across 8 nests, and used color-coded rings to track inter-city movements[^2]. In 2015, the team established the "Malayan Night Heron Story House" Facebook page, and in 2018, launched a color-ring query and reporting platform, turning "where was this specific bird seen" into a long-term record that citizens across Taiwan can participate in[^4]. Taiwan is currently the only place within the global distribution range of this species to have a complete citizen science banding and reporting system.

If one looks only at the expansion curve, it is a story of successful adaptation. But "successful adaptation" is a conclusion, not a mechanism. To understand the mechanism, we must dig deeper.

## Camphor and Banyan Trees: 5-Meter-High Low-Altitude Forests

![A side view of a Malayan Night Heron foraging intently in an urban green space in Kaohsiung, head down and neck tilted forward, next to a manicured lawn](/article-images/nature/malayan-night-heron-kaohsiung-foraging-2026.webp)
_March 2026, a Malayan Night Heron foraging in an urban green space in Kaohsiung. From its expansion in Taipei to southern Taiwan, urban parks have become its food supply stations over the past thirty years. Photo: The Nature Box. [Public domain (CC0) via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Malayan_night_heron_foraging_Kaohsiung_Taiwan_2026-03-28_0846.jpg)._

The Malayan Night Heron requires two things: high tree canopies for nesting and an open understory for foraging with its head down. A summary of breeding season surveys in several major Taipei parks (Da'an Forest, Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall, Youth Park, NTU, and 228 Peace Memorial Park) shows that Malayan Night Heron nests are preferred at tree trunk forks with near-horizontal angles, and canopy coverage is typically above 70%[^6]. Yuan Hsiao-wei's NTU survey also noted that banyan trees are the preferred nesting trees, emphasizing that the ratio of trees to grass is key to selecting activity areas—neither too dense nor too sparse[^7].

This is precisely a byproduct of Taipei's greening efforts. Da'an Forest Park (opened in 1994), the camphor and banyan street trees planted by various universities after the 1990s, and the mature tree zones around Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall matured into a mixed structure of "dense canopy + open lawns + leaf litter + no pesticides" by the 2000s. Ecologically, this structure is a slice of a low-altitude broadleaf forest—just cut into long strips and laid between concrete.

The second key is food. Earthworms are an uncontested gold mine in pesticide-free green spaces. Yuan Hsiao-wei stated bluntly in an interview with PTS:

> "It has a bit of an exclusive monopoly on this urban resource."[^3]

Birds that rely primarily on earthworms are rare in Taiwan. The Malayan Night Heron's competitors are almost exclusively other small terrestrial birds that eat earthworms, but these rarely forage at the edges of large park lawns. A 2011 dietary study by Nakamura et al. in the Yaeyama Islands made an interesting finding: when analyzing the diet of Malayan Night Herons via pellets, earthworms left **no residue at all** because they are so completely digested—meaning the land snails, spiders, freshwater crabs, and insects seen in pellets are just the tip of the iceberg; the true proportion of earthworms may be much higher but is simply unquantifiable[^8].

Predator pressure has also reversed. The predation pressure from Crested Goshawks found in original habitats has vanished in the city; instead, the presence of humans in urban areas deters some predators. Multiple urban breeding season surveys point to the same direction: the breeding success rate of Malayan Night Herons in Taipei's urban parks reaches **95.7%**, with both hatching and chick survival rates exceeding 90%[^6]. Compared to their original forest habitats, this is a massive discrepancy.

## Two Misinterpretations of "Stiff-Necked Standing"

"Standing still" is the pose by which humans most recognize the Malayan Night Heron. When a student approaches, it does not fly; instead, it stretches its neck straight toward the sky and remains motionless until the person passes—this is the live scene of the "one-two-three wooden bird" legend passed down on Taiwanese campuses.

This posture has a technical name in ecology: **freezing posture** or **bittern posture**, a common anti-predator instinct shared by all birds in the Ardeidae family. The standard movement of the American Bittern (beak pointing up, feathers compressed, slow swaying to track an intruder) can be maintained for hours, allowing it to blend invisibly into reed beds[^9]. The Malayan Night Heron inherits this same genetic expression.

The problem is that NTU's Coconut Grove has no reeds. In a 2018 in-depth report, _WoWo_ (窩窩) wrote bluntly: "When it stands still, it is actually simulating vegetation, using mimicry to deceive predators"[^10]. It isn't unafraid of you—**it thinks you cannot see it**, using an instinct unchanged for tens of thousands of years, misplaced in a concrete environment.

The second common misinterpretation is the "neck vibration" during foraging. Taiwanese popular science articles often cite the claim that "Malayan Night Herons vibrate their necks to lure out earthworms." However, returning to primary research literature, this hypothesis currently **lacks peer-reviewed sources**. What can be confirmed is another matter: after pulling out an earthworm, the Malayan Night Heron vigorously vibrates its neck to snap the worm's head before swallowing, and uses a serpentine movement to push the long prey into its esophagus—this is swallow-assistance, not luring[^10].

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> All members of the Ardeidae family perform the "freezing posture," but each species is misplaced in different environments: the American Bittern lives in marsh reeds, where this movement provides perfect camouflage; the Malayan Night Heron is placed on urban lawns, where the same movement turns it into a "clumsy bird." **The action isn't wrong; the stage is.**

## Post-Rain Campuses, Emerging Earthworms, and Turning Motorcycles

The flip side of expansion is new ways to die.

On rainy days, earthworms emerge, and Malayan Night Herons stand at the edges of campus lawns to forage intently. At these times, campus bicycle paths, alleys used by motorcycles, and back gates open to cars all become lethal routes. The NTU research team found that roadkill incidents are common on campus paths and alleys, leading students to launch a movement for "slow-traffic signage"[^11]. The organization _roadkill.tw_ has listed the Malayan Night Heron as a focal species[^12].

Rodenticides are an even more invisible killer. All rodenticides approved in Taiwan are anticoagulants, causing animals to die from internal bleeding within 5 to 7 days. Cumulative testing from three institutions, including the Wildlife Emergency Station at National Pingtung University of Science and Technology, indicates that **over half of the raptors in Taiwan have tested positive for rodenticide**[^13]. While the Malayan Night Heron is not a raptor, it preys on small rodents and theoretically faces the same risk of secondary poisoning—it is just that public detection statistics currently remain at the raptor level, and data on drug residues in Malayan Night Herons has not yet been systematically released.

Records of human-inflicted harm are disturbingly specific. In 2019 in Tainan, a Malayan Night Heron was found with its beak halved, flight feathers clipped, and its right leg broken, abandoned in a park[^10]. There have also been incidents at NTU's Department of Horticulture where grandparents used BB guns to shoot nestlings or used cloth bags to capture breeding adults. Rescue statistics from the bird societies in Taipei and Tainan indicate: "The proportion of individuals judged capable of flying, foraging independently, and being released into the wild is only between 20% and 30%"[^10].

Habitat loss is the least noticed but most critical pressure. The disappearance of adjacent green space following the demolition of the Jinghua City project has triggered territorial disputes among Malayan Night Herons; similarly, a rehabilitation green space in the Songshan area disappeared due to development, causing the local population to drop from "several seen daily" to "it's good if you see two occasionally"[^10]. These cases serve as a reminder: after entering the city, the Malayan Night Heron has not become a super-adapted urban winner; it relies on an anthropogenic habitat that was formed by chance and is easily disrupted.

## The Reverse Onomastics of the "Clumsy Bird"

Almost everyone on Taiwanese campuses calls it the "clumsy bird," the "one-two-three wooden bird," or the "sweet potato bird." A 2024 report by _China Times_ noted the origin of this nickname: "Because they appear frequently in campuses and parks, people are often asked 'What kind of bird is this?'"[^14]. The title of _WoWo_'s 2018 in-depth article was directly: 〈The Clumsy Bird is Actually Not Clumsy at All〉—the failure rate of sit-and-wait predators is extremely low; the Malayan Night Heron's gaze is sharp and can sense ground vibrations from earthworms, so it shouldn't be called "clumsy" by any measure[^10].

Researchers dislike this nickname. The title of Yuan Hsiao-wei's 2015 lecture on PTS's _Humanities Lecture Hall_ framed the issue as "The Little Nature in Everyone's Heart"—observing urban green spaces is the gateway for urban children to first encounter wildlife, which is where interest in deeper forest conservation begins[^15].

Translating "clumsy bird" into "Campus Animal Ambassador" represents the reverse value of this nickname—a bird that looks a bit dazed and won't fly away when walking by a road is precisely the gateway for urban children to first encounter wildlife. The Taipei City Department of Urban Animal Protection and the NTU team co-organized a "Malayan Night Heron Reporting Contest," encouraging citizens to upload sighting locations and use color-ring combinations to query individuals[^16]. Teachers from kindergarten to middle school have written them into biodiversity lesson plans, using their foraging behavior as standard teaching material for campus ecological observation[^17].

On June 29, 2020, PTS's _Our Island_ aired episode 1061, 〈[The Distance Between Us and the Malayan Night Heron | A Rare Resident Taking Root in Taiwan](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NFlfKHDWnOE)〉, bringing the story of this bird to mainstream media. Taiwan is currently the only place within the global distribution range of this species (which includes India, China, southern Japan, the Philippines, and Southeast Asia) to have a complete citizen science banding and reporting system[^3].

## Conclusion

Expansion is not necessarily adaptation.

From a rare resident in the late 1980s to a campus daily in the 2010s, the Malayan Night Heron seems to have conquered the city, but it relies on a city that has been coincidentally shaped into a low-altitude forest—dense camphor and banyan trees, pesticide-free lawns, a breeding environment without natural predators, and an exclusive resource of earthworms. If any one of these conditions is removed (roadkill, rodenticides, habitat loss), this breeding success rate of over 90% will plummet.

So next time you see it standing still in the Coconut Grove, remember that it isn't "unafraid of you." It thinks you cannot see it—using an instinct unchanged for tens of thousands of years. It is the city that has quietly become like its original home, not the bird that has ceased to be a forest bird.

One hundred and sixty years ago, Swinhoe wrote the word "rare" in Tamsui. Today, we hear the low "wuh, wuh, wuh" calls every day in Da'an Forest Park. What happened in between is the story of how an island preserves a small patch of moist understory amidst the concrete.
