> **30-Second Overview:** _Palakaw_ is the millennial ecological fishing wisdom passed down by the Amis people of the Fataan tribe in Hualien. By utilizing bamboo tubes, tree branches, and aquatic plants, the tribespeople construct a "three-story apartment" within the wetlands to simulate natural habitats and attract aquatic life. This method of "catching without hunting" is a pioneer of artificial reef technology in Taiwan and serves as a global model for Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK)—a sustainable contract of symbiosis between humans and nature.

"This is a home for the fish, not discarded waste." In the Fataan (馬太鞍) wetlands of Guangfu Township, Hualien, elders often point to the seemingly cluttered piles of wood and bamboo tubes along the riverbanks to explain the essence of _Palakaw_ (巴拉告) to the younger generation. In the Amis language, "_Lakaw_" originally refers to waste or trash in daily life; however, by adding the verbal prefix "_Pa_", the term transforms into an act of active protection—"to make a home for fish and shrimp" [^1].

The Fataan wetlands possess a unique spring-fed environment, where the perennial flow of the Fudeng River (芙登溪) nurtures a rich variety of freshwater fish and shrimp. Legend has it that the river deity of Fataan, _Idek_ (伊得), taught the people how to fish and farm; although the deity is described as possessing boundless power and a mischievous temperament, the wetland environment left behind became the foundation for the people's survival [^2]. Unlike the proactive hunting styles of maritime peoples, the Amis people residing here developed an "ecological engineering method" that is more subtle and rooted in a spirit of circulation.

📝 Curator's Note: The most fascinating aspect of _Palakaw_ lies in its semantic shift—transforming "waste" into a "home." This transformation is not just a material cycle, but a crucial mental shift from being a "predator" to becoming a "builder."

## The Fish's Three-Story Apartment: Precise Habitat Simulation

The core structure of _Palakaw_ is a rigorous three-story "apartment," where each level is designed according to the habits of specific species, transforming discarded natural materials into vibrant artificial reefs [^3]. Researchers consider this technique a precursor to modern artificial reef technology in Taiwan, existing long before the Dutch period [^4].

| Floor            | Primary Materials                                    | Residents                                       | Ecological Characteristics                                                          |
| :--------------- | :--------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bottom Layer** | Large bamboo tubes or hollow wood segments           | Eels, various freshwater eels, mudfish, catfish | Benthic fish that prefer dark, stable, and sheltered spaces.                        |
| **Middle Layer** | Bundles of _jiu-xiong_ (九芎) branches or fine twigs | Small fish, small shrimp                        | Dense crevices provide protection from predators while fostering biofilms for food. |
| **Top Layer**    | Thin bamboo tubes, aquatic plants, betel nut leaves  | Tilapia, various stream fish                    | Shades the sunlight, providing a cool and stable floating space.                    |

The operational logic of this system is extremely sophisticated: the large bamboo tubes at the bottom provide a sanctuary for scaleless fish; the _jiu-xiong_ branches in the middle act as a natural nursery, allowing small fish and shrimp to grow safely within the fragmented spaces; and the betel nut leaves and aquatic plants covering the top layer block harsh sunlight and regulate water temperature [^5]. Even more ingenious is that waste from the fish on the upper layer falls through the levels, becoming food for the small fish and shrimp in the middle, creating a miniature food chain cycle [^6].

## Catching Without Hunting: A Philosophy of Moderation

Once this "apartment" has been placed in a pond for a period, fish and shrimp will naturally migrate there to settle. When it comes time to harvest, the tribespeople do not need to go through the trouble of draining the pond; they simply move the middle-layer branches, and the aquatic life is revealed. At this point, the people use traditional fish baskets (_kanas_) or nets (_cadiway_) for collection [^7].

📝 Curator's Note: In an era where modern fisheries pursue efficiency, _Palakaw_ reminds us of the power of "slowness." It is not about chasing the fish, but making them want to move in.

The essence of this fishing method lies in "moderation." The Amis people follow the principle of taking only what they need to eat. Furthermore, the structure of _Palakaw_ itself functions as a filter—small fish and shrimp can easily escape through the gaps between the branches, while only mature individuals are caught [^8]. This selective harvesting ensures that the population's reproduction is not interrupted, achieving true sustainable utilization.

## From "Place of Tears" to a Global Ecological Model

In the Amis language, besides referring to the _Fataan_ (樹豆) plant, the name "Fataan" also carries the connotation of a "place where tears gather" in certain legends, symbolizing the hardships faced by the people during their migrations and their deep emotional connection to the land [^10]. Today, the _Palakaw_ on this land has transformed from a traditional fishing method into a powerful tool for modern ecological conservation.

From the perspective of modern ecology, _Palakaw_ is regarded as a masterpiece of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) [^11]. In 2021, the _Palakaw_ techniques of the Fataan tribe were officially granted "Indigenous Traditional Wisdom Creation Copyright" protection, signifying that this wisdom has become a national-level cultural asset [^12]. Even government agencies must obtain authorization to use this traditional technology when conducting river restoration projects [^13].

On the international stage, _Palakaw_ has also garnered widespread attention. The 2026 International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Congress will feature it as a key case study of cross-cultural fishing technology, exploring how indigenous wisdom can provide solutions for wetland restoration under modern climate change [^14]. Academic research indicates that this model, which integrates local culture with environmental knowledge, is key to the sustainable development of local cultural industries [^15].

📝 Curator's Note: While the world discusses the "Satoyama Initiative," the people of Fataan have been practicing it for centuries. _Palakaw_ is simultaneously a fishing method and a complete system of water resource management and social organization.

To the people of Fataan, _Palakaw_ is a way of conversing with the land. It reminds us that before humans demand from nature, they must first learn how to give [^16]. As long as the fish's home remains, the vitality of the stream will not run dry. This wisdom of coexistence with nature continues to transmit its profound influence to the world through modern environmental education and ecotourism [^17][^18].
