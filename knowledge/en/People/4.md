> **30-second overview:** In 2015, _Thursday 4 Super Play_ premiered on McBee Live; the first "One Day" episode appeared in 2016, and a separate YouTube channel was launched in 2017. The most interesting aspect of the show isn't sending James Tsai, KID, or Winnie to work for a day — it's placing craftsmen, aides, and behind-the-scenes staff who are usually never cast as protagonists into a scene visible to viewers across all of Taiwan. Thursday appears to be turning stars into ordinary people, but in reality it's turning the invisible expertise of ordinary jobs into a mainstream show.

When James Tsai first climbed a utility pole, the real difficulty wasn't whether to be funny on camera. The wire at the Taiwan Power Company training center, he cut more than ten times before succeeding. The high-voltage tower required wearing safety gear and climbing step by step along the pegs. _Taiwan Power Monthly_ later documented the shoot, recalling how he changed his work clothes two or three times due to sweat.

This is an ideal object for understanding _Thursday 4 Super Play_: a pair of scissors that can't be cut through, rather than a beautiful view count. The show places stars at job sites, but this doesn't make the work any easier. On the contrary, the stars' clumsiness gives shape to expertise that is usually hidden by daily routines.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Thursday's most valuable "special effect" is often the host's first attempt at failure. Failures aren't edited into a single motivational slogan, but left with the feeling of swollen fingers, inability to climb, and having to start over.

## From Live-Streaming Platforms to "One Day" Experiments

_Thursday 4 Super Play_ didn't start out knowing what it would become. National Taiwan Normal University research places it within the "post-television" context: the show simultaneously carries television variety production experience, McBee's live-streaming platform, and the format of YouTube videos that viewers can actively rewatch and discuss. The research also points out that live-streaming chat and sponsorship interactions allow audiences not only to watch the show but gradually form a "Thursday" community.[^2]

McBee general manager Liao Chi-chung recalled in an interview with _Manager_ that McBee started from online live-streaming in 2013, and _Thursday 4 Super Play_ premiered in 2015. The team once tried live-streaming but found that artists aren't necessarily suited to handing every second over to viewers. Later, they inserted pre-recorded VCR segments into the live stream, letting James Tsai return to the more familiar edited variety show rhythm.[^3]

This turning point was crucial. Thursday didn't simply move television unchanged to YouTube, nor did it discard all television experience. It preserved the sense of scheduled programming, then used the conditions of online video — extended lifespan, repeated viewing, and real-time response — to gradually find its rhythm.

On October 20, 2016, the first "One Day" episode appeared. In February 2017, the team launched the _Thursday 4 Super Play_ YouTube channel. Early planning didn't go viral from the start, but gradually tested whether audiences would watch someone complete an entire work process, starting with "One Day as an Athlete," one day with variety show staff, and one day at a convenience store.[^4]

## One Episode as a Mayor's Aide — Not Just Political Promotion

On July 5, 2018, James Tsai followed Mayor Ko Wen-sheng from 6:30 AM until evening, producing a nearly one-hour _One Day as a Mayor's Aide_. The video surpassed 3 million views in less than four days after going live. The official _Thursday 4 Super Play_ video page still retains the original 58:15 length.[^5] [^6]

The numbers are eye-catching, but what's more worth watching is how the filming power was exchanged. When interviewed by the Taipei City Government team, they said they chose to let the mayor follow the existing culture and units of the YouTuber, rather than letting the government lead the filming approach. This statement is both a publicity strategy and a risk acknowledgment: once a political figure steps into a web show, they can no longer fully control who the audience sees as the protagonist.[^7]

The video let viewers see the mayor taking the bus, attending meetings, and running errands, and also made aides Huang Tsai-hsing, Ko Yu-an, and Liu Yi-hsin visible. However, one shouldn't equate high view counts directly with successful public communication. It's still content jointly produced by the city government and the show team; viewers might be drawn to the political figure, or simply want to see if James Tsai can make it through the day until he gets off work. Thursday's influence lies in this hybrid: it's not a news interview, yet it can turn political daily life into something many people are willing to spend 58 minutes watching.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** The most counterintuitive part of _One Day as a Mayor's Aide_ isn't that internet celebrities can get close to politics, but that politics must first accept a viewing approach not centered on official documents before it has a chance to be seen.

_Manager_ records that Ko Yu-an believed filming should start from the citizens' perspective. The same report also preserved Liu Yi-hsin's comment about "walking a tightrope" in online promotion. Putting these voices together reveals that Thursday didn't eliminate the boundary between political promotion and entertainment content — it just moved the boundary to the audience's side.[^7]

## Making Power Company Employees Characters in the Story

If _One Day as a Mayor's Aide_ brings public power into web variety, then _One Day as a Power Company Employee_ brings infrastructure into viewers' living rooms. _Taiwan Power Monthly_ reports that the collaborative video reached 1 million views in ten days of release, and exceeded 1.41 million by January 2019. The timing and sources of these numbers are clear, so they shouldn't be rewritten as an eternal "million-view myth."

The shoot initially took place at the Da-tan Power Plant, but the production team found that the automated equipment was highly professional yet hard to let viewers see the physical burden of frontline work. So the shoot moved to the Kaohsiung Training Center, where James Tsai started by cutting wires and then attempted to climb high-voltage towers. The official Taiwan Power Company article specifically emphasized full safety gear and occupational safety requirements, reminding us: the dangers shown in the program can't be treated as mere punchlines — they must be supported by professional standards.

_One Day as a Power Company Employee_ still carries the image that the collaborating party hopes to convey, and shouldn't be misread as completely neutral labor documentation. But the show at least lets audiences see a profession that usually only appears during power outages: power company employees aren't abstract figures in electricity policy debates, but people who cut wires, climb towers, and enter emergency response centers before typhoons.

This is what researchers call "social perspective": the subjects of the One Day series aren't necessarily more unfamiliar than traditional television, but the difference is that the host must actually enter the work process, placing the craftspeople's demonstrations, the host's failures, and post-production rhythm into a single narrative.[^2]

## The Laughter of Variety and the Labor Behind the Scenes

At the end of 2018, the show appeared at YouTube FanFest, and _Tech New Media_ recorded James Tsai's self-naming as "YArtist." This term is funny but also precise: he didn't transform from a TV star into a completely different person, but moved his existing performance skills to a platform that requires continuously maintaining audience relationships.[^8]

So the real protagonist of the show isn't just in front of the camera. Research shows that Thursday's production team mixes television and new media experience. _Manager_ records the team's journey from live-streaming failures, difficulty finding advertisers, to building a stable audience through original planning. These data all point to a less romantic but more reliable answer: the "natural feel" of web shows requires a lot of production decisions behind the scenes.[^2] [^3]

They also don't turn every profession into an unboxing video. Liao Chi-chung said in an interview that planning must find the hardship or novelty behind the work, allowing audiences to feel it. For companies, a beautiful factory isn't sufficient reason — whether strangers can feel that this job is worth understanding is the real question of whether the "One Day" format can stand.[^3]

In 2020, _CommonWealth Magazine English_ discussed Taiwan's YouTube monetization, mentioning a video with 2.88 million views from Thursday's collaboration with "this group of people," also reminding us that web creators face highly unstable attention and income structures. This English report isn't a Thursday exclusive interview, but provides an important external measure: a video with millions of views doesn't mean the team has stable content production conditions.[^9]

## After Going Viral — Can the Show Stay?

_Online Thermometer_ used KEYPO to monitor websites, social media platforms, discussion forums, and blog texts from December 2018 to December 2021, compiling the One Day series topics with higher audience discussion. The article itself also clarifies that the rankings represent online buzz, not voting or positive/negative evaluations.[^10] This limitation cannot be omitted, because being discussed and being liked are two different things.

Later, the show faced another problem: how to continue a show that grew through team chemistry after organizational changes. In a 2023 _Manager_ updated article, Chen Pai-hsiung stated that the company reorganized due to policy, with some people laid off and others leaving because they couldn't accept the arrangement; the production team has left the company. The article also records that Liao Chi-chung led the subsequent One Day series shoots. What can be confirmed here is the organizational change, and we cannot write unmentioned departure reasons for any individual.[^11]

This ending makes the words "Thursday spirit" dangerous. If the show's success is attributed to a particular host's personality, it overlooks planning, cinematography, editing, collaborating parties, and craftspeople. If success is attributed to a copyable formula, it pretends that team reorganization won't change the show's flavor. What Thursday leaves behind isn't a "internet celebrity success" answer, but a question: when audiences love the sight of a group of people working together, how does the show rebuild trust after that group changes?

## The Production Craft of the One Day Series: Breaking "Work" into Observable Steps

The One Day series appears to be one person doing a day's work, but in reality it contains at least four production levels. The first level is occupational selection: the subject must have a sense of unfamiliarity, yet not be completely unrelated to viewers' lives. The second level is on-site instruction: the host doesn't directly obtain a job title, but must first accept tool, safety, and process training. The third level is failure and retry: the host's clumsiness provides humor, and also makes the craftspeople's expertise visible. The fourth level is post-production, compressing long work time into a narrative that viewers are still willing to follow. Both research and production team interviews point out that Thursday's format was gradually explored between live-streaming, pre-recording, interaction, and commercial pressure, rather than having a complete formula from the start.[^2] [^3]

These four levels also explain why a simple "unboxing" is not enough. Unboxing usually only needs to display the object. The One Day series requires the show to put the object back into a set of work relationships: who teaches the host, who checks safety, who cleans up the failures, and who continues the work after the host leaves. When discussing planning, Liao Chi-chung emphasized that the products or venues provided by the company can't be filmed just because "they think it's beautiful," but must let external viewers feel the hardship, novelty, or universal value of the work.[^3]

| Show level                | What audiences see                                           | Originally easily overlooked work                                  |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Occupational selection    | A strange or rarely filmed profession                        | How planners judge whether the subject has public interest         |
| On-site training          | Host handling tools, wearing gear, listening to instructions | Craftspeople's teaching, occupational safety, and risk management  |
| Failure and retry         | Clumsiness, mistakes, being corrected, and re-challenging    | Expertise is the result of long-term training, not innate reaction |
| Post-production narrative | Humor, rhythm, and a complete story in one episode           | How editing decides which labor is kept and which is omitted       |

Therefore, Thursday's "natural" isn't without design, but design hidden where audiences don't need to pay attention. Audiences see James Tsai failing to cut wires, but usually don't think about camera positions, safety personnel's judgments, on-site waiting time, or how editing turns repeated failures into an understandable segment. It's precisely this hidden production labor that makes the work in front of the camera look like it happens naturally.

## View Counts Are Not the Same Thing

_One Day as a Mayor's Aide_ surpassed 3 million views in less than four days after going live in 2018 — this was the speed record at the time of the event; the 58:15 on the official video page is the length of the work itself; _Online Thermometer_'s KEYPO compilation of discussion volume over a period represents the degree to which it was discussed online; _CommonWealth Magazine English_ mentioned a collaborative video with 2.88 million views, which is an external review in a 2020 article. These numbers may all be true, but they answer different questions, and cannot be mixed into a dateless "Thursday has many views."[^5] [^6] [^9] [^10]

This distinction is important for understanding internet culture. View counts are platform counts, buzz is data analysis results, on-site reactions are media reports, and public communication is an even harder-to-measure effect. When a political collaboration video quickly surpasses millions of views, at most it can be said to have successfully gained attention; whether viewers thus understand municipal policies, support policies, or change their voting judgments — existing sources don't provide sufficient causal evidence. On the contrary, the reminder about "walking a tightrope" in the report exactly shows that both sides of the collaboration know that entertainment effects and public persuasion are not the same.[^7]

## Collaboration Doesn't Mean Neutrality: Reading the Sources of the One Day Series

Thursday's craftspeople videos often need government agencies, companies, or event organizers to provide venues and personnel. This allows the show to obtain job sites that ordinary production teams can't access, and gives collaborating parties the opportunity to explain their image. _Taiwan Power Monthly_ describes _One Day as a Power Company Employee_ from the perspective of public service; the Taipei City Government Public Works Department's Park Streetlight Engineering Office records _One Day as a Streetlight Repair Worker_ from the perspective of streetlight maintenance and citizen safety. These primary articles are important, but shouldn't be treated as completely neutral third-party observations.[^12]

A more reliable reading isn't to exclude the collaborating sources, but to ask three questions at the same time: who provided the filming conditions? What does the article want readers to see? What still hasn't been filmed? Taking streetlight repair as an example, the official article clearly writes about pre-shift training, tools, safety measures, the 1999 hotline, daytime patrols, and night emergency repairs; this makes the profession's process concrete, but it's still an article published by the collaborating agency, and can't represent all streetlight repair workers' experiences alone.[^12]

This reading method also applies to _One Day as a Mayor's Aide_. The show brought the mayor's work to the audience's eyes, and the Taipei City Government team publicly explained that they hoped the mayor would follow the YouTuber's existing culture and units, rather than letting the government lead the content. Both are real parts: the show did obtain a special viewing entrance, and the government did need to face the interpretive risks brought by entertainment. Saying one side manipulates and the other is pure only weakens the complexity worth preserving in this episode.[^7]

## Why "One Day" Is Easier to Remember Than a Simple Interview

Interviews can let craftspeople explain their work, but the One Day series lets audiences see how the explanation happens physically. Will the gear be too hot, how far the ladder needs to be carried, how many times the line fault needs to be checked, and when the day's schedule ends — these aren't information that can be replaced by a single "the job is hard." Both the Taiwan Power Company and streetlight repair cases show that the show breaks work into a series of observable actions, giving audiences the opportunity to connect abstract hardship back to concrete scenes.[^12]

On the other hand, this format also has clear boundaries. The host only works one day, but the craftspeople must bear the consequences long-term; the camera can stop before the danger increases, but the real workers can't avoid risks every day through editing. Therefore, the best function of the One Day series isn't to claim "I already understand this job," but to let audiences know that they didn't understand before, and start paying attention to training, tools, safety, and time.

## The Visibility of Ordinary Work Is Not Yet Complete

The Taipei City Government Public Works Department's Park Streetlight Engineering Office recorded in a 2019 article that James Tsai and Xie Kun-da followed the streetlight seniors to receive pre-shift education, tool and safety training, and then went from daytime patrol to night emergency repairs. This isn't the show's own promotional copy, but a record left by the collaborating agency on the job site; it grounds the "One Day series" public value to a concrete issue: who is actually repairing the roads citizens walk past every day?[^12]

Thursday's achievement isn't in introducing every profession completely; one video can't replace training, certification, occupational safety regulations, nor can it let viewers understand the full complexity of the Taiwan Power Company, police, aides, or concert backstage after watching once. What it can do is open a door that's usually closed for a few hours, letting audiences see someone practicing behind the door.

James Tsai's failed wire-cutting scene, the aides guiding through the city government for 58 minutes, and the official video's 1 hour 46 minutes of concert backstage work record[^13] all point to the same thing: Taiwan's daily life isn't maintained by "ordinary" people, but by many unseen experts. Thursday temporarily turns stars into apprentices, and temporarily lets audiences put aside the illusion that "this job should be simple."

It looks like turning stars into ordinary people; more deeply, it's reminding us that ordinary people never have ordinary jobs.
