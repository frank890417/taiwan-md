> **30-Second Overview:** On the evening of October 3, 2020, the Album of the Year award at the 31st Golden Melody Awards was presented to an all-Paiwan language electronic music album titled _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_. The winner is Abao (Aljenljeng Tjaluvie; Chinese name Chang Ching-wen), a Paiwan singer-songwriter born in 1981 in the Zhengxing community of Jialan Village, Jinfeng Township, Taitung. She debuted in 2003 as part of the R&B duo Abao & Brandy, winning Best Vocal Group at the 15th Golden Melody Awards. After disbanding in 2004, she stepped away from the music scene to work as a nurse for nearly ten years. She returned in 2016 with her solo all-Paiwan creation _vavayan: Woman_, and in 2019 collaborated with electronic producer Dizparity to create _kinakaian_. On stage while receiving the Album of the Year award, she said: "I want to tell all the indigenous people watching on TV: do not waste your talent, and do not rely solely on it." This was a pivotal moment for indigenous language music in Taiwan moving from the sidebar to the main stage.

The evening of October 3, 2020, Taipei Music Center. The 31st Golden Melody Awards ceremony. The grand finale: Album of the Year.

When the winner was announced, the presenter read out: **_kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_**. [^1]

This is an **all-Paiwan language** electronic music album. Since the Golden Melody Awards established the "Best Indigenous Language Album" subcategory in 2007, most indigenous language works have remained within this subcategory—receiving recognition within their ethnic classification, but rarely catching the eye of judges for main categories like Album of the Year, Song of the Year, or Producer of the Year. [^1]

The 31st Golden Melody Awards in 2020 was a rare exception. That same night, _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_ took home three awards: Album of the Year, Best Indigenous Language Album, and Song of the Year (for "Thank You"), with eight nominations in total. [^1]

The person who walked onto the stage to accept the award was a 39-year-old woman with short hair, wearing dark, loose clothing, looking more like a friendly neighbor than most stage performers. Her name is Abao (Aljenljeng Tjaluvie; Chinese name Chang Ching-wen).

📝 Curator's Note: The significance of the Album of the Year award is not just that "an indigenous singer won," but that "the aesthetic standards of indigenous language works were recognized by the mainstream judging system on an equal scale." These two things are very different.

### From Zhengxing Community to Kaohsiung

Abao was born on August 25, 1981, in the Zhengxing community of Jialan Village, Jinfeng Township, Taitung County (East Paiwan). [^2] Both of her parents are from this community, but she moved with her family to Kaohsiung as a child and grew up there. [^3]

This migration pattern is common in the demographic history of Taiwan's indigenous peoples: from the 1970s to the 1990s, many indigenous families from Taitung and Pingtung moved to urban areas for work. Children grew up in metropolitan areas, leading to a generational decline in language proficiency. Abao's later project of "reclaiming her language" was largely built upon the daily linguistic material provided by her mother, Wang Chiu-lan—her own foundation in the language was something she had to relearn alongside her mother as an adult. [^4]

### 2003: R&B Debut with Abao & Brandy

In 2003, Abao and Brandy Chen (Chen Hai-wen) formed the duo "Abao & Brandy," releasing a self-titled album in the R&B style. [^5]

The album was almost entirely in Mandarin. The two brought American R&B harmonies and rhythms to the Mandopop market, making them a unique act among early 2000s Mandopop R&B duos. In 2004, they won **Best Vocal Group at the 15th Golden Melody Awards** for this album. [^5]

Then, in that same year, the group disbanded.

Regarding why they disbanded, she did not offer a dramatic reason in later interviews. The general explanation was: "Music was not the center of my life at that time." [^6] She returned to a realistic path—her background in a five-year nursing program at Chang Gung Junior College provided a clear off-ramp: working as a nurse.

📝 Curator's Note: Winning Best Vocal Group at the 15th Golden Melody Awards made them representatives of a new generation. Disbanding one year later and disappearing for over five years was an extremely counter-intuitive decision for a 22-year-old award-winning singer.

### 2004–2014: Ten Years as a Nurse

For these ten years, what she did had almost nothing to do with music. [^6]

In the rules of the Mandopop industry, a Golden Melody winner leaving the scene for ten years is equivalent to "disappearing." But she didn't just "disappear"—she returned to a job with **real-world weight**, waiting until she had decided what kind of relationship she wanted to have with music before returning.

The importance of this period only became apparent after her return in 2016: **she no longer needed to rely on music for her livelihood**; nursing provided her with an economic foundation that did not depend on the record market. This gave her the freedom of choice later on—to make all-language albums, to experiment with electronic music, to decline commercial compilations, and to choose to collaborate with independent electronic producers like Dizparity.

### 2014: _East Paiwan Three Generations_, Three Generations of Paiwan Ancient Ballads

In 2014, Abao officially returned to music through her language. Her first project was not a solo album, but **a collaborative recording titled _East Paiwan Three Generations_ with her mother, Wang Chiu-lan, and her grandmother, Liang Chiu-mei**. [^7]

The three generations on this album all sang Paiwan ancient ballads. Grandmother, mother, and daughter appeared in the studio together to record East Paiwan tribal songs that were on the verge of being lost. Abao's role in this album was closer to that of a **midwife**: she preserved what her mother and grandmother could sing but had never recorded, while placing her own voice into the same sonic field as the two elders.

_East Paiwan Three Generations_ was not a commercial work; it was her ticket back into the world of her language. From this point on, her musical work revolved around the concept of "the mother's tongue."

### 2016: _vavayan: Woman_, Her First Solo All-Language Creation

In August 2016, she released her first solo creative album, _vavayan: Woman_ (_vavayan_ is Paiwan for "woman"). [^8] The producer was Arai Juichi, a Japan-born producer who worked in China and collaborated extensively with Asian independent musicians. [^8]

While _vavayan: Woman_ was entirely in Paiwan, the musical style fused rock, soul, reggae, and R&B. The album had two defining characteristics:

1. **Indigenous language does not equal nostalgia.** The musical style was contemporary and even cross-cultural (reggae from Jamaica, R&B from African American music).
2. **A female perspective.** The title _vavayan_ is Paiwan for "woman," and the entire album revolves around female experiences—not the women of traditional tribal narratives, but contemporary women who walk the streets, think, feel anger, and experience exhaustion.

Arai Juichi later described his experience producing the album:

> "When Arai Juichi was producing Abao's album, he felt her sense of humor as a host, combined with Abao's sense of mission regarding her mother tongue and the R&B style she had explored with 'Abao & Brandy,' which together completed the up-tempo style of _vavayan: Woman_." [^9]

At the 28th Golden Melody Awards in 2016, _vavayan: Woman_ won Best Indigenous Language Album, and Arai Juichi won Best Album Producer at the same ceremony. [^8]

### 2019: _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_, Indigenous Electronic Music

Released on December 30, 2019, her second solo album, _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_, went even further: **all-Paiwan language + all electronic**. [^10]

The arrangement was by Dizparity (Yeh Po-chien), a Taiwanese independent electronic producer. Their collaboration began at the 2018 CMW Festival in Canada; at the time, Dizparity performed a full electronic set without vocals, which Abao watched from the audience. [^11] After returning to Taiwan, the two communicated online—Dizparity sent beats, and Abao provided spontaneous wordless humming, gradually building a rapport.

> "Dizparity did a performance entirely without vocals; afterward, the two communicated via messages upon returning to Taiwan. Dizparity sent some beats for Abao to hum atmospherically, and through these small experimental fragments, their collaborative chemistry gradually grew." [^11]

This collaboration model was not the standard Mandopop production of "singer paired with arranger," but rather two independent musicians responding to each other through texture and linguistic sense. Dizparity's electronic music was not a "sugar coating" designed to make Abao's Paiwan easier to accept; instead, it treated the language as a sonic material on equal footing: placed on the same level as synthesizer timbres, rhythmic patterns, and atmospheric drops.

The lyrics of the album came from her mother, Wang Chiu-lan. Abao described their collaborative process:

> "My mother and I write the lyrics we want more like we are just chatting. The amount of vocabulary my mother provided for the second album, _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_, was even more abundant and difficult, which was the most practical feedback." [^12]

It wasn't about looking up a dictionary or conducting interviews; it was **recording their conversations at home**. Everyday language became the raw material for lyrics.

### "Since we have our own language to use, why not?"

In interviews surrounding this album, Abao repeatedly said something that would become the core of her musical philosophy:

> "Actually, making music in your mother tongue is a liberation. For example, if I want to try faster-paced songs, like the dance tracks people associate with Beyoncé, it's really hard to do in Chinese. Mandopop has its own beauty, but when you want to make music with strong grooves, English isn't our language either. So, since we have our own language to use, why not?" [^13]

This statement flipped the long-standing framework into which indigenous music in Taiwan had been placed: **Indigenous language music $\neq$ cultural preservation $\neq$ museumification**. For her, the language was a technical condition for making future pop—the syllables, tones, accents, and phrasing are different from Mandarin or English; certain grooves can only be sung properly in the native tongue.

She explained this logic even more directly:

> "I think the old and the new must be fused together. Young people might not have the chance to learn these songs. If we embed ancient melodies into popular music, at least young people can hear those ancient folk songs through pop music." [^13]

"Embedding ancient melodies into pop music" is not preservation; it is translation: allowing the youth of the 2020s to encounter the DNA of tribal folk songs from the 1950s–1980s through the musical forms they actually listen to.

### The Significance of the 2020 Album of the Year Award

On October 3, 2020, during the 31st Golden Melody Awards ceremony.

That year was unique due to the pandemic: the ceremony was delayed by two months, and the judging process had extra time for adjustments. The chairman of the jury, Chen Chen-chuan, said in a later interview:

> "Due to the pandemic, the jury had more ample time to read non-Mandarin works closely. They specifically invited indigenous language teachers to explain the meaning of the lyrics line by line to the judges, so that during the first round of voting, 'almost every language was represented among the nominees for Best Lyricist'." [^14]

The fact that the jury **invited indigenous language teachers to translate lyrics line by line** was a first in the history of the Golden Melody Awards. What was the result? The Album of the Year award went to an all-Paiwan work.

This difference in mechanism was crucial: **even under the premise that translation is required for evaluation, the indigenous work could still be recognized as the best**. Mainstream Mandopop and indigenous works were placed on the same scoring sheet, no longer relying on "ethnic bonus points."

Her acceptance speech for Album of the Year was cited by many media outlets:

> "I want to tell all the indigenous people watching on TV: do not waste your talent, and do not rely solely on it."
>
> "A little more understanding, a little less misunderstanding. If you still don't understand, please listen to this album one more time; if you still don't like it, then please listen to it a second time." [^15]

"Do not waste your talent and do not rely solely on it" carries two meanings: internally (to the indigenous community), she is saying not to take the artistic ability given by the group for granted or stay within a comfort zone; externally (to non-indigenous audiences), she pushes back against the label "indigenous artistic talent" that Mandopop society often applies: **my work is the result of labor, not an ethnic bonus**.

📝 Curator's Note: The phrase "do not rely solely on talent" is rarely heard in the Mandopop industry. Most winners thank their talent, God, or their parents. By framing talent as something one should "not rely on," she makes a rare statement of professional ethics.

### "Without text, other senses are amplified"

Paiwan is one of the languages without a written script. Abao's perspective on this is actually positive:

> "I feel that because there is no written text, other senses are amplified."
>
> "Crossing through ancient and modern, crossing the barriers of language, our feelings are actually so similar." [^13]

No text = oral tradition = memory = body = rhythm = something contemporary electronic music can connect with. This seemingly paradoxical logical chain explains why her collaboration with Dizparity was so smooth: **electronic production does not rely on text or semantics; it relies on the sensory transmission of rhythm, timbre, and atmosphere**.

By singing electronic music in Paiwan, she isn't "forcing the language into electronic music"; she is allowing electronic music to encounter the sound source it was meant to find.

### Nanguaq Music: From Individual to Mechanism

After 2020, Abao does more than just make her own music. She leads "Nanguaq" (Nanguaq Music), a label and production institution centered on indigenous music. [^16]

Nanguaq does not follow the predefined path of "industrializing indigenous music"; instead, it acts as an enabler to **allow young indigenous musicians to make the music they want to make**. Works such as 2024's _tlupupia_ and the 2025 _886 Waves_ performance in New York's Central Park are all products of the Nanguaq framework. [^17]

The significance of this institutional move is: **it does not rely on a single star singer to carry the weight of ethnic representation**. Abao ensures that the subsequent "Nanguaq Girl Group" and other Nanguaq collaborators do not have to repeat her path of "carrying indigenous pop alone," because the label already exists.

### Family: Mother's Tongue, Grandmother's Voice

The title _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_ is itself a family narrative. Her mother, Wang Chiu-lan, provided the vocabulary, and her grandmother, Liang Chiu-mei, lent her voice to _East Paiwan Three Generations_—three generations have all recorded music.

Her mother, Wang Chiu-lan, passed away in February 2021 at the age of 66. [^18] _kinakaian_ was released in December 2019 and won the Golden Melody Award in October 2020—her mother witnessed the entire process of its birth, release, and victory.

Abao's discussion of her mother's influence is very concrete: her mother was a tribal missionary and singer in her youth, and after marriage, she was a homemaker who also hosted and sang at large community banquets (_shuǐxí_). She was not an academic researcher of the language; she **was a person living within the language**. Abao's collaboration with her was an exchange of life-based linguistic material between two generations, not a one-way transmission of "the older generation teaching the younger." [^12]

### Conclusion: "I hope everyone's playlist includes an indigenous pop song"

After the 31st Golden Melody Awards in 2020, Abao said in an interview with Marie Claire:

> "I hope that in the future, everyone's playlist will include an indigenous pop song." [^19]

Broken down, this is a concrete goal: **indigenous language pop songs should sit on equal footing with Mandopop, English, and Japanese pop in daily playlists**. It shouldn't be a ceremonial moment of "playing indigenous music specially," but a daily reality where "you swipe to the next song on your Spotify playlist and it happens to be Paiwan, or Bunun, or Amis."

This goal has not yet been reached. On Chinese-language pop streaming platforms in 2026, the vast majority of Taiwanese users' daily playlists do not include indigenous language songs. But with her 2019 album _kinakaian: Mother's Tongue_, Abao raised the ceiling of possibility by one level.

How would she summarize all this? Perhaps it is those two sentences from her Golden Melody acceptance speech:

**"Do not waste your talent, and do not rely solely on it."**

This applies to herself, to the next generation of indigenous musicians, and to the entire Mandopop industry: talent is the starting point, not the answer.
