> **30-Second Overview:** Ceylan is a YouTuber who creates content in Chinese. After the 2022 Shanghai lockdown, he brought his life experiences to the camera; in 2023, after living in Taiwan for about six months and obtaining residency, he announced he was considering moving to Japan due to the pressure of being recognized, requested for photos, and subjected to surreptitious filming. This article does not provide a final verdict on Taiwan, but rather tracks how a person who became famous by "being seen" chooses a new place to live after being excessively observed.

In 2022, Ceylan left China following the Shanghai lockdown. In 2023, he lived in Taiwan for about six months and obtained residency. In August of that same year, he announced his intention to leave Taiwan and consider moving to Japan. [^1][^2][^3] While this timeline looks like a record of migration, the true human tension lies in the contrast: a person who built a channel based on "being seen" ultimately lost the ability to enjoy an ordinary daily life—the kind where one can simply go out—because he was too easily seen.

## His Visibility Did Not Start in Taiwan

Ceylan is a Belgian-Chinese YouTuber and streamer who creates content in Chinese. During the Shanghai lockdown in 2022, he used videos to record his feelings, describing experiences ranging from the announcement of the lockdown to the delivery of supplies. _Newtalk_ reported that he once mentioned the last time he received rations, he only had eight eggs. These contents showed viewers more than just "a foreigner looking at China"; they showed how someone living long-term in Shanghai suddenly lost the ability to move and organize their life in a familiar city. [^4]

After leaving Shanghai, Ceylan turned his lens toward Taiwan. On April 24, 2023, he released 〈After Living Here for 6 Months, These Are My Real Thoughts on Taiwan〉, with video chapters sequentially discussing media, transportation, and urban appearance, while also retaining positive observations regarding democracy, friendliness, and the cost of living. [^5][^6][^15] This structure is significant: Ceylan does not merely list Taiwan's flaws; instead, he uses "expectations vs. reality" as a narrative framework to organize his living experience into a comparative critique. Reports noted that he also mentioned Taiwan's freedom of speech, democracy, passionate people, and relatively low cost of living. [^5][^6] His tone is often blunt, even intentionally making people uncomfortable, because he does not film Taiwan as a tourism promotional video; rather, he places the _qilou_ (covered walkways), roads, and news broadcasts he encounters every day on the same examination table. This creative method explains why he easily triggers two diametrically opposed reactions: supporters believe he speaks out about problems people have grown accustomed to, while critics worry that a newcomer's short-term living experience is being edited into overly definitive conclusions. Ceylan's characteristic lies not just in "daring to criticize," but in packaging personal feelings into public texts that can be widely shared, cited, and refuted.

The importance of this content lies in how it moves Ceylan's channel from a personal testimony of "escaping Shanghai" to a comparative critique of his place of residence. The core of the Shanghai videos was how to obtain food, move, and leave after the lockdown. The Taiwan videos, however, turn the lens toward more mundane and complex issues within a democratic society: how media reports, how transportation risks are distributed, and how the urban appearance accumulates into resident habits. The video also includes segments such as "Expectations Before Coming to Taiwan," "The Larger Structures Behind the Problems," and "Some Small Things," showing that he wants to discuss not just street scenes, but how institutions, habits, and quality of life are interconnected. [^10][^11][^15] _Mirror Media_ (Jing Weekly) summarized his three criticisms into issues of media, transportation, and architecture/urban appearance, but this remains the observer's framework and does not constitute a complete diagnosis of Taiwanese society.

## Why "Real Thoughts" Sparked Controversy

Ceylan's Taiwan video sparked discussion not only because of what he criticized, but because his identity as a critic became news itself. Taiwanese media reported on his Chinese fluency, mixed heritage, residency status, and subscriber count all at once. Among these, reports from August 2023 noted his channel had approximately 1.17 million subscribers, though that was the figure at the time and cannot be used directly to represent his current scale. [^1][^2][^7]

In his video, he stated that Taiwanese people do not always need to seek validation from the outside; they should speak less falsehoods and accept guidance. Supporters saw this as constructive, while opponents might argue that someone who has only lived there for six months should not condense a complex society into a few flaws. Both sides actually face the same dilemma: YouTube requires clear stances, but social reality rarely offers only one answer. The media ecosystem, transportation, and urban appearance may be real issues, but they cannot be finalized based solely on one creator's observations.

More noteworthy is that Ceylan's videos serve dual functions as both "life logs" and "arguments." The streets in the frame, news footage, and personal narration make viewers feel as if they are watching a live scene. However, when he infers an entire society from a few examples, viewers must still ask whether the sample size is sufficient, whether the comparison is fair, and whether the video omitted counter-examples for the sake of pacing. This is not a requirement unique to Ceylan, but a media literacy issue that all commentary-style creators must face.

## What Left Taiwan Was Not the Country, But the Gaze

In August 2023, Ceylan said he was "not very happy" in Taiwan. Reports from TVBS, _Mirror Media_, and CTS all noted that his primary concern was the freedom to go out: being recognized or asked for photos is not entirely unacceptable, but being surreptitiously filmed and having photos posted online caused him to gradually reduce his outings. He also mentioned Taipei's long periods of hot and humid weather and viewed Japan as a potential next destination. [^1][^2][^3][^7]

This declaration of departure did not judge Taiwan as "bad." On the contrary, Ceylan still said he has friends in Taiwan and would return. His judgment was more like, "This place is currently not suitable for me to live." For an ordinary office worker, being recognized while out might be an occasional nuisance. For someone whose work involves putting their face and voice on the internet, it can become a daily cost to be calculated. Fame brought him before more people, but it also pushed him away from an anonymous life.

This timeline does not compress Ceylan's life into a few years, but rather marks how his role in public discourse changed: first as a person experiencing life restrictions, then as a creator commenting on places of residence, then as an object of media criticism and reporting, and finally facing the controversies generated by his own commentary. The dates and content in the timeline should be subject to their respective page-by-page sources. It provides a reading order, not a final verdict on all events.

Ceylan's experience in Taiwan cannot serve as a final assessment of Taiwan, nor can his departure be simplified into gossip about "an influencer who dislikes Taiwan." To understand this journey, one can view it as a creative trajectory of constantly shifting positions: the 2022 Shanghai lockdown made him a witness to restrictions and daily disorder. His 2023 residency in Taiwan turned him into a commentator comparing cities, media, and institutions. Subsequent media criticism, the A-Di incident, and the pet communicator incident turned him into a public figure who is examined, cited, and refuted by others. This timeline shows that his content has been moving back and forth between "observing others" and "being observed by others." [^4][^12][^13][^14]

### Pet Communicators: From Debunking to Professional Boundaries

On March 26, 2026, after the program "The Great Pet Communicator Showdown" aired on Haha Tai, Ceylan released 〈Pet Communication Makes Me So Angry!!! Why Do People Believe This Stuff!!!〉 on April 18, raising doubts from the perspectives of scientific basis, quantum claims, lost pets, and deceased pets. He later released a video of himself attending a class, extending his critique into a verification path of "watch the program first, then question the methods." [^12][^16][^17] Consequently, the incident expanded from a web program into a debate between healing needs and scientific skepticism. PeoPo interviewed pet communicators, owners who had used the service, and professionals with animal behavior training backgrounds: some viewed the service as psychological comfort when losing a pet or facing anxiety, while others worried that incorrect specific advice would delay the handling of animal behavior and health issues. [^12]

The point of this incident was not to declare "who is definitely right" on behalf of Ceylan, but to separate three different claims. The first is owners receiving emotional comfort, which belongs to personal experience; the second is whether communicators can provide verifiable animal behavior judgments, involving methods and evidence; the third is whether they can offer medical or training advice, which involves professional qualifications and actual risks. PeoPo's interviews also presented a middle ground that should not be ignored: some owners stated that while the service did not necessarily find lost pets, it provided psychological comfort in times of helplessness; animal behavior trainers argued that if incorrect specific advice is used as a basis for action, it might mask true causes such as stress, environment, or disease. [^12] If these three are conflated, pet communication is easily categorized between "faith" and "fraud," losing the ability to examine service content and responsibility boundaries.

Ceylan's subsequent decision to attend a pet communication class added a methodological twist to the event. This does not mean personal experience equals scientific verification, but it moved his critique from watching a program and offering intuitive reactions toward engaging with service processes and checking claims. For viewers, a more stable way to read this is to treat the videos as public questioning and records, rather than taking Ceylan's conclusions, the communicators' claims, or the mockery in the comments section as final answers. [^16][^17]

### Spiritual Courses: From Self-Growth to Power Dynamics

In 2025, Ceylan used a long-form video 〈Your Soul Does Not Need a Course〉 to organize his investigation into LGAT (Large Group Awareness Training) and three-stage spiritual courses. _Xin Media_ reported that the video was approximately 2 hours and 20 minutes long; Ceylan stated he spent about two months reading materials, contacting researchers, and participants, focusing on how courses use intensive activities, group pressure, and the reinterpretation of personal struggles to make participants believe they are undergoing a major transformation. Here, two levels should be distinguished: what Ceylan proposed was a creator's investigation and critique. The participants, researchers, and helping professionals mentioned in reports provided different experiences and judgments, which cannot be simplified into a single conclusion. [^18]

The reason these types of courses are worth discussing is not just because some people believe or disbelieve in spiritual growth, but because the courses may involve specific relationships regarding fees, confidentiality, freedom to exit, group pressure, and instructor authority. Comments from clinical psychologists remind us that if participants are required to cut off external information, are forbidden from questioning the course, or are told to attribute all suffering to their own lack of effort, then informed consent and autonomy must be re-examined. These are risk frameworks proposed by professional commentators, not judicial determinations made about every spiritual course or individual institution. [^19]

### Organ and Body Communication: Spiritual Language Meets Medical Risk

In 2026, Ceylan further filmed 〈I Attended an Organ Communication Class! And Found Out It's Even Worse Than I Thought...〉. The official video page lists segments such as the definition of organ communication, "conversations" with a pancreatic cancer patient's intestines, claims regarding tumor surgery, the origins of Theta Healing, and responses from Taiwanese spiritual teachers. _Social Lab_ summarized the discussion volume after the video release. This event continued his method of "entering the scene first, then deconstructing the claim," but it pushed the responsibility of verification into a higher-risk domain: when a course discusses cancer, tumors, or surgery, the content cannot be measured solely by personal healing experiences. [^20][^21]

"Organ and body communication" can be treated as a form of symbolic self-awareness language, such as prompting attention to stress, pain, or lifestyle habits. However, if it goes further to claim it can read the will of organs, explain the causes of tumors, or imply that courses can replace diagnosis and treatment, then a higher level of evidence must be required. This article does not make medical judgments on behalf of Ceylan or the course instructors, but rather establishes clear boundaries: psychological comfort can be understood, but decisions regarding cancer and surgery should be left to qualified medical professionals following evidence-based procedures. This also makes the organ communication incident different from the pet communication incident—the former directly touches upon major diseases and medical choices, which cannot be proven safe simply because "some people find it helpful." [^20][^21]

From spiritual courses to organ communication, the question Ceylan's content leaves behind is not "should all mind-body services be mocked," but rather who makes the claim, who bears the consequences of errors, and whether participants have sufficient information and the freedom to exit. This way of questioning is more meaningful than simply taking sides, and it avoids conflating an individual's emotional needs with medical facts.

### Criticism of Taiwanese Media: Who Decides the Public Narrative?

In September 2023, Ceylan released a video criticizing Taiwanese media for taking quotes out of context to attract traffic through headlines, arguing that journalists in a democratic society cannot respond to errors merely with "freedom of speech," but should accept public scrutiny and accountability. _The Initium_ reported that Sanlih News acknowledged that the journalists named by Ceylan were indeed employed there, stated they would reflect on editorial processes, and invited scholars and members of society to initiate a news self-regulation committee. The report also recorded the pressure media professionals face from click rates and platform recommendation mechanisms, explaining that the problem is not just a single headline, but involves a cycle shaped by media, platforms, and audiences. [^13]

This incident made Ceylan more than just a commentator on Taiwan; he was contesting "who has the right to name an event." When media clips a long video into a few quotes, a creator may feel their original intent has been rewritten. When an influencer uses a longer, more intense video to fight back, the criticism of the media might be repackaged as another traffic-driven event. Ceylan's official Taiwan commentary video also lists segments such as "post-truth environment" and "unscrupulous media consumption," showing that media issues are not an incidental complaint in his narrative, but one of the core arguments of the entire video. [^13][^15] Therefore, what truly needs to be examined is not "whether the influencer or the media is always right," but whether every retelling provides full context, whether it allows the criticized party a chance to respond, and whether there is a traceable correction mechanism after an error occurs.

### The A-Di Incident: Criticism Can Also Produce New Misreadings

Media criticism later extended to a public confrontation between Ceylan and A-Di (Didi). _Newtalk_ reported that Ceylan called A-Di because he believed A-Di was being affected by media taking things out of context. After a conversation lasting about 40 minutes, Ceylan apologized, and A-Di stated he accepted it. This follow-up filled in the parts that cannot be seen through livestream clips alone: Ceylan's motivation, A-Di's feelings, and the rectification after their direct communication. [^14]

The A-Di incident also serves as a reflexive reminder: when a creator opposes media using snippets to manufacture conclusions, their own livestream snippets can similarly be stripped of context by viewers and the media. Public criticism does not automatically guarantee correct judgment; rather, it requires creators to bear higher responsibilities for verification and correction. Likewise, viewers cannot take "the snippet I saw" as the complete event.

### Conclusion: From Being Seen to Learning Responsibility

Therefore, what Ceylan leaves behind is not a template for grading Taiwan that others can copy, but three persistent questions: when a creator offers criticism, where are the evidence and boundaries; when media retells an influencer's words, do they provide full context; and when viewers share and spectate, do they simplify a person's life, profession, and mistakes into consumable drama. Ceylan began by being seen, but left because of being seen; and in the pet communication, Taiwanese media, and A-Di incidents, he also became part of the very public gaze mechanism he criticized. This is not a period at the end of a sentence about whether Taiwan is good or bad, but a conclusion regarding how we observe, verify, and take responsibility in the digital age.
