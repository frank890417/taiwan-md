> **30-second overview**: In 1979, NTU Hospital performed a 12-hour separation surgery on Chang Chun-jen and Chang Chun-yi with a 36-person multidisciplinary team. This was a major milestone in Taiwan's medical history, but not the end of the brothers' story. Post-surgery illness, rehabilitation, work, family, and media scrutiny were what made "becoming two people" a lifelong journey.

On the morning of December 23, 1976, in a maternity ward in Qianzhen, Kaohsiung, midwife Wu Yue-xia first saw a baby's head, then three legs, and finally another child's body and head. Chang Chun-jen and Chang Chun-yi were born. The two shared one pelvis and a third leg, yet each had their own consciousness and life. [^3]

Three years later, on September 10, 1979, NTU Hospital spent 12 hours separating the two children—from the abdomen, intestines, urinary system, pelvis, and shared limbs. 36 medical staff took turns in the operating room; this was the first successful separation of ischiopagus tripus conjoined twins in Asia. [^1] [^2]

But this is not a story where "the surgery succeeds and the story ends." After their bodies were separated, illness, prosthetics, rehabilitation, work, family, and media attention composed the truly long life ahead. [^3]

📝 **Curator's note**: Separation surgery gave the two a chance at independent bodies, but did not automatically grant them independent lives. This is precisely what makes Chang Chun-jen and Chang Chun-yi's story worth preserving.

## At Birth, Two Names Had No Room of Their Own

Wu Yue-xia later recalled that Mrs. Chang had a natural delivery, with Chun-jen's head coming out first, followed by three legs, and finally Chun-yi's body and head. The brothers cried loudly at birth. This detail was preserved by the midwife, giving the "case file" a voice of birth rather than just an anatomical diagram. [^3]

Their parents were poor and unable to provide long-term care, so the brothers were first sent to the hospital attached to Chung Shan Medical University in Taichung for examination and care. Historical records compiled by United Daily News indicate that Zhongshan Hospital, under the hospital administration and founder Zhou Ru-chuan, cared for the brothers until they were three years old before transferring them to NTU Hospital for evaluation of separation. [^2]

This experience was later often simplified into "parents abandoned the children." But a 1979 report also preserved another quote from their father, Zhang Yi-sheng: "This is the only chance for the two children." He knew the separation might let the children survive, and he also knew the children would have to endure a highly risky surgery. [^4]

At the time, the real debate involved not only whether doctors could cut apart the shared body, but also who could decide the next steps for children who could not yet speak: maintain the status quo, or risk it for the possibility of separate lives. A 1979 report preserved by Taiwan Panorama documented both the arguments for and against separation. The brothers could not walk or rest independently, and shared organs also led to gradual deterioration of health. Opponents worried about surgical success rates, post-operative physical deficits, and long-term care. [^4]

📝 **Curator's note**: Whether to separate or not is not merely a technical issue. It is a dividing line where society views the two children as medical conditions, family members, or rights-bearing individuals.

## From the Old Building to the Operating Room: How a Hospital Became the Address of Memory

The old building of NTU Hospital, still recognizable today, is an appropriate visual entry point for this history. The file page on Wikimedia Commons indicates the photo was taken by Chien-Ping KAO under CC BY-SA 4.0. It allows readers to see the concrete location of the hospital, but should not be mistaken for the actual surgery site in 1979. [^10]

This distinction is important. Pre-surgery photos of the conjoined twins belong to cultural heritage, the surgical process has records from the media and hospital at the time, and today's hospital building photos are another form of later viewing. All three types of images can help understand history, but their timelines, authors, and licenses must not be mixed. The article chose freely licensed architectural photos to supplement the scene, not to use a contemporary photo to fill in the missing surgical images.

The architecture of NTU Hospital is therefore not just a backdrop. In 1979, the medical team integrated models, laparoscopy, anesthesia, organ separation, and reconstruction into a coordinated effort within the hospital. For the brothers, this was also the address from their shared body to their separate lives. Building photos won't tell us every decision made on the operating table, but they can remind readers: this medical event took place in real rooms, corridors, and institutions—not in the abstract phrase "Taiwan medical history."

📝 **Curator's note**: The value of freely licensed images is not just in being usable, but in honestly stating what they captured and what they did not. This old building photo makes the location tangible and clearly states it is not the surgery site.

## How a Multidisciplinary Team Handled a 12-Hour Surgery

NTU Hospital in 1979 did not have today's high-resolution imaging or three-dimensional surgical planning. The surgical team first used models for simulation, then used laparoscopy to inject air into the abdomen, gradually stretching the skin to prepare for separating and covering the wounds afterward. The brothers' shared third leg was also first removed of bone, leaving skin as reconstruction material. [^1] [^4]

NTU Newsletter records that the surgery was organized by Hong Qi-ren, with Chen Wei-zhao as the chief physician, involving pediatric surgery, orthopedics, plastic surgery, urology, general surgery, and anesthesiology departments. Official data states the surgery lasted 12 hours, completed by a 36-person multidisciplinary team. [^1]

Reports from the time left more precise time references. At 8:32 AM, Dr. Hong Wen-zong made the first incision. The surgery first handled the digestive system, then the urinary system, then separated the pelvis, genitals, ischium, and shared limbs, finally with two groups of doctors simultaneously reconstructing and suturing. [^4]

After opening the abdominal cavity in the first stage, the team encountered an unexpected situation. Doctors originally assumed the livers were separate, but discovered that the livers of the two were still connected. The connected part was about 7 cm long and 2.5 cm thick; the team had to change the plan on-site, first stop the bleeding, then complete the liver separation. [^4]

At 5:16 PM, the two brothers were truly separated. Chun-jen opened his eyes at 7:37 PM, and Chun-yi woke up at 8:43 PM. United Daily News reported at the time that the brothers had been physically connected for 992 days before the surgery. The weight of this number does not lie in its apparent precision, but in how it places the 12 hours on the operating table back into the nearly three years of shared life between the two children. [^2] [^4]

This surgery also marked the first time Taiwan's medical community entered the international spotlight with such collective effort. The Taiwan Medical Association's medical history lists it as an important event in 1979, documenting 18 doctors, over 30 medical staff, and 250 blood donors supporting the effort. Here, "250 people" refers to the number of blood donors, not the volume of blood transfused. [^5]

A 1986 paper published in the Journal of Pediatric Surgery included this case in international medical literature. The paper's authors came from the surgery, urology, and anesthesiology departments of NTU Hospital and Chung Shan Medical University, titled "Successful separation of ischiopagus tripus conjoined twins," with the abstract noting follow-up after 76 months post-separation. [^8]

📝 **Curator's note**: The protagonists of this surgery were not just the hands holding the knives. Surgery, anesthesia, urology, orthopedics, plastic surgery, and care all had to be connected along the same timeline for the children to have a chance to get off the operating table.

## "Becoming Two People" Is Not "No More Illness"

The first good news after surgery was that both brothers survived and began rehabilitation. NTU Alumni Bi-Monthly wrote in 1999, reviewing the 20th anniversary of the separation, that the hospital still saw itself as the caregivers of the brothers, while the brothers' greatest wish was to reunite with their family. [^7]

But successful separation did not eliminate the physical limitations left behind. Reporter records that Chun-jen was hospitalized or underwent surgery multiple times a year, while Chun-yi needed weekly enemas. Both also encountered work, financial, and legal issues during their growth due to physical conditions and insufficient family support. [^3]

In an interview, Chun-jen clearly spoke about his own separation. He said: "Although our bodies are not complete, if I have autonomy, I would still choose to separate. After separation, that is when true life begins, when survival begins—this is a test given to us by heaven." This is the words of the person involved, not a motivational conclusion arranged by onlookers. [^3]

He and Chun-yi once sold chewing gum or small accessories at NTU Hospital gates, markets, night markets, and street stalls. Later, Chun-jen worked at the Environmental Protection Administration, while Chun-yi worked at a private fitness and beauty company. The separated bodies did not fit the simple narrative of "returning to normal," but both continued to seek their own work and life rhythms. [^3]

A pre-surgery life photo preserved by the National Culture Database shows the two brothers taking a bath together. The Ministry of Culture entry also records the photo's source, collection, and online public display/retrieval status. [^6] The value of such images is not in treating the body as a spectacle, but in reminding readers: before the surgery, newspapers, and medical terms, they were just two children who needed to bathe, sleep, and grow.

## The Second Half of a Surgery Is Long-Term Care

On the night the separation was completed, the medical team finished the most urgent part of the work. The two children still had to learn how to use their asymmetric lower limbs, face excretion and organ issues, and understand why their bodies were always seen by others first. None of these could be resolved in one go in the operating room. [^3]

NTU Alumni Bi-Monthly wrote on the 20th anniversary, noting that at the time, doctors did not know how long the brothers would live, and simply getting them safely out of the operating room was already considered a success. By 1999, Chen Wei-zhao was still blessing the brothers at the event, hoping they would one day reunite with their biological father. This reflection is rarely preserved under the title "Asia's First," yet it shows that medical success is always tied to care responsibilities. [^7]

The 10th anniversary event in 1989, the 20th anniversary photo party in 1999, and the 40th anniversary image exhibition in 2019 are like three time markers. Each commemoration not only replayed that one incision, but also asked: How are the brothers doing now? Is the hospital still there? Is society's concern a companion, or just a return once a year on the anniversary? [^3] [^7]

Reporter records that Chun-jen may still undergo multiple surgeries or hospitalizations per year, while Chun-yi needs weekly enemas. Their lives cannot be summarized as "returning to normal after a successful surgery." In medical history, success becomes in daily life a stack of clinic visit slips, segments of rehabilitation, a job, and the ability to decide whether to answer strangers' questions in public. [^3]

Therefore, parents, Zhongshan Hospital, NTU Hospital, foundations, nannies, friends, and photographic journalists all left different traces of care in the brothers' lives. These roles cannot be compressed into a vague sentence about an "empathetic society." Some provided medical care, some provided housing, some provided job opportunities, some preserved images, and some had to step back after the brothers grew up, letting them make their own decisions. [^3] [^4]

📝 **Curator's note**: Pediatric medical care is easiest to remember on the day of surgery, but a person's autonomy usually only truly grows after years of care gradually let go.

## Images Let Them Leave Their Names

Photographer Yang Yong-zhi began documenting the brothers in 1983, continuing for nearly 37 years. He photographed the brothers entering Tianmu Elementary School, Chun-jen undergoing spinal correction, the 10th anniversary, hospitalization, work, and Chun-yi's marriage. In 2019, when Reporter published this photo-text story, the article not only reviewed the surgery but also let readers see how time left its mark on the two bodies. [^3]

Chun-jen spoke at schools, prisons, and various institutions, accumulating nearly 300 speeches in recent years. He wanted to share his experience with others and hoped to complete his dream of speaking around the world. In February 2019, he died of a brainstem hemorrhage, and this dream remained unfulfilled. [^3]

Chun-yi married nurse Jiang Wen-yan in 2014, and the couple later had a pair of fraternal twins. On the eve of the 40th anniversary, the brothers originally planned to look back on their shared years through a photography exhibition and an oil painting exhibition. After his brother's passing, Chun-yi continued to take on the responsibility of telling their story. [^3]

NTU Alumni Bi-Monthly wrote in 1999 that the brothers had made a VCD "Tracing the River of Life," compiling 20 years of growth into images. This action was significant. When the media called them "Asia's First," they were medical records. When they edited the footage themselves, talking about family and the future, they became the authors of their own lives. [^7]

📝 **Curator's note**: A person should not be remembered only because they were once "the first case." What Chun-jen and Chun-yi truly left behind is two names growing their own voices beyond medical records.

## How a Medical Event Became Collective Memory

The reason this surgery is repeatedly revisited is tied to the era in which it occurred. In September 1979, television was still the most direct shared viewing medium in households. NTU Hospital set up closed-circuit TV in the seventh lecture hall, while China Television recorded the surgical process. Newspapers reported it on the front page the next day, and the hospital even hosted a month-end tea party to celebrate the surgery's success. The bodies of the two children thus became part of the medical scene, family conversations, and national sentiment simultaneously. [^2] [^4]

This "nationwide watching together" had real public value. NTU Newsletter records that at the time, the breaking of diplomatic ties between the United States and China caused social anxiety, and the success of the separation allowed donations, blood drives, and medical resources to quickly concentrate. The Taiwan Medical Association also included it in the annual records of Taiwan's medical history. From an institutional perspective, this was a demonstration of multidisciplinary collaboration. From the brothers' perspective, it was also the beginning of their lives being watched by others very early on. [^1] [^5]

Different sources do not cover the same scope of narrative. NTU Newsletter used the qualifiers "world's 4th, Asia's 1st" and "first successful post-operative twin survival globally." United Daily News' compilation of reports emphasized "the world's first separation of ischiopagus tripus conjoined twin boys." The apparent discrepancy comes from differences in statistical scope: one discusses all ischiopagus tripus conjoined twin surgery records, while the other discusses a specific range of male cases and post-operative survival. The article retains the qualifiers precisely to avoid rewriting medical history into slogans. [^1] [^2]

The same caution applies to numbers. 18 doctors, over 30 medical staff, a 36-person team, 250 blood donors, 8,270 units of Westland transfusion volume, and 60,250 units of pre-donation Westland all appear in different levels of sources. They are not a scoreboard that can be arbitrarily added together. The Taiwan Medical Association provides a medical history summary, Taiwan Panorama preserves longer on-site narratives, and Shih-chieh Time even transcribed contemporary newspapers. Readers need to know where each number comes from to understand what statement it can support. [^2] [^4] [^5]

This is also why the article does not portray all doctors as "heroes," nor all blood donors as nameless crowds, nor rewrite the brothers' later lives into inspirational textbooks. The credibility of history does not come from stacking up beautiful conclusions, but from placing different perspectives side by side, allowing the technical aspects of the surgery, the cost of care, and the choices of those involved to coexist.

## The Problems Left by Surgery Are Still Worth Asking

The 1979 surgery was broadcast on television and received nationwide attention. NTU Newsletter described how society was in a state of anxiety after the US-China break, and the success of the separation briefly became an outlet for collective emotion. [^1] Reporter also documented how the surgery was under close scrutiny from public opinion, donations, and the media. [^3]

This attention had two sides. On one side, blood donations, funding, and medical resources allowed the two children to have a chance to survive. On the other side, the bodies of the two were perhaps seen as a nationwide shared viewing event. When every drop of blood in the operating room was transmitted to the lecture hall via live broadcast, the children's privacy and society's expectations entered the same space.

Taiwan Panorama's original report called the separation "two new lives." This sentence had an encouraging effect in 1979, but it also easily led people to forget that these "new lives" did not begin on the day of surgery. They had already existed in the maternity ward in Kaohsiung, the ward at Zhongshan Hospital, and the days when the brothers fell asleep holding each other. [^4]

Therefore, the story of Chun-jen and Chun-yi should not be placed only under the title of "Taiwan Medical Breakthrough." It is simultaneously a story of medical history, pediatric care history, media history, and two people fighting for autonomous lives. NTU Hospital completed the separation of bodies, and society must continue to learn how to maintain distance between concern and staring.

## After Separation, Life Only Truly Begins

On September 10, 1979, Chun-jen and Chun-yi were separated into two people. That day can be described by surgical time, number of doctors, number of blood donors, and world rankings, but numbers cannot finish the days that followed.

A more truthful way to say it is: the medical team opened a door for them, and the brothers had to spend their lives walking through it. Walking, defecating, hospitalization, work, marriage, giving speeches, losing each other, and even living quietly outside the media—all of these happened only after "successful separation." [^3] [^7]

Chun-jen once said that life truly began after the separation. This statement returns the decision-making power to the two children. Afterward, they gradually moved away from the shared medical terminology of their bodies, away from the single line in the case file, becoming two people who had to endure pain, make choices, and leave memories.

This is also the best way Taiwan preserves this history: remembering the 12 hours of technique, and remembering the 40 years of life that followed. Surgery separated the bodies, but time allowed the two to gradually become themselves. When we discuss this case again, we should first call out the names of Chun-jen and Chun-yi, then discuss what record it represents. Because medical history needs numbers, but human history needs names, choices, and the space to not be spoken for.
