> **30-second overview:** Chen Chih-chung is the eldest son of former President Chen Shui-bian. A graduate of National Taiwan University's Department of Law, he holds two Master of Laws degrees from UC Berkeley and New York University. Between 2010 and 2022, voters in Kaohsiung's Qianzhen-Xiaogang constituency sent him to the City Council three times; however, his terms ended after only eight months and four months respectively. These removals were based on Article 79 of the Local Government Act, following official notices issued by the Executive Yuan. In the finalized verdict for his money laundering case on April 26, 2023, the ruling did not include a deprivation of political rights. One month later, the "black exclusion clause" in the _Public Officials Election and Recall Act_, which passed its third reading in the Legislative Yuan, added money laundering crimes to the list of offenses that disqualify individuals from registering as candidates. The text does not use the word "lifetime," nor does it specify a period for the restoration of rights.

On August 18, 2008, orientation began at the University of Virginia School of Law on the U.S. East Coast. There was a student from Taiwan on the list, but he never showed up.

## He Missed the Orientation at Virginia

The student's name was Chen Chih-chung, and he was intended to pursue a Juris Doctor (J.D.) degree. Hannah, a senior director in the public relations department at the University of Virginia, later explained clearly to Taiwan media: "Because Chen Chih-chung did not attend the orientation courses that began yesterday, he has not completed the registration process and has forfeited his admission to the University of Virginia School of Law." If he wishes to re-enroll, he "can only start from the next academic year" [^1].

He did not return to Virginia; while he truly earned his two Master of Laws (LL.M.) degrees from Berkeley and New York University, his J.D. at Virginia never actually commenced [^2].

Prior to that, his path was a standard trajectory for a legal professional: Taipei Municipal Cheng Kung High School, graduation from the Judicial Group of the Department of Law at National Taiwan University in 2001, an LL.M. from the University of California, Berkeley in 2005, and a second LL.M. from New York University in 2006, after which he planned to return to the United States to prepare for his doctoral studies [^3]. At that time, elections were not part of his life plan.

What prevented him from attending was an ongoing legal proceeding. His father, [Chen Shui-bian](/en/people/chen-shui-bian-controversial-president/), ran alongside [Lu Hsiu-lien](/en/people/annette-lu/) and was elected President and Vice President in 2000, marking Taiwan's first transition of power between political parties. After leaving office in May 2008, the family's overseas fund flows came under investigation; the State Affairs Fund case, the Longtan land purchase case, and the Nangang Exhibition Center case successively moved through the courts. The money laundering aspects of the latter two cases would, fifteen years later, determine whether he could still serve as a Kaohsiung City Councilor [^4].

For someone who studied law, his life path was rewritten by the law for the first time—and it did so by making him miss a registration.

## The Same Constituency, Three Times Sending Him In

Two years later, he appeared on the candidate list for the Kaohsiung City Council elections.

On November 27, 2010, running as an independent in Kaohsiung City's 10th constituency (Qianzhen and Siaogang), he secured 32,947 votes with a 16.83% vote share, making him the highest vote-getter in that constituency[^5]. This was a multi-member constituency where several seats were to be filled at once, with over a dozen names competing on a single ballot. In the 2022 term, 17 candidates competed for 8 seats[^6]. Under this electoral system, the threshold for being the "highest vote-getter" is typically only a little over 10% of the total votes.

The year after securing the highest vote count, he raised the stakes. In September 2011, he announced his run for the Legislative Yuan, switching to Kaohsiung City's 9th constituency. When the votes were counted in January 2012, he lost with 48,941 votes; then-DPP legislator Kuo Min-cheng also lost with 59,258 votes, while Kuomintang's Lin Kuo-cheng was elected with 70,600 votes[^7]. PTS News reported at the time that a "split drama was playing out within the green camp," and in an interview after the election, Kuo Min-cheng attributed his defeat to Chen Chih-chung dividing the vote share[^8]. This electoral loss left his position within the DPP awkward: in 2013, he applied to rejoin the party, but the central party headquarters rejected him based on the "5-year clause" of the _Party Member Admission Regulations_. It was not until February 17, 2015, that the DPP Arbitration Committee overturned this decision, allowing him to return to the party[^9].

In 2018, he returned to the 10th constituency. Running as a member of the DPP, he received 15,938 votes and was elected as the 6th highest vote-getter in the district[^10]. That was the same year Han Kuo-yu was elected Mayor of Kaohsiung, and his vote count dropped by half.

Halfway through his term, someone tried to force him out. In August 2020, the "Zhen-Gang Pest Control Team," composed of residents from Qianzhen and Siaogang, launched a recall campaign. In the first stage, they submitted 3,500 proposals, 3,300 of which passed review, easily clearing the threshold[^11]. His response at the time consisted of only two sentences: "Respecting different public opinions and fulfilling the responsibilities of a representative" and "Working twice as hard to repay the voters"[^11]. The second stage was not as smooth: 29,018 signatures were submitted, but over 13,000 were disqualified due to reasons such as duplicate signatures or not being registered voters in the constituency. This left them 12,417 people short of the legal threshold of 28,062. Kuo Lun-hao, spokesperson for the initiating group, spoke bluntly: "The disqualification rate in the first stage was only 10%, but unexpectedly, the second stage's disqualification rate reached over 40%." He added, "The political landscape of the Siaogang area is quite deeply green; to push for a recall of Chen Chih-chung in this constituency is very unfortunate. We respect the choice." On December 25, 2020, the Central Election Commission declared the recall unsuccessful[^12].

In the same constituency, one side sent him up while the other tried to pull him down; neither side managed to overwhelm the other.

On November 26, 2022, he received 18,545 votes with an 11.53% vote share, returning once again as the highest vote-getter in the constituency[^13]. Across these three election nights, the power of decision truly rested in the hands of the people.

## 60,000 to 70,000 People, 154 Parking Spaces

At 9:00 AM on November 24, 2020, the 35th meeting of the 4th regular session of the 3rd Kaohsiung City Council began with general municipal inquiries. When it was his turn, the first thing he asked about was parking spaces: "Take Zhengangshanzi as an example... there are 60,000 to 70,000 people there, but there are only 154 public roadside parking spaces." He then laid out a comparison of the forward-looking parking budgets for the six major cities: Kaohsiung applied for over NT$1.3 billion and was approved for over NT$800 million; within that same batch of funding, New Taipei applied for over NT$9 billion, Taichung for over NT$3 billion, and Tainan for over NT$2 billion. Kaohsiung had requested the least among the six cities. Mayor Chen Chi-mai responded that the urban planning for the reconstruction of the Taiwan Railways Administration (TRA) Kaohsiung Workshop had already required the addition of parking spaces, and he also promised to inventory city-owned land to increase supply[^14].

![Kaohsiung City Council Chamber](/article-images/people/chen-chih-chung-kcc-chamber.webp)
_The Kaohsiung City Council chamber. The verbatim record of the general municipal inquiry on November 24, 2020, is contained in the Kaohsiung City Council Gazette, Vol. 34, No. 5. Photo: Kaohsiung City Government Information Bureau via Wikimedia Commons, Attribution_

The true centerpiece of that inquiry was Dalinpu, located at the southernmost tip of Siaogang District. It is surrounded on three sides by the CPC Corporation Dalin Refinery, the Taipower Dalin Power Plant, and the Linhua Industrial Park; discussions regarding village relocation have been ongoing since the mid-2010s. On the inquiry floor, he pressed for health surveys and compensation conditions following the discovery of arsenic levels exceeding safety standards in residents' bodies. When the topic turned to funding, he asked: "If the central government doesn't have the money, do we have to budget it ourselves?" The Mayor replied: "If there truly is no funding, the City Government will take responsibility." The Mayor then made itemized promises: for housing compensation, "it will be rebuilt using new prices with the same materials as the original house; you won't have to pay a single cent"; floor area ratios could be relaxed to 300; the City Government would coordinate low-interest loan programs with Bank of Kaohsiung; and regarding pollution compensation and employment quotas for neighbors in state-owned enterprises, he would continue to coordinate with the Ministry of Economic Affairs to ensure priority employment for Dalinpu youth[^15].

_Fenglin Temple in Dalinpu. The relocation area covers six villages in the Dalinpu and Fengbitou areas; how the temples within the settlement will be relocated together is one of the residents' primary concerns. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

During the same inquiry, he raised five other issues: establishing a labor condition evaluation system ("The Labor Affairs Bureau evaluates 'Happy Enterprises,' but those companies only receive trophies—then what?"), establishing a Kaohsiung 228 Memorial Hall, the teacher-student ratio in special education, nutritional lunch subsidies for disadvantaged high school students, and investment and employment figures following the expansion of the Asia New Bay Area 5G+AIoT Park. Regarding the 228 issue, he spoke of the memories left in Kaohsiung by the 1947 suppression: "Many families of the 228 victims—these associations and groups hope Kaohsiung will have its own 228 Memorial Hall, especially since our sacrifices were so heavy at that time, along with heroic stories like the Kaohsiung Commercial High School Self-Defense Force"[^15] (For more on the 228 incident and subsequent purges, see [Taiwan's Transitional Justice](/en/history/taiwan-transitional-justice/)). The Mayor did not provide individual responses to the questions regarding special education teacher-student ratios or high school lunches at that moment.

These were the promises he secured on the inquiry floor. Six years later, they have each taken different paths. The funding for Dalinpu has indeed steadily increased. On October 8, 2019, the Executive Yuan approved the "New Material Circular Industry Park" project with a total budget of NT$105.4 billion, of which NT$58.981 billion was allocated for relocation and resettlement. On December 4, 2023, the Executive Yuan increased the specific fund for relocation and resettlement to NT$80 billion, while the total revised project budget simultaneously rose to NT$151.999 billion. A report by the Central News Agency (CNA) at the time detailed the City Government's successful lobbying path: "The Dalinpu relocation case moved from the Executive Yuan's original approval of NT$58.981 billion in relocation funds, which the City Government successfully lobbied up to NT$69.431 billion, and then further to the NT$80 billion approved by the Executive Yuan today"[^16].

With the money in place, the timeline continues to move. According to the Kaohsiung City Government as of August 2026, the park's "budgeting and planning" has entered the central review process; construction of new roads in the resettlement area began in June 2026 and is scheduled for completion in August 2027; land lotteries and compensation disbursements are not expected to start until 2027. The City Government also disclosed the consent rates for the compensation plan: 94.8% for owners in residential/commercial areas, 92.7% for non-residential/commercial areas, and 95.5% for owners of land improvements; as of the end of July 2026, over 2,500 owners had signed agreements for negotiated purchases[^17]. These figures represent the City Government's own official stance.

The reports did not include the voices of the residents. Their dissatisfaction lies in an earlier period. On May 9, 2024, over three hundred residents from Dalinpu and Fengbitou gathered to petition in front of the Kaohsiung City Council, stating that the conditions in the resettlement plan differed from what was explained during the initial briefing sessions. Hung Hsiu-chu, leader of the "Healthy Grandmothers' Group," spoke about how fragmented plots of land could not be exchanged for adequate land. Chang Hsiao-ming, the village chief of Siaogang Fenglin, calculated the discrepancy for reporters: "The land being collected from us as commoners is NT$147,000 per _ping_, but because we don't have enough total area, we have to buy it back from the City Government at NT$200,000 per _ping_."[^18] In June 2025, PTS's _Our Island_ revisited this case, with Hung Hsiu-chu stating: "It has been 14 years since the relocation issue was first raised; not only is the relocation stuck, but local public construction has also almost come to a standstill"[^19]. In the progress reports for this round in 2026, no opposing parties were interviewed.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> What a city councilor can obtain on the inquiry floor are promises and timelines. What he cannot obtain is whether he will still be in office before those timelines are completed. The Dalinpu land lottery is scheduled for 2027, which is the seventh year since the issue he pursued in 2020—yet his term as a councilor ended in April 2023. The term of a local representative is four years, while the cycle for major engineering projects is ten years or more; this time gap is a shared reality for all local representatives.

The outcomes of the other two issues he raised in 2020 were different. Starting from the 2026 school year, Kaohsiung City will provide free nutritional lunches for all public elementary and junior high schools, but this policy explicitly excludes high schools. A report in January 2026 revealed that public comprehensive high schools would face a "two-system" controversy—free for junior high students and paid for high school students. The report only featured an anonymous high school principal suggesting that subsidies should "first target disadvantaged high school students," but the City Government has not reached a final decision[^20]. Back in 2020, he argued that "subsidies should not be cut off once they reach high school"[^15]; six years later, that line remains exactly where it was. Regarding the follow-up on special education teacher-student ratios, there is no mention in the public records of the City Government or the Council.

## Sentenced to Three Months, So Why Did He Still Lose His Seat?

The first time was not decided by him; it happened in the eighth month of his term as a city councilor.

On August 17, 2011, the Supreme Court concluded the perjury case involving the Chen family: Wu Shu-chen was sentenced to 9 months, while Chen Chih-chung, Chen Hsing-yu, and Chao Chien-ming were each sentenced to 3 months, with only Chen Hsing-yu receiving probation[^21]. The facts of the perjury itself had nothing to do with money: when the High Prosecutors Office's Anti-Organized Crime Center investigated the state secret expenses case, they discovered that the invoices used for reimbursement came from these three individuals. During questioning, they claimed the invoices were for hosting banquets for their parents or for gift-giving, but it was later confirmed to be private consumption[^21].

In most people's intuition, a three-month prison sentence is the lightest level, usually settled by paying a fine. He lost his seat because of how Article 41, Paragraph 1 of the Criminal Code is written. That article sets two conditions that must both be met to qualify for a fine in lieu of imprisonment: the statutory maximum sentence for the crime must be five years or less, AND the sentence handed down by the judge must be six months or less[^22]. The first condition looks at the crime itself, regardless of how long the judge actually sentences the person to. Perjury is covered under Article 168 of the Criminal Code, with a statutory penalty of "imprisonment for not more than seven years"[^23]. Since seven years is above the five-year threshold, this crime was never within the scope of being convertible to a fine from the very beginning; even if a judge sentences someone to three months, it remains the same.

What took over was Article 79, Paragraph 1, Subparagraph 4 of the Local Government Act. The text of that subparagraph states: "A person who commits a crime other than those mentioned in the preceding two subparagraphs, and whose sentence of imprisonment or higher has become final, but who has not been granted probation, has not paid a fine in lieu of imprisonment, or is not permitted to perform community service." A special municipality councilor who meets these criteria shall be removed from office by the **Executive Yuan**[^24]. He had no probation and could not pay a fine; with all three conditions met, his eligibility as a councilor vanished the moment the judgment became final, without waiting for official documents to reach the council[^25]. His term lasted eight months.

Two of his responses at the time were recorded. Upon receiving news of the finalized judgment, he said, "This political maneuvering is too despicable; it is absolutely unacceptable"[^25]. To reporters, he offered four words: "If one wishes to frame a person, there is no shortage of excuses."[^26]

Article 41, Paragraph 3 of the Criminal Code provides an alternative path: for sentences of six months or less that do not meet the requirements for a fine in lieu of imprisonment, one may still apply to perform community service[^22]. He proactively reported to the District Prosecutors Office to apply and was granted permission[^27]. The total duration was 546 hours, calculated by multiplying three months (91 days) by 6 hours per day. The execution site was Dalinpu Prison, where his task was teaching English classes; when there were no classes, he handled library archiving. The prosecution made this arrangement considering his dual master's degrees in law from Berkeley and New York University and his strong foreign language skills, while specifically excluding legal consultation work[^28].

For a person with complete legal training to teach English in a correctional facility, the reason was that his foreign language skills were more useful than his legal expertise. These 546 hours provided no help regarding his status as a councilor: removal from office depends on the status at the moment the judgment becomes final, and community service granted after the fact is not retroactive[^24].

The first time, the law decided for him. It looked at the statutory maximum of the crime, regardless of how severe his actual sentence was.

## What the Verdict Didn't Write, the Legislature Wrote a Month Later

On April 26, 2023, the Supreme Court issued Judgment No. 4948-Tai-Shang of Year 111, dismissing the appeal regarding the money laundering aspects of the Nangang Exhibition Hall case and the Longtan land purchase case. Chen Chih-chung was sentenced to one year in prison and fined NT$1.5 million; the US$8,169,015.3 involved in the case was confiscated. His wife, Huang Jui-ching, was sentenced to 10 months in prison for the same offense, fined NT$1 million, given a four-year reprieve, and ordered to pay NT$1 million to the national treasury[^29]. It was the same machine running again: under Article 79, Paragraph 1, Subparagraph 4 of the Local Government Act, the Executive Yuan issued a removal from office. By the time the Kaohsiung City Council received the official document, his status as a councilor had already lapsed[^30]. This term lasted only four months. He entered prison on May 11, paying the NT$1.5 million fine that same day; his place of incarceration was Kaohsiung Second Prison[^30].

The official press release issued by the Judicial Yuan for this verdict listed only the prison term, the fine, and the confiscation[^29]. The next day, a summary of the judgment in the _Taipei Times_ also listed these three items[^31]. Neither document mentioned "deprivation of civil rights." A month later, he put it more bluntly himself: "The court sentenced me to one year without depriving me of my civil rights, but due to the 'black exclusion clause' in the Election and Recall Act, I am deprived of my civil rights for life."[^32] During that month, the Legislative Yuan did something else.


On May 26, 2023, the Legislative Yuan passed the third reading of the amendment to Article 26 of the _Public Officials Election and Recall Act_, commonly referred to by the media as the "black exclusion clause." It excludes anyone with a finalized conviction for a specific list of crimes from being a candidate: national security, vote-buying, corruption, organized crime, drugs, and firearms, plus Articles 14 and 15 of the Money Laundering Control Act. The voting result was 89 present, 56 in favor, 30 against, and 3 abstentions[^33]; this count was consistent in reports by the _Taipei Times_[^34]. This law covers legislators, local representatives, and local chief executives; presidential and vice-presidential elections are governed by a separate law, the _Presidential and Vice Presidential Election and Recall Act_, which also underwent corresponding amendments that same year[^35].

There is a discrepancy between the text of the article and how the media describes it. The main body of Article 26 does not contain the words "for life." The opening sentence reads, "Any person who falls under any of the following circumstances shall not register as a candidate"; what applied to him was Subparagraph 6, which uses the phrasing "having been convicted of..." without specifying any duration[^36]. The text on the two official government pages—the National Law Database and the Central Election Commission's regulatory system—is identical[^37]. The media's common phrase, "permanently barred from running for office," is a generalization of the effect that "this subparagraph does not specify a period for restoration or any clause for the reinstatement of rights." The article itself leaves a clue: among the 16 grounds in Article 26, four items—bankruptcy, suspension from office, deprivation of civil rights, and declaration of guardianship—all include phrases like "rights not yet restored," "term not yet expired," or "not yet revoked," implying that once the status is cleared, rights are restored. However, the first nine subparagraphs, including Subparagraph 6, contain no such proviso[^36].

What this article removes is the right to be elected. The action regulated by Article 26 is only "registering as a candidate"; Article 14 of the Election and Recall Act, which states that "a citizen of the Republic of China who is twenty years of age or older shall have the right to vote," has not been changed by these two amendments[^38]. He can still vote today.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The common narrative is "because his money laundering conviction was finalized, he is permanently barred from running for office." This sentence connects two events into a single causal line, skipping over one month and one third reading. Deprivation of civil rights under the Criminal Code is an ancillary punishment declared by a judge after weighing an individual case; it has a duration, and rights are restored once that term expires. Article 26 of the Election and Recall Act is a line drawn by legislators across entire categories of crimes; it takes effect even if the court does not mention it, and it specifies no time for restoration. The same person can be judged not to have been deprived of civil rights, yet still be unable to register as a candidate.

The reasons supporting this law are easy to find. Black gold politics in Taiwan has been an accumulating problem for decades; the purpose of the 2023 amendment was to block individuals with criminal records in organized crime, vote-buying, or drugs from appearing on candidate lists, garnering 56 votes in favor across party lines. Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) legislator Wu Li-hua stated on the day of the third reading: "The amendment is intended to prevent those convicted of black gold, firearms, or drug crimes from using elections to obtain power granted by the people, which causes further harm."[^39] In the June 2026 amendment three years later, Kuomintang (KMT) legislator Weng Hsiao-ling used a similar framework but added new crime types: "The amendment to the Election and Recall Act passed in this third reading is to uphold the principle of proportionality regarding constitutional political rights. In recent years, fraud has broken many families, therefore such individuals shall not register as candidates." This same amendment also relaxed candidacy eligibility for those convicted of minor offenses receiving suspended sentences or community service[^40].

This law does not only target one person: KMT proportional representation candidate Lin Wen-rui withdrew from the 2023 Yunlin County legislative election because of a 2004 vote-buying case (sentenced to 8 months, 2 years reprieve, and 2 years deprivation of civil rights, with the judgment finalized in 2005); he was replaced by Ting Hsueh-chung, the mayor of Huwei Township[^41].

Critics focused their fire on the method. New Power Party (NPP) legislator Chiu Hsien-chih called it a "massive democratic rollback" at the time, arguing that restrictions on candidacy should be based on the length of the sentence rather than the type of crime[^39]. Taiwan People's Party (TPP) legislator Chang Chi-lu questioned whether this was an "unlimited expansion that violates the presumption of innocence and the principle of proportionality"[^39]. Liao Yuan-hao, an associate professor in the Department of Law at National Chengchi University, wrote a commentary using former Tainan City Councilor Wang Chia-chen as an example: when she committed her offense, the law stated that a suspended sentence would not affect candidacy; however, after she chose a suspended sentence and paid the fine, the law changed so that those under a suspended sentence could no longer run. His argument was that this violates the principle of legality (_nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege_), which dictates that "punishment for an act shall be limited to what is expressly provided by the law at the time of the act." Since deprivation of civil rights is an ancillary punishment under Article 36 of the Criminal Code, the black exclusion clause effectively adds a punishment that did not exist at the time of the offense[^42]. Wu Wei-chih from the National Institute for Non-profit Organizations argued from another angle: "For those with sentences of 10 years or more whose cases are not yet finalized, defendants lose their political rights; this clearly violates the presumption of innocence and the principle of administrative proportionality."[^43]

Article 26, Subparagraph 6 of the Election and Recall Act still reads "Articles 14 and 15 of the Money Laundering Control Act"[^36], yet the Money Laundering Control Act was amended in its entirety on August 1, 2024, to consist of 31 articles; the original money laundering penalties are now found under Article 19[^44][^45]. Both of these facts can be verified via the original legal texts. The citation in the article did not update alongside the new law—a gap left by legislative technicality. As for what effect this gap has on those with finalized judgments under the old article numbers, neither the Central Election Commission nor the Ministry of the Interior has issued a public interpretation, and the legal community has not held any public discussion on it.

On June 12, 2026, the Legislative Yuan amended the Election and Recall Act again: Subparagraph 6 of Article 26 added "crimes under the Fraud Prevention Act," and Subparagraph 9 added "provided that those sentenced to a fine or a prison term that may be commuted to community service shall not be subject to this limit"; the presidential order was announced on July 1[^40][^46]. This amendment is colloquially known as the "Ko Hung-an clause." During caucus negotiations, DPP legislator Li Po-yi accused the TPP of amending the law for a specific individual; TPP legislator Hsu Chung-hsin countered that it was the DPP who was tailoring laws to individuals, while also asserting that "Article 17 of the Constitution guarantees citizens' rights to vote and be elected... if the Election and Recall Act uses administrative law to further restrict people's right to run for office, it is unconstitutional"[^40]. While both parties accused each other of amending the law for specific people, this political tug-of-war had nothing to do with him: the original list of serious crimes in Subparagraph 6 was not altered by a single word, and he remains on that list.

His own words remain from 2023. During his incarceration, he issued a statement: "The goal of black exclusion is certainly correct, but the method cannot be unconstitutional," as well as, "If it is said that a political death sentence is beneficial to the greater good, then what more can I say? 'I am speechless and looking to the heavens; I want to cry but have no tears!'"[^47] The debate stops there. In the more than three years since this clause took effect, no one in the public record has challenged it in the Constitutional Court, including himself[^48]. Thus, the situation is this: on one side is the argument that the legislative purpose of removing black gold is legitimate, passed with bipartisan support, and applies to more than just one person; on the other side is the argument that permanent exclusion based on crime type violates the principles of proportionality and legality. Both sides have said all they can in public, and the authority capable of providing an answer has received no petitions from anyone. As for migrant workers who work, pay taxes, and live in Taiwan long-term but have never possessed the right to vote or be elected, they stand outside this debate—and their problems are a different matter entirely.

## Is Political Dynasty a Problem? Taiwan Lacks Its Own Research

Scaling him up from an individual to a broader question: in a country's politics, how much of a role do family backgrounds play, and is that influence positive or negative?

There is no consensus in international political science on this matter. A case study on Taiwan published by Christian Schafferer in the 2023 _Asian Journal of Comparative Politics_ stated in its abstract that "legacy politicians are not per se the Pandora's box of low-quality politics"—politicians from political dynasties do not inherently equate to low-quality politics; their behavioral patterns are no different from others with similar social networks. The real variable is whether the degree of political modernization can prevent them from turning public resources into family prey[^57]. In 2018, Nathan Batto conducted a systematic study in _Asian Survey_ on the scale of family politicians at central and local levels in Taiwan, finding that dynasty candidates tend to avoid constituencies with intense competition and perform best in environments with moderate competition[^58].

Literature from the other side reaches the opposite conclusion. Using term limits for local chief executives in the Philippines as a basis for research, Querubin found in 2016 that term limits actually strengthened the persistence of political dynasties because when an incumbent cannot run for re-election, the family will put forward a spouse or child to take over[^59]. Tusalem (2013) studied Philippine provinces individually and found that provinces dominated by dynasties performed worse on governance indicators such as infrastructure, public health expenditure, public security, and employment[^60]. Within the same body of literature, Dal Bó et al. (2009) studied two hundred years of data from the U.S. Congress, concluding that the longer a family remains in office, the more their descendants' advantages in entering Congress become self-reinforcing.

The data in these papers comes from the Philippines and the United States. How many political families exist in Taiwan, how they compete within constituencies, and whether they have a measurable impact on local governance—currently, there is no local empirical study that provides a direct answer. Taiwanese political scholars have discussed this phenomenon, but most of what they say are descriptive commentaries rather than data that can be used to draw definitive judgments.

How rules are written depends on how reliable the understanding of the phenomenon is. The list under Article 26 of the _Public Officials Election and Recall Act_, whether there is a door for those on the list to have their political rights restored, and in which year that door should open—all of these are such judgments. The way Taiwan currently makes these determinations is by relying on votes in the Legislative Yuan in the absence of almost any local empirical evidence (for other aspects of this same issue, see [Taiwanese Elections and Party Politics](/en/history/taiwan-elections-and-party-politics/) and [Taiwan's Judicial Reform and Preventive Detention System](/en/society/taiwan-judicial-reform-and-preventive-detention/)).

A more common version of this question exists in daily life. Whether former felons should disclose their criminal records when seeking employment, how many years a criminal record should be kept, or when a professional whose license has been revoked can reapply for one—they are all asking the same thing: once a person has done what the law requires of them, when does society consider it finished? His case is simply the most visible manifestation of this question.

At around 8:00 PM on February 6, 2024, after serving 272 days in prison, he walked out of the gates of Kaohsiung Second Prison carrying his personal belongings. Facing the media, he only said "Thank you," and his wife, Huang Jui-ching, drove to pick him up and take him home[^61]. That night, he wrote two sentences on Facebook: "I'm home; it was like a dream" and "It's been a long time since I used a mobile phone; it's a bit laggy"[^62].

The prison sentence has an end date; the fine was paid in full on the day he entered prison, and the confiscation has been executed. It won't be until 2027 that Dalinpu residents reach their turn for land allocation through the lottery; the matter he pursued on the interpellation stage is still unfolding. There is only one date that has not ended.
