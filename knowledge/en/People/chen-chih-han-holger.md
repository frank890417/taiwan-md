> **30-second overview:** Kuan Chang did not start from political commentary but from a gym. Chen Chih-han placed weight training, combat sports, e-commerce, and live streaming on the same stage, letting audiences see a boss who bound his body, enterprise, and personal trust together. Later, he brought this "I'm on the ground, you watch along with me" format into anti-red-media mobilization, China livestreams, and cross-Strait issues. Whether this trajectory should be called a pivot, an extension, or simply the continuous metamorphosis of a personal brand, the article leaves the question to the evidence and readers, not prejudging the figure.

Chen Chih-han encountered fitness and sanda (Chinese kickboxing) early on, beginning combat sports training at age 17 under Pi Ching-lung, chairman of the Chinese Combat Sanda Association. In a 2017 interview, he recalled that a bodybuilder's physique without speed, rhythm, and technique remains insufficient in actual combat; this approach of treating the body as something to be trained, tested, and verified later became the brand language of the Genghis Khan Fitness Club. [^1]

In the same interview he discussed his entrepreneurial process: early capital was insufficient, so he first opened a bar, took on disciples, then found investors; the Linkou flagship gym invested roughly NT$100 million, and the venue, equipment, and safety facilities all required real capital. [^1] Thus "Kuan Chang" possessed two identities from the start: a coach teaching people to train their bodies, and a boss handling leases, equipment, staff, and cash flow. His later livestreams were not pure talk shows precisely because they were connected from the beginning to a genuinely operating business.

## The Gym Was the First Studio

In the 2017 interview, Kuan Chang talked about more than muscle. He discussed Taiwan's sports training, scientific division of labor, athletes going abroad to accumulate experience, and criticized how Taiwan often treats athletes as punching bags after defeats. [^1] He understood supporting athletes as long-term investment: send athletes abroad, then pass their experience to the next generation. This content shaped his public image before any explicit political stance formed — someone impatient with the system, yet willing to discuss how to improve it.

His livestreams turned this impatience into a format. Long duration, minimal editing, direct responses to comments — letting audiences feel they were not watching a post-produced speech but standing beside a boss handling today's problems. This immediacy shortened the distance of traditional media and weakened the buffer traditional editors provide: a remark can reach a massive audience in seconds, and be clipped and forwarded within minutes, stripped of its original context.

English-language media refer to him as "Gym Boss," describing him as a fitness influencer who moved from a fitness enterprise into commentary on politics and cross-Strait issues. [^2] This label captures his brand posture: he speaks not with the authority of a scholar or official, but with the stance of a boss who "has lived, fought, and earned money on the ground." Supporters see this as authenticity; critics ask whether personal experience can substitute for data, procedure, and professional scrutiny.

## From Anti-Red-Media to Political Megaphone

In June 2019, Kuan Chang and then-New Power Party legislator Huang Kuo-chang jointly called for the "Reject Red Media, Defend Taiwan's Democracy" parade, gathering crowds on Ketagalan Boulevard; demands included opposing Chinese Communist Party and pro-China media infiltration, defending Taiwan's democracy and freedom, and expressing solidarity with Hong Kong's anti-extradition movement. 2025 coverage reviewing this mobilization also recorded Kuan Chang later stating in a livestream that he would apologize and thank the owners of China Times and CTi TV if he met them. [^3]

The timeline here can be verified; the meaning of the timeline should not be decided by the article on the reader's behalf. The early parade demands, later livestream statements, and the 2025 China trip were indeed placed in the same public discussion; but labeling it a "pivot," "route change," or other political judgment must preserve the subject's own account and the accounts of different observers.

The difference between influencer politics and party politics also emerges here. Kuan Chang does not need to first propose a complete platform, undergo party nomination, or vote clause-by-clause in a legislature; he can open a livestream, issue a call, and gather dispersed emotions onto the street. The cost is that this political trust is deposited in personal tone and personal promises. When the individual's public statements change, followers treat "what you said before" as a political ledger.

## The China Trip: Self-Funded Journey, or a Watched Exchange

In June 2025, Kuan Chang traveled to Shanghai, embarking on a six-day, five-night China livestream itinerary. He styled himself a "peace ambassador," saying he wanted Taiwan audiences to see China with their own eyes; China's Taiwan Affairs Office publicly stated that Kuan Chang and his team went to Shanghai on their own, welcoming Taiwan compatriots to exchange in China. [^4]

The South China Morning Post reported that Kuan Chang described the trip as self-funded, hoping to let Taiwan audiences see the "unfiltered" face of Chinese society; he also said in the livestream that Shanghai's infrastructure and quality of life were better than he had originally imagined. [^2] The key is not whether audiences agree with his observations, but that "unfiltered" itself still contains selection: which streets, malls, urban developments, and companions he chose also determined which China the audience saw.

Yahoo News recorded Kuan Chang's departure, the peace ambassador framing, itinerary arrangements, and the TAO's response regarding his independent travel and cross-Strait exchange. [^4] The Reporter crawled public livestreams, viewership and donation data, and traced the Chinese influencers, media, officials, and cross-border content networks appearing in the itinerary. [^5]

These materials can be placed side by side, not directly assembled into a single answer. Kuan Chang's own account is self-funded civilian exchange; Chinese officials say he traveled independently; investigative reporting focuses on companions and content networks. The article can present how these accounts collide, but not decide for readers what nature this trip ultimately was.

Another Reporter investigation further uses Li Hsiang, the Chinese influencer who hosted Kuan Chang in Hangzhou, as a case study, analyzing traceable links among his media platforms, united front organizations, and cross-border content networks. [^6] This makes the "China trip" not just a travelogue of which cities one person visited, but also a question: do the people in front of the lens, the people arranging the scenes, and the people providing the platforms all jointly decide what the audience sees?

## Livestreaming Is Not Transparent Glass

"Full livestream" sounds like removing editing, but it remains a form of selection. Which city to start from, which street scene enters the frame, who gets to speak on camera, and what stays off-camera — all are part of the content. Real-time does not mean frameless; no editing does not mean no framing.

This difference is especially important in cross-border content. Taiwan audiences see several visible facets of a Shanghai trip; China audiences may see a Taiwan influencer describing China; the Taiwan government sees a livestream crossing information controls and cross-Strait political contexts. [^7] These viewings can coexist, yet no single one can monopolize the whole reality.

In July 2025, Kuan Chang appeared on China Central Television, with his arm tattoos mosaiced in post-production. Taiwan media recorded netizens mocking this as "invisible wings"; Kuan Chang himself said he could understand it and regarded appearing on CCTV as something to be happy about. [^8] Here at least three layers of fact must not be conflated: the footage was mosaiced, what netizens called it, and how Kuan Chang interpreted it. As for CCTV's specific internal decision-making, existing articles do not provide sufficient evidence for this article to reach a conclusion.

The mosaic is worth observing not because tattoos themselves can prove a person's political stance, but because it places a body once used to build a "dare to speak, dare to fight, dare to act" image into another set of public image rules. For some Taiwan audiences, this resembles entering another market and having to accept the other side's framing rules; for Kuan Chang, it may simply be an experience of accessing a major platform. Both interpretations should be labeled as interpretations, not written as a single fact.

## The Boundaries of "Dare to Speak"

Kuan Chang's bluntness is a brand asset and a risk source. In October 2025, the Taipei Times reported that in a livestream he discussed China's "decapitation" tactics against Taiwan, using phrasing like "chop off Lai Ching-te's head"; Chen Chih-han told media his words were taken out of context, that he was paraphrasing a military concept and opposing war; the Criminal Investigation Bureau has launched an investigation. [^9] Here the investigation cannot be written as a conviction, nor can his defense be deleted; the public significance lies in how irony, paraphrase, and threat in a live broadcast may produce different effects once removed from their original context.

In November the same year, Taiwan News reported a former partner's allegations that Kuan Chang accepted money from the Chinese side and used livestreams to criticize the Taiwan government, also recording Chen Chih-han acknowledging he sought financial opportunities in the China market, stating he misjudged the situation, and denying the allegations. [^10] The article only writes it as the allegations and responses carried by media, not determining facts for either side. For figures like Kuan Chang, the verification difficulty lies in fan support, business interests, political speech, and legal or workplace disputes all appearing simultaneously in a single personal brand.

## The Two Faces of Influencer Publicness

Kuan Chang's story should not be reduced to "fitness influencer suddenly becomes political figure." He still speaks through his body. TaiwanPlus reported his participation in a Brazilian jiu-jitsu tournament, winning his first match then withdrawing from subsequent matches due to an arm injury, also recording his promotion of the sport through competition. [^11] The body remains his public language, except this language now simultaneously addresses sports, enterprise, cross-Strait relations, media, and national identity.

His influence comes not just from follower count but from a seemingly contradictory combination: he uses "I'm not an expert, I just say what I see" to lower the comprehension threshold; precisely because he is seen as unvarnished, some audiences may treat intuition as more credible than institutions. This relationship may be encouraging in fitness instruction, but in public policy and cross-Strait politics it demands higher data transparency.

Viewership fluctuations across different platforms and stages also remind us that volume does not equal stable audience, much less long-term trust. Viewers can support his China exchange, or question the itinerary choices and companions; can accept his claim of being misunderstood, or demand full livestreams, financial flows, and conflicts of interest be examined. Publicness is not an honor automatically granted by follower count, but the accountability to be questioned after every utterance.

Chen Chih-han went from gym boss to livestreamer to protagonist of cross-Strait exchange, not by leaving his original business model but by pushing it onto a larger stage. The gym provides bodily authority; livestreaming provides real-time trust; e-commerce provides revenue; political conflict provides reasons for sustained viewing. These four stack together to form "Kuan Chang," a role larger than any individual résumé.

Therefore, understanding Kuan Chang does not require first answering "which side is he really on." More useful questions are: on each stage, what does he choose to put in the frame and leave out of it, and when audiences demand verification, how much is he willing to hand over. As for how to name the space between the 2019 anti-red-media mobilization and the 2025 China livestream, this article gives no answer — public records can confirm timing and accounts; the naming is a judgment readers must make for themselves.
