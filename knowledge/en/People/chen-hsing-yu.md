> **30-second overview:** In 2009, Chen Hsing-yu went to New York to take a dental exam, but was continuously pursued by Taiwanese media outside the exam site, leaving the English quote, "I am not a wanted fugitive."[^3] In 2016, she opened a dental clinic in Tainan, shifting the topic from her political family to equipment, disinfection, and patient care.[^2] In 2026, she confirmed outside her clinic that she had been divorced for some time, stating that "everything is normal"; the key contrast in this life is that while the public knows her best for her political identity, she has rearranged her daily life through her profession as a dentist.[^1]

## Outside the New York Exam Site, Private Itinerary Becomes a Public Trial

On January 31, 2009, Chen Hsing-yu arrived at Newark Airport in New York. Citing a Central News Agency report, BBC Chinese stated that she was scheduled to take a nationwide American dental exam on February 2 and had previously studied short-term training related to dental implants in New York.[^4] These are the itinerary details confirmed by reports. As for whether her trip to the US had other political purposes, media and commentators at the time offered different guesses, but those were not actions confirmed by her and should not be conflated with the exam itself.

The next day, the _Taipei Times_ reported that she was followed by Taiwanese reporters on the streets of Manhattan, who had been pursuing her since her arrival in the US. The report recorded her saying to the reporters: "Why do you keep following me? I am not a wanted fugitive." The same report also noted that her father and other family members were facing judicial pressure related to money laundering cases at the time, but Chen Hsing-yu herself was not implicated.[^3]

This English quote can be found verbatim in the original report, so the original text is preserved rather than back-translating it into Chinese to pretend she said those Chinese sentences. Another quote from her was: "Do you want me to die? What exactly do you want?"[^3] Readers need not add a diagnosis to her emotions. The question itself is enough to reveal the sense of oppression on the other side of the lens.

> "I am not a wanted fugitive" — Chen Hsing-yu's English quote in response to pursuing reporters in New York in 2009; in Chinese, it can be understood as "I am not a fugitive," but the English is retained here to avoid mistaking a translation for a verbatim Chinese quote.[^3]

📝 **Curator's Note:** When a person's father is the president, society often understands "family members of public figures" as "public property." But press freedom does not automatically cancel out the private space of family members. Pursuing political responsibility and chasing a person preparing for an exam must be two different things.

## Her Profession Is Not a Footnote to Politics

Public data listed in the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Medical Practitioner Inquiry System shows that Chen Hsing-yu practices in Tainan City, with her main specialty being General Dentistry. Her main registration category and certificate category are both Dentist, and she holds a specialist qualification in Orthodontics.[^5] This official record proves her professional identity and specialty. It cannot lead to deductions about her income, number of patients, private life, or character evaluation.

In December 2016, ETtoday reported that she opened a dental clinic in Tainan after more than a year of preparation. The report noted that she introduced an intraoral scanner in the clinic, a device that synthesizes 3D images of the oral cavity to assist in making dental molds. She also stated that the clinic hopes to provide professional and warm treatment, and values infection control.[^2] These contents come from interviews before the opening and do not constitute permanent guarantees for the clinic's current status, so this article places them back in the 2016 timeline.

The clinic's English name is **FORMOSA**. According to ETtoday's report at the time, Chen Hsing-yu explained that this name points to the land of Taiwan that nurtured her upbringing.[^2] This is a small object, yet more reliable than "she wants to start over": a signboard, a scanner, a clinic open for consultations in Tainan—all are things that can be seen by readers and verified by sources.

In her 2016 interview, she said she hoped the clinic would "exclude political factors," allowing patients to receive professional and warm treatment.[^2] The significance of this statement lies not in her ability to completely leave politics, but in her clear understanding that when patients come to the dental chair, they first need to face teeth and pain, not the Presidential Office, political parties, or news channels.[^2]

Here we must also pause to explain the boundaries of the data. Public sources allow us to confirm her professional qualifications, statements made at the time of the clinic's opening, and several public scenes captured by the media. They do not allow us to write her childhood, marital details, parent-child relationships, or patient personal information. If a biographical article fills the unknown with stories, it may read more smoothly, but it loses its most basic credibility.

📝 **Curator's Note:** For Chen Hsing-yu, the clinic is not a stage to "whitewash" a political family. It is more like a way to shorten her name. Hanging on the door is not "The President's Daughter," but "Dr. Chen" and a place where a patient can sit down and speak.

## What the Media Pursuit Left Behind Is Not Just Her Anger

A recognizable scene is often closer to the truth than a circulating nickname. In New York in 2009, there were airports, exams, Manhattan, reporters, and continuous questioning. These elements together constitute the event; it cannot be reduced to just five words: "She flew into a rage again."[^3] [^4]

In July 2009, the Democratic Progressive Party publicly called for media self-discipline, stating that during interviews with Chen Hsing-yu, microphones and cameras were used to block her path, and demanding that the media respect the interviewee's willingness and human rights.[^6] This is a party statement, not an independent investigative report. It can prove that there was indeed public controversy and a party stance at the time, but cannot alone prove every detail of the scene.

During the same period, English data published on the Legislative Yuan website also recorded different reactions from the outside world regarding Chen Hsing-yu being pursued by the media, including some legislators expressing sympathy for her situation.[^7] These data are easily simplified into "she just lost control," but that would shrink the problem of an entire interview environment into the expression of one woman.

More importantly, the news in 2009 did not provide a label sufficient to conclude her life. She could fly into a rage in front of the camera, or prepare for exams in her professional field. A person's reaction should not be used to replace the verification of the event's context.

BBC's 2009 report similarly placed her trip to the US for the exam in the context of the Chen Shui-bian family's judicial cases, and mentioned that society had different associations regarding the purpose of her departure.[^4] But between "society speculated" and "the speculation is true," there is a line that cannot be omitted by news narratives. For a biographical article, this line is the starting point of honesty.

## In the Tainan Clinic, Life Gained a Schedule Again

If the object on the New York streets was a microphone, the object in the Tainan clinic is an intraoral scanner. The former pushed her into the lens; the latter gave her the opportunity to return the topic to the patient's mouth and treatment.[^2]

The 2016 report provided a rare glimpse of daily life: Chen Hsing-yu introduced equipment in her own clinic, discussed infection control, and said the clinic only sees patients until 5:30 PM, with no evening sessions. The report connected this arrangement with family life.[^2] This does not mean we know all her family divisions of labor; it only means that in the interview at the time, she viewed clinic operations and life arrangements as the same problem requiring thought.

This is also the time scale most different from political news. Political news is calculated by "whether it explodes today," while dental work advances through appointments, examinations, treatments, and follow-ups. The former requires immediate response; the latter requires patients to sit back in the same chair again and again. The work she chose inherently requires that trust cannot be built in one night.

In that clinic photo, Chen Hsing-yu wore a white coat and sat beside the equipment. The photo is not her entire life, nor does it prove every patient's experience. It only preserves a verifiable moment: before the 2016 opening, she, as the clinic owner, explained her work to the media.[^2]

## 2026: She Pushed the News Back to the Origin of the Question

On August 13, 2026, the Central News Agency reported that Chen Hsing-yu accepted media inquiries outside her dental clinic in East District, Tainan, confirming that the divorce had been "quite a while," and stating that "everything is normal." She also said that if the media was interested, it should ask Zhao Chien-ming, not her.[^1] A same-day report published by Yahoo News recorded the same core response, adding that she said she was "afraid the media was too bored, so [she] created a little news."[^8]

This incident also involved online comments, clinic responses, and various accusations. The Central News Agency explicitly wrote: some content was signed online comments, and Chen Hsing-yu stated she could not verify them. Zhao Chien-ming's clinic responded that the relevant claims were "completely not facts."[^1] Therefore, this article does not write those unverified accusations as facts, nor does it judge her based on marital details.

What can be confirmed is only a few things: she publicly stated that they had been divorced for some time; she accepted inquiries outside her Tainan clinic; she said she was in good spirits and seeing patients as usual; the media and the parties involved had different statements regarding the authenticity of online comments.[^1] Other content, if no reliable independent source is found, remains in the position of "unverified."

This choice may not be sensational enough, but it is closer to the responsibility of biographical writing. A person is not about cutting all rumors into a climax, but letting readers know: which words were spoken by her, which were relayed by the media, and which are just claims raised by someone but not yet proven.

📝 **Curator's Note:** In 2009, she asked reporters, "What exactly do you want?" In 2026, she said, "You should ask him, not me." These two sentences, separated by seventeen years, may point to the same thing: when the news pushed her to the center, she still tried to send the question back to the person who could truly answer it.

## Do Not Reconcile for Her, Nor Condemn Her

Chen Hsing-yu's public image has undergone several rapid switches: the president's daughter, the New York examinee, the pursued family member, the dental clinic operator, and the 2026 protagonist appearing again in the news scene. Every title is true, but no single title is sufficient to cover her.

Official medical data provides her verifiable position as a dentist; 2009 domestic and international media provided a historical slice of media pursuit; 2016 interviews left scenes of her introducing the clinic and medical equipment; 2026 Central News Agency recorded her responding to marriage news with a few sentences outside the clinic.[^1] [^2] [^3] [^4] [^5]

These sources pieced together do not form a complete conclusion of "she is finally understood." They only allow us to see a more accurate position: Chen Hsing-yu has not left the public gaze, but she has always been pulling her life back to places where she can work—the exam site, the treatment room, the clinic door. The news remembered her anger; what patients might remember is another, slower fragment: whether she opened on time, whether she did the examination well, whether she was willing to still call your name at the next follow-up.

The question on the New York streets in 2009 was not solved by a headline; the marriage news in 2026 should not have its conclusion completed by bystanders for her. What can be verified, we leave. What cannot be verified, we do not supplement with answers. The clinic door must still open after the news disperses, and her name, after all, is not just the surname of a political family.
