# Kevin Chen: From Yongjing to Berlin, a Writer Forever Chased by His Hometown

In 1976, Chen Sihong was born in Bade Lane, Yongjing Township, Changhua County. It was a farming family; he was the ninth child, with seven older sisters and one older brother. [^2]

Before age eighteen, he was cared for by his family. Only after moving north to attend university at eighteen did he first discover that daily life required handling things himself. In 2004, he went to Berlin as a special correspondent for Macroview Television, and later made that city his second home. [^3]

Chen Sihong graduated from the Department of English at Fu Jen Catholic University and the Graduate Institute of Drama and Theatre at National Taiwan University. Besides writing, he works in translation, acting, and film festival interpretation. These cross-disciplinary experiences give his novels the precision of language work, the pacing of theatrical scenes, and a performer's sensitivity to the body. [^15]

This life trajectory resembles a successful escape: from the Changhua countryside to Taipei, then from Taipei to Europe. But Chen Sihong's novels and essays repeatedly prove that leaving one's hometown does not mean shaking it off. On one hand he wants to flee; on the other he writes Yongjing into _Ghost Town_. In Berlin he gains distance, yet through that distance he sees his family, patriarchy, and his own body anew.

These four numbers compress his creative trajectory into a readable line: birthplace provides the native soil, birth order provides family structure, Berlin provides distance, and translation rights bring local stories to more distant readers. [^2] [^3] [^4] [^10]

> "I've always wanted to escape Yongjing, yet I keep writing Yongjing." [^3]

📝 Curator's Note: Chen Sihong's contrast isn't "country bumpkin becomes international writer," but rather the farther he goes, the more clearly Yongjing follows behind.

## How a Family Became the Initial Impetus for Writing

In his essays, Chen Sihong looks back at his own birth. His father was the eldest son of a farming family, bearing the pressure to produce a male heir. His mother gave birth to seven daughters in succession; only when the eighth child was a son did the family temporarily cease its expectations. Afterwards, Chen Sihong was born as the ninth child. This is not merely a family register, but a system that sequences male inheritance, female labor, and family values. [^2]

He regards the clamor of eleven family members crowded in a small Changhua countryside house as the source of stories. At age seven or eight, he was already composing sentences on the backs of expired calendars. In fourth grade, he wrote his first novel. Those quarrels, funerals, sibling ruptures, and irrational scenes in his later works are not fabricated spectacles, but family life with the volume turned up by literature.

This also explains why he doesn't write "place" as a scenic postcard. Yongjing is not a few historic houses, an old street, or a snack list; it is a place where, from birth, a body is assigned its position. Who is the eldest son, who can inherit property, who must care for others, who gets to leave—these questions precede literature, yet all eventually enter it.

## Escaping to Berlin, Only to Discover Distance Is Also a Writing Tool

In interviews, Chen Sihong says what drew him to Berlin was not romantic exoticism, but that it allows people not to conform to a single life template. After moving to Berlin in 2004, he maintained a fixed writing routine: rise early, write until noon, cook, nap, then exercise. [^4]

He once described his first night in Berlin: unable to sleep due to jet lag, he went out for a walk. From the clamorous streets of Taipei during a presidential election, he walked into the calm black night of a European city, feeling for the first time a "vast and concrete loneliness," yet simultaneously saying he was happy. [^4]

This seemingly contradictory sentence approaches the core of Chen Sihong's work: freedom is not perpetual bustle, but the capacity to endure being alone with oneself. Leaving his native place gave him space; solitude gave him the chance to discern which of the voices from family, gender, and place still belong to him.

> "You can be completely outside that box; as long as you don't disturb others, it's fine—you can freely live as yourself." [^4]

📝 Curator's Note: Berlin didn't turn Chen Sihong into another person. It finally gave him the distance to look back and see his original self.

## Ghost Town: The Hometown Is Not a Backdrop, but a Character That Chases You

Published in 2019, _Ghost Town_ sets its story in Yongjing, Changhua. The novel's Chen Tien-hung flees to Berlin, Germany, where his queer partner's death, imprisonment, and post-release return home intertwine. The absurd plot where humans and ghosts coexist wraps family memory, small-town transformation, and queer identity. [^5]

The "ghosts" in the novel refer not only to spirits. They also refer to a person who thinks they've left, yet is still summoned back by family discipline, local gazes, and unprocessed trauma. Mirror Fiction later reconstructed the novel's geography through a Yongjing walking tour, passing Yongxing Swimming Pool, Yongjing Elementary School, Yong'an Temple, Chengjiao Ma, Beast Spirit Stele, Yongjing Story Wall, and Yongjing Junior High. Literature here no longer stays on the page, but lets readers walk into the borderland between the author's childhood and the novel. [^6]

It must be clearly distinguished: currently verifiable work data point to Yongjing as the core prototype for _Ghost Town_, not Erlin. Erlin is certainly an important place in Changhua, but it belongs to a different local-history lineage. In 1924, sugarcane farmers in Erlin and surrounding areas petitioned officials over cane prices; in 1925, Dr. Li Ying-chang led the formation of the Erlin Sugarcane Farmers Association, and on October 22 a clash erupted when the sugar monopoly forced cane harvesting, creating what later became known as the Erlin Incident. [^7]

Mistaking Erlin for _Ghost Town_'s story setting gets both the novel's geography and local history wrong. A better reading lets Yongjing and Erlin each keep their place: the former is the familial native soil Chen Sihong repeatedly writes; the latter is the social memory left by Changhua's peasant movement.

## From The Florida Metamorphosis to the "Summer Trilogy"

After _Ghost Town_, Chen Sihong turned his gaze to Florida in 1991. _The Florida Metamorphosis_ writes of six sixteen-year-olds on an overseas study tour, briefly escaping family discipline amid scorching heat and foreign distance, only to be forced thirty years later to confront buried memories by a tragedy. [^3]

He calls _Ghost Town_, _The Florida Metamorphosis_, and _The Good Person Upstairs_ the "Summer Trilogy." The three books have different geographies: Yongjing, Florida, Yuanlin, and Berlin; but summer in them is not seasonal decoration, but pressure that deforms people. Heat makes bodies sweat, and also makes desires, fears, and secrets seep out from under the rules that once contained them.

_The Good Person Upstairs_ shifts to a female perspective viewing Yuanlin and Berlin, addressing women's appearance, sex, mother-daughter relationships, and family trauma. In _The Three Sisters of Shetou_, he pulls the gaze back to Shetou beside Yongjing, writing of three sisters from a spirit-medium family, using black comedy to handle the sorrow and absurdity of a small place. These novels can be seen as a map of Changhua, but more like an emotional map: where people flee from, and where they ultimately return.

> "The place everyone finds least sexy right now is the place I most want to write about." [^8]

This statement doesn't apply a romantic filter to the nativist. It acknowledges that small places can be conservative, noisy, hurtful, and that those places not seen by urban culture as "having stories" often hide the densest lives.

## "Absurdity" as Counterattack Against the Compliant Life

Chen Sihong says he deliberately pursues absurdity because Taiwan culture places great emphasis on rules and becoming compliant people. In this context, absurdity is not escapism, but rather pushing reality to a position readers cannot ignore. [^8]

His characters often do irrational things, but the predicaments they face are concrete: how parents view their child's gender, how families allocate care and property, how localities treat those who don't fit norms, whether one must return after leaving. Magical realism and black comedy prevent these questions from being sealed off by a preaching tone.

He also brings his drama studies training into his novels. Every character has their own notebook, containing living habits, bedsheet colors, shampoo used, and temples worshipped. For him, characters cannot merely serve themes; they must first become people who quarrel, make mistakes, and change. [^8]

> "Pressure is good for a creator—it makes the author less narcissistic. Narcissism is a writing taboo; the novel must leave the 'I' behind to take shape." [^9]

## From Taiwan Awards to Global Readers

_Ghost Town_ won the Taiwan Literature Golden Classics Award Annual Million-Dollar Grand Prize and the Ministry of Culture Golden Tripod Award for Literary Books. The English version was translated by Darryl Sterk (Shih Tai-lun) and supported by the Ministry of Culture's Translation and Publication Subsidy Program. In 2022, the English edition _Ghost Town_ entered the New York Times Autumn Book List. Europa Editions' English publishing materials also document the book's overseas promotion history. [^5] [^13]

This international publishing experience is not merely "a book translated into English." The National Museum of Taiwan Literature notes that foreign rights involve translation, rights promotion, cover design, publisher marketing, and overseas reviews. Chen Sihong also said in lectures that a complete English translation helps attract international scouts, literary agents, and rights buyers. By the end of 2023, _Ghost Town_ had sold rights in twelve languages including English, Greek, Polish, Thai, and Korean. [^10]

Here is a contrast worth remembering: Chen Sihong's novels write about a "place hardly anyone has heard of," but they don't reach the international stage by erasing the place. On the contrary, the more concrete Yongjing, the less respectable the family, the harder the characters are to categorize, the more likely the story is to cross language borders.

> "Literature isn't a money-losing product." [^10]

This statement points not only to writer income, but to whether Taiwan literature can be treated as a cultural asset worth translating, investing in, and managing long-term. Starting from the local need not mean being trapped in the local; but going global should not come at the cost of erasing the local.

## When "Swim Trunks" Enters High School Textbooks

In August 2026, Chen Sihong's essay "Swim Trunks" sparked discussion after being selected for the Han Lin Publishing Grade 12 Chinese textbook. What was first amplified online was the wording describing accidentally grabbing a lifeguard's genitals while drowning; support and criticism quickly split into two camps. [^11]

One basic fact in this incident cannot be omitted: this essay is an older work, originally published in the Liberty Times Supplement, later collected in the essay collection _The Ninth Body_, and Chen Sihong stated the work was authorized by him personally. Therefore, the controversy is not just "why does a certain word appear in the textbook," but also includes how the publisher selects texts, how reviewers read them, and whether an essay dealing with bodily anxiety can be fully discussed in the classroom. [^11]

When responding to the public, Chen Sihong asked readers to finish the full text first. He said he hopes the next generation can have less anxiety about their bodies and grow up happy. On the other hand, some critics examined the essay's grammar and textbook suitability sentence by sentence, questioning whether it is worthy of student emulation, and demanding the selection and review process be examined. [^12]

This controversy deserves to be preserved, not reduced to "conservatives don't understand literature" or "textbooks shouldn't contain vulgar words." A textbook is a public choice. Once a work enters a textbook, it no longer belongs only to the author and original readers, but must face how teachers guide, how students understand, and how teaching materials handle body, sex, shame, and humor.

More importantly, "Swim Trunks" continues Chen Sihong's consistent writing direction: first put the body before the reader, then ask why we only see that word, but not what the character fears. This doesn't mean everyone must like this essay, nor that textbook selections shouldn't be criticized; it only reminds us that before criticizing a work, we should at least let its full context appear.

## What the Hometown Ultimately Leaves Behind

In Chen Sihong's works, returning home is usually not a healing-style grand reunion. Returning to Yongjing might mean a mother's funeral, family quarrels, ancestral land debts, or a walking tour; returning to the body might mean acknowledging one's difference from traditional family expectations; returning to literature means entrusting the unspeakable to characters to bear for oneself.

He once wrote of his mother's cremation, where the family followed custom by having males use chopsticks to pick up the bones. When he took the chopsticks and saw the absurd gender order, death did not become solemn; instead the system revealed its ridiculousness. This scene has power not because it condemns the hometown, but because it lets one see: some traditions persist only because everyone keeps pretending they cannot be changed. [^2]

So Chen Sihong is not simply a "Changhua writer," nor just a "writer residing in Germany." He is more like a constant shuttle-translator: translating family into novels, translating place into scenes world readers can enter, translating bodily shame into discussable language, then bringing Berlin's distance back to Yongjing.

📝 Curator's Note: He writes not "finally successful after escaping hometown," but "after successfully escaping, still willing to look back and write the hometown clearly."

## Conclusion: Leaving Is Not a Period

From Bade Lane in 1976, to post-2004 Berlin, to the _Ghost Town_ Yongjing walking tour, Chen Sihong's life has been in constant motion. But his literature is not a straight line leaving place and arriving at the world; it is a circle: escape grants distance, distance lets one re-recognize home, and only after re-recognition can one write works that neither prettify nor abandon the hometown.

This is also where his works truly become international. Readers may never have lived in Yongjing, Shetou, or Yuanlin, nor experienced large families, queer identity, or funeral customs, but everyone may have a place they don't want to return to, yet always returns to in memory.

Chen Sihong calls that place "Ghost Town." Readers reaching the end may discover: ghosts are not necessarily the dead; ghosts may be the part of us we thought we'd left, yet still speaks inside our bodies.
