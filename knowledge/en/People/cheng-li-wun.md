# Cheng Li-wun

## 1988: A Hunger Strike at the Gates of NTU

In the winter of 1988, a 19-year-old female university student was on a hunger strike at the gates of National Taiwan University (NTU).

Her name was Cheng Li-wun (鄭麗文), a native of Yunlin. Her father was a veteran from Yunnan who had moved to Taiwan as part of the _rongmin_ (military dependents) population, and her mother was a local from Yunlin. [^1] Raised in Jingzhong Third Village in Tainan, she grew up in a household that blended second-generation "mainlanders" with local Taiwanese. During her freshman year, she joined the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), which had been formed shortly after the lifting of martial law, identifying herself as a "fundamentalist" supporter of Taiwan independence. [^2]

The hunger strike was for a man named Huang Hua. Because Huang advocated for Taiwan independence, the government indicted him on charges of "sedition." Cheng set up a station at the gates of NTU, refusing to eat until the authorities released him. At that time, she believed she was engaged in a pure act of resisting authoritarianism and fighting for independence.

She later recalled in an interview: She waited for several days, but those who came to persuade her to withdraw were not KMT agents, but members of the DPP. They told her, "Huang Hua is a troublemaker; don't get involved with him. Your hunger strike is making the party very embarrassed." [^3]

This was her first realization that the "Taiwan independence" in her mind and the "Taiwan independence" actually practiced by the DPP might not be the same thing at all. Thirty-seven years later, on April 10, 2026, she walked into the Fujian Hall of the Great Hall of the People in Beijing and said to Xi Jinping: "Both sides of the Strait are Chinese."

What happened between that hunger-striking student and this KMT Chairperson speaking in Beijing?

> **30-Second Overview**: Cheng Li-wun was born in 1969 into a Tainan _rongmin_ family. In 1988, she joined the DPP and went on a hunger strike at NTU for the Huang Hua independence case. She was elected as a member of the National Assembly in 1996, left the DPP in 2002 following the Wu Rui-ren incident, and was recruited into the Kuomintang (KMT) by Lien Chan in 2005. Since 2008, she has served three terms as a legislator and as a spokesperson for the Executive Yuan; from 2014 to 2015, she hosted the political talk show _Li-wun's Strait Talk_ on TVBS. In October 2025, she was elected Chairperson of the KMT, becoming the second woman in party history to be directly elected as leader; her campaign slogan was "I am Chinese." After taking office, she became embroiled in controversy regarding a memorial service for a communist spy during a White Terror Autumn Memorial. On April 10, 2026, she represented the KMT in a meeting with Xi Jinping in Beijing, making her the first leader of a major political party in the Republic of China to meet the General Secretary of the CCP in a decade.

## How an Academic Achiever Learned International Politics

Cheng Li-wun is not just a student activist with a single hunger strike story.

In 1993, she obtained an LL.M. in International Law from the Beasley School of Law at Temple University in the United States. [^4] Then, in 2000, she earned an MSc in International Relations from the University of Cambridge while serving as a PhD candidate in International Relations. [^5] These two degrees played a subtle role throughout her subsequent career—when she later discussed terms like "KMT-CCP platform," "peace framework," and "institutional arrangements to prevent war," she was someone who knew exactly what those terms meant in international political textbooks.

From 1996 to 2000, Cheng represented Taipei City as a member of the National Assembly under the DPP banner for four years. During this time, she also served as the Deputy Director of the Youth Development Department at the DPP Central Committee and as the Deputy Convener of the DPP caucus in the National Assembly. This was a typical trajectory for the first generation of student activists entering the establishment after martial law.

In 2002, following a sexual harassment case involving Wu Rui-ren within the DPP, Cheng resigned from her positions in the party due to dissatisfaction with how the central committee handled the matter. [^6] This was her formal departure from the "Green" camp, but her later explanation was more blunt: "I realized that Taiwan independence had long since become a joke and a scam." [^7]

In 2005, she officially joined the KMT at the invitation of Lien Chan. A woman in her thirties, with an LL.M., Cambridge international relations training, and a history of hunger-striking for Taiwan independence—such a resume was an anomaly within the KMT of 2005. And that was exactly what Lien Chan wanted: an anomaly.

## Warrior, Controversy, and a Television Show

After joining the KMT, Cheng first served as a spokesperson, then as a spokesperson for the Executive Yuan, and in 2008 entered the Legislative Yuan as a party-list legislator.

Within the party, she earned the nickname "Warrior" (_zhanjiang_). This was due to her sharp rhetoric, her skill at appearing on political talk shows, and her refusal to shy away from the camera. During the Ma Ying-jeou era, the "Blue" camp often felt like it was being suppressed; Cheng was one of the few who could fight back head-on. _CNA_ described her election as chairperson as "breaking through with her warrior image." [^8]

But being a "warrior" came at a cost. In 2005, while serving as the Chairperson of the KMT's Culture and Communications Committee, she named and criticized the DPP Taichung mayoral candidate Lin Chia-lung as a "corrupt mobster" in an election advertisement. Lin sued her under the Election and Recall Act; the first instance sentenced her to three months in prison and one year of deprivation of civil rights, which was later commuted to one month of detention with probation. [^9] Another controversy involved her violating KMT regulations regarding party positions by hosting more than the permitted four hours of radio programming per week. Neither controversy was fatal, but they left a common label on her political career: **"Highly combative, but often crosses the line."**

From 2014 to 2015, she hosted the TVBS political program _Li-wun's Strait Talk_. [^10] This show solidified her "political commentary style": direct, sharp, and incisive, often using a single sentence to corner an opponent. This style was directly related to her path toward becoming KMT Chairperson—she did not rely on organizational power or local factions, but on being "good at speaking."

On October 18, 2025, the results of the 12th KMT Chairperson election were announced. Defeating former Taipei Mayor Hau Lung-bin and current legislator Lo Chih-chiang with approximately 50% of the vote, Cheng became the second woman in KMT history to be directly elected as party leader, after Hung Hsiu-chu. [^11]

The key slogan of her campaign was only six words: **"I am Chinese."**

## From "Hunger Strike for Independence" to "I am Chinese"

This is the most difficult part of Cheng Li-wun's persona to understand.

Her version is this: In her youth, she believed Taiwan independence was a righteous path, but after entering the political establishment, she found the DPP's definition of "Taiwan independence" to be fundamentally contradictory—claiming to want independence while never actually pushing for it institutionally. She called this gap "the Taiwan independence scam," [^12] and even more fiercely, "Taiwan independence fascism." [^13] She maintains that her political stance hasn't changed; rather, her judgment of "who is telling the truth" has.

The opponents' version is this: Cheng Li-wun was a principled student activist who was systematically "reconstructed" by the old KMT system under Lien Chan into a combatant friendly to Beijing. Every one of her "anti-independence" declarations was merely a means to climb to the next position. Her ability to sit in the Fujian Hall of the Great Hall of the People in 2026 to converse with Xi Jinping is the ultimate result of this reconstruction.

Both versions have evidence, and both have blind spots.

The blind spot in the first version is: If the judgment that "Taiwan independence is a scam" came from genuine experience, why did that judgment lead precisely to "joining another party that happens to welcome such a judgment"? A purely epistemological shift would not coincide so perfectly with a professional trajectory.

The blind spot in the second version is: After joining the KMT, Cheng did indeed belong to a more radical faction within the Blue camp, often viewed as "disobedient" by the old guard. Her path was not a mere replication of the standard KMT line, but an independent sub-line. To say she is "just any substitute for the KMT" is inaccurate—she does indeed possess her own political judgment.

The truth likely lies in between: An academically trained, debating individual seeking influence who could not find her place within the Green camp in the 2000s political landscape, thus moved toward the Blue camp—and the Blue camp happened to need someone "articulate, educated, and with a student movement background" to change its image at that exact moment. They met in mutual interest. This was not betrayal, nor a pure shift in conviction; it was a political actor choosing a path that was most advantageous to her while remaining self-consistent.

But the cost of this choice is: **She must continue moving toward Beijing so as not to let this choice lose its internal logic.** From "Taiwan independence is a scam" to "I am Chinese" to "Both sides of the Strait are Chinese"—she cannot stop in the middle.

## The White Terror Autumn Memorial and the Wu Shih Photo

If there is one event that concretizes the dynamics described above, it is the White Terror Autumn Memorial service in November 2025.

Less than three weeks after being elected KMT Chairperson, Cheng attended the "1950s White Terror Autumn Memorial Service" organized by the "Taiwan Region Political Victims Mutual Aid Association." Among the names on the memorial list was **Wu Shih**. [^14]

Who was Wu Shih? He was a former Deputy Chief of Staff at the Ministry of National Defense of the Republic of China, executed in 1950 at the Mabangding execution ground in Taipei. His crime: **Communist spy**. He was indeed a high-level intelligence agent for the CCP embedded in Taiwan, codenamed "Secret Envoy No. 1," who provided intelligence to the CCP around the time the National Revolutionary Army moved to Taiwan in 1949. History recognizes him as a communist spy—even the CCP later publicly acknowledged this and listed him as a "revolutionary martyr."

The Autumn Memorial service Cheng attended placed Wu Shih on the same memorial list as other "White Terror victims," and played the Chinese revolutionary song _An Xi Ge_ (Rest in Peace) at the scene. The Ministry of the Interior's subsequent statement was extremely harsh: it described the event as "whitewashing a criminal who betrayed the nation and sold out his comrades into a narrative of mutual slaughter and historical tragedy between the KMT and CCP," calling it "the most serious injury to national dignity." [^15]

Cheng's response had two parts. The first was a denial: she stated she did not know Wu Shih was on the memorial list beforehand, and that she attended a "White Terror memorial," not a "communist spy memorial." [^16] The second part was an extension: she mentioned her own youth hunger-striking for Huang Hua, suggesting that the tragedies between the two sides of the Strait should be viewed within the same historical narrative—a language of "grand historical reconciliation." [^17]

But this second part is precisely the core of the controversy. When a "national security narrative" places a **treasonous intelligence agent** and **ordinary citizens wrongfully imprisoned by the government** in the same memorial ceremony, history is rewritten. The suppression of people by an authoritarian state is transformed into a "civil war tragedy between Chinese people." This rewriting is no small matter—it shifts the position of "victims of the Republic of China" from being "Taiwanese" to being "Chinese."

The core of the Ministry of the Interior's criticism was not "you shouldn't memorialize the White Terror," but rather "you are reshaping the White Terror from a narrative of local victims into a narrative of Chinese civil war." This is a shift in identity.

This event and her statement at the Cheng-Xi meeting five months later—"Both sides of the Strait are Chinese"—are two appearances of the same narrative in different contexts.

## April 10, 2026: The End of the Trajectory, or the Midpoint?

On the afternoon of April 10, 2026, Cheng Li-wun entered the Fujian Hall of the Great Hall of the People in Beijing and met with Xi Jinping for approximately ten minutes. [^18] She proposed five points, the most notable being "promoting the institutionalization of the peaceful development of cross-Strait relations to gradually achieve a 'peace framework'." Xi Jinping's response was: "Both sides of the Strait are Chinese, one family." [^19]

She called this meeting a "peace journey." According to analysis by NPR, her strategy was to utilize the uncertainty within Taiwanese society regarding the Trump administration to craft a "risk-avoidance, middle-ground" cross-Strait positioning for the KMT. [^20] Song Wen-ti, a researcher at the Atlantic Council, noted that Cheng's "institutional arrangements to prevent war" actually implied policy implications of "slowing down defense construction and reducing U.S. arms procurement."

After the meeting concluded, the Ministry of the Interior summarized her performance in one sentence: "We repeatedly requested Chairperson Cheng to present the three major demands of the Taiwanese people to Xi Jinping—recognizing the existence of the Republic of China, respecting the will of the Taiwanese people, and stopping military aircraft and vessel harassment—but she did not mention a single one." Deputy Minister Liang Wen-chieh's tone was weary. [^21]

There is an absurd symmetry to this moment.

In 1988 at the gates of NTU, Cheng Li-wun went on a hunger strike to force the government to release a Taiwan independence activist indicted for sedition. At that time, she believed "the government" was an authoritarian system. In 2026 in Beijing's Fujian Hall, she sat across from Xi Jinping and did not speak for a single Taiwanese person detained by Beijing—not for Taiwanese political prisoners in mainland China, not for Taiwanese writers abducted to China for trial, nor for those Taiwanese scholars harassed abroad. She was no longer protesting; she was engaging in dialogue.

Only this time, the person across the table was another kind of authority—one with even greater power and less regard for procedural justice.

## Conclusion: Both Cheng Li-wuns are Real

Some say that the Cheng Li-wun who went on a hunger strike in 1988 is dead. The person nodding to Xi Jinping in Beijing is a completely different person wearing the same name.

Others say these two Cheng Li-wuns are actually the same person—she has always been doing what she believes most effectively shakes "existing structures." In 1988, challenging the authoritarianism of the KMT; in 2026, challenging the Taiwan independence discourse of the DPP. The tools have changed, but the posture remains the same.

Both arguments make sense. But perhaps there is a third: both the 1988 Cheng Li-wun and the 2026 Cheng Li-wun are real, and they are both products of the same Taiwanese society at different stages. An island just emerging from martial law produced a youth who hunger-struck for Taiwan independence; an island seeking a foothold under the shadow of China's rise produced a middle-aged politician discussing a "peace framework" in Beijing. It is not that Cheng Li-wun changed, but that the shape of the "Taiwan issue" has changed.

But this argument also has its cruel side: if Cheng Li-wun is a product of the "changing shape of the Taiwan issue," then her trajectory being successful means the shape of the Taiwan issue has been altered in the very direction she originally hunger-struck to resist.

So the real question is not "Has Cheng Li-wun changed?", but rather: **Has Taiwan changed?**
