# Chih-pei-lin: The Civil Servant Who Photographed National Highways and Mortgaged His House to Film _Seeing Taiwan_

> **30-Second Overview:** Chih-pei-lin (1964–2017) was originally a civil servant at the Bureau of National Highway Construction under the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, using helicopters rented by the bureau to take aerial photographs of highway construction projects. He later became a full-time aerial photographer and the director of _Seeing Taiwan_. Released in 2013, the film's box office surpassed NT$200 million, remaining the highest-grossing Taiwanese documentary as of 2025, and won the [Golden Horse Award](/en/art/golden-horse-awards) for Best Documentary. On June 10, 2017, while scouting locations for a sequel, he died in a crash in Hualien alongside pilot Chang Chih-kuang and assistant Chen Kuan-chi.

Ou Chin-te, then Director of the Bureau of National Highway Construction under the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, once asked a young staff member in charge of procurement, "Are you interested in civil engineering?" The young man answered honestly, "I'm not quite sure." Ou replied, "Focus on doing what you love." [^1]

That young staff member, named Chih-pei-lin, loved photography. He eventually boarded helicopters rented by the Bureau to take aerial photos of highway construction projects from high above, his lens pointed at the expressways being built by the government. From that same altitude, he later saw something else entirely—something that led him to resign from his job, even though he was only three years away from retirement.

## Taking Construction Photos of National Highways from a Helicopter Rented by the Bureau of National Highway Design

Born in 1964, Chih-pei-lin loved running around with a camera since his student days. His first job after being discharged from military service was as a wedding photography assistant, but he quit in less than a month, finding it too boring and fake to have to guide people into posing for expressions. Because photography work sometimes involved irregular hours and inconsistent pay, his parents were quite worried. Following his parents' expectations, he passed the civil service exam and joined the Bureau of National Highway Design, where he was assigned to procurement—a role that had nothing to do with photography[^1].

At that time, [Northern Second Highway](/en/lifestyle/national-highway-system) was about to be constructed, and the Bureau of National Highway Design already had the idea of integrating public works with natural landscapes, which required a large amount of promotional material and construction records. Ou Chin-te read many new concepts from abroad and frequently tasked Chih-pei-lin with capturing them in photographs. Once, he asked Chih-pei-lin to find a high vantage point to photograph Pinglin and the Beishi River. His reasoning was that while Swiss castles face lakes, the houses in Pinglin face the roads for business, leaving the streams behind the houses, visible only when one goes to the restroom[^1].

After working at the Bureau of National Highway Design for over a year, Chih-pei-lin suggested to Ou Chin-te that if construction photos were taken from an aerial bird's-eye view, the images would appear more macro in scale[^2]. Before that, he had already been up in the sky: after seeing aerial real estate advertisements by photography senior Huang Chin-fa in _Ta-cheng Pao_, he called to inquire and went on a flight with his senior. "The first time I went up for aerial photography with my senior, I decided that this would be my lifelong vocation!"[^2] Having seen his performance in photography competitions, Ou Chin-te believed in his talent; from then on, the Bureau of National Highway Design began renting helicopters, with Chih-pei-lin responsible for photographing highway construction projects. Whenever an airline had an opportunity for aerial photography, he would join them as well[^2].

Looking back on those early years of aerial photography, he described himself to a reporter from _Vision Magazine_ this way: "I was born into a loyal and patriotic family; growing up, I always felt that all government projects were one hundred percent correct and that everything was beautiful"[^1]. This belief would later be shaken, bit by bit, in the mountains.

## After 921, He Said the Most Beautiful Places Are Where There Are No Roads

When he first started aerial photography, his goal was to capture a beautiful Taiwan so that more people could see it. He once believed that clearing forests on high mountains to carve out farmland—driven by the "spirit of the Foolish Old Man moving mountains"—was a story worth praising[^1]. Starting in 1998, he collaborated on a special series with _Vision Magazine_, photographing high-mountain farmland and the reclaimed lands of the coastal plains from the air, which received significant reader response[^2].

Before the [921 Earthquake](/en/history/921-jiji-earthquake), he had never filmed footage of natural disasters. After the earthquake, Taiwan's environment seemed to undergo a drastic transformation; once the New Central Cross-Island Highway was completed, human destruction accelerated, and debris flows occurred one after another. His perspective on these events began to shift[^1]. He described this transition through the metaphor of fatherhood: "It’s like learning how to be a father only after becoming one; it was only after flying to the edge of the sky that I truly came to know my own land"[^1].

In July 2009, he rented filming equipment from the United States and spent NT$3 million and 30 hours flying around Taiwan in the sweltering heat. The following month, [Typhoon Morakot](/en/history/typhoon-morakot) brought about the "88 Flood." The government invited him to film in the disaster areas. He later recalled his first impression: "The moment I saw it, I was utterly terrified. I truly felt that the debris flows I had seen before didn't even count as debris flows; this place looked like the end of the world!"[^2]

In 2012, _Seeing Taiwan_, a series of twenty-four aerial short films produced in collaboration with musician Chen Ming-chang, was broadcast on PTS. When a home version was released in August 2013, Chih-pei-lin noted during an interview with the Environmental Information Center that the most important thing a proactive government can do is to properly preserve existing natural resources. When asked where the most beautiful parts of Taiwan are, both he and Chen Ming-chang gave the same answer: places where there are no roads—places without roads or human activity, such as high mountains above 3,000 meters in altitude and Taiwan's last stretch of primitive coastline, Ānlángyī[^3]. At the time, the man who gave this answer worked for the Bureau of National Highway to take aerial construction photos of national highways.

## Three Years Before Retirement, He Discussed Mortgaging the House with His Mother

Around 2008, the idea of filming aerial movies occurred to him. Before that, he wrote articles, attended symposiums, and gave speeches across the country, wanting to tell people about what he saw from the sky; however, he often found that no matter how stunning the photos were, some people in the audience would still zone out or doze off[^4]. He later explained to reporters that telling stories through photos requires a lot of text and speech, and it takes immense effort to convey the message, whereas video can use simple methods to let more people get to know Taiwan[^3].

At the time, an aerial documentary about Taiwan sounded like a boring ecology film; a business model that could make audiences pay to go into theaters did not yet exist, and he said many people didn't understand what he was talking about. It wasn't until the release of _Home_, a collaboration between Yann Arthus-Bertrand and aerial photographers, that those around him began to understand what this was all about[^4], and he himself drew inspiration from the filming style of that movie[^2].

In the year of the Morakot typhoon, he made a decision: first, he applied for unpaid leave from the National Construction and Planning Agency, and finally resigned from his public office, giving up his pension[^2]. He was 47 years old when he resigned; in just three more years, he could have collected his pension[^5][^4]. He explained why he couldn't wait: "I thought back then that it would take about three more years to retire, but if I waited until retirement to do this, my physical strength and eyesight might not hold up. So, in the end, I decided to give up my public office and my pension first."[^2]

To achieve better image quality, he decided to purchase an aerial photography stabilization system from the United States costing nearly NT$30 million. He brought together Wanchuanli and several friends he had worked with to establish Taiwan Abu Film Company. Even though the company members all chipped in their own money, they still couldn't raise enough. He went to discuss it with his mother and decided to take out a mortgage on the house; his mother knew that he didn't care for food, drink, or play, but only loved photography[^2]. In the end, this film cost a total of approximately NT$90 million, with the helicopter flying for four hundred hours[^1][^6].

His son, then in his second year of high school, left a note on the table asking if his father would still have enough money for him to attend university. He feigned strength and told his son not to worry too much as long as he got into a national university, but the moment he saw the note, he felt both sadness and fear; at that time, the filming budget was not yet fully secured, and there was no telling if the project would succeed[^2]. He began calling companies one by one to seek corporate sponsorship: "Taiwan's richest, second richest, third richest... I went down the list and called every single company."[^1]

Some companies replied that they had two or three hundred people coming to ask for money every day; others asked him where his business model was or where his audience was, to which he could only give a bitter smile and say the audience is at home[^1]. Chang Yang-chien, Deputy Executive Director of the Delta Electronics Foundation, said that when they watched what Chih Tsai-lin filmed, they would even be moved to tears. Delta Electronics later became the largest sponsor in the first year of this project, and corporate foundations such as Wistron and Fubon gradually joined as well[^2][^1].

[Hou Hsiao-hsien](/en/people/hou-hsiao-hsien), who did not originally know him, agreed to let him use his name after watching only six minutes of the rough cut, later serving as an executive producer[^1][^6]. Wu Nien-jen broke his principle of only providing voiceovers for friends or his own works and did not take a single cent for the narration[^7], while the music was entrusted to Ho Kuo-chieh[^6]. Chih Tsai-lin had previously only taken still photographs and had never made documentaries or films; he sought advice from people all along, with Hou Hsiao-hsien, Tai Li-jen, and Yu Kan-ping all volunteering to help. He later said: "Now, when I am called a director, I actually feel quite embarrassed, because this movie was 'asked' into existence."[^1]

## NT$200 Million at the Box Office: Watching the Wounds of Our Own Land

On October 30, 2013, a large screen was erected at Liberty Square in front of the Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall in Taipei for the open-air premiere of _Seeing Taiwan_. The funding for this screening was raised by netizens on the crowdfunding platform flyingV; the campaign launched in late September and reached its goal before closing[^8][^4]. A week earlier, tickets for the premiere at the Kaohsiung Arena, attended by ten thousand people, had also completely sold out. That evening, Kaohsiung Mayor Chen Chu purchased the image rights from Chih-pei-lin to use the film as educational material for Kaohsiung's elementary and junior high schools in the future[^9].

![The open-air premiere at Taipei Liberty Square on October 30, 2013, with Chih-pei-lin standing in front of a stage backdrop printed with the film title and release date](/article-images/people/chi-po-lin-premiere-stage-2013.webp)

_October 30, 2013, Chih-pei-lin at the open-air premiere stage at Liberty Square; the backdrop reads "Released Nationwide on November 1." Photo: othree (Flickr), CC BY 2.0._

After its release on November 1, the nationwide box office exceeded NT$200 million, setting a record for the highest-grossing documentary in Taiwan's cinematic history. _Vision Magazine_ described how the film sparked a wave of environmental concern that transcended political divides between the Pan-Blue and Pan-Green camps[^10]. Lee Gen-cheng, Executive Director of the Earth Citizen Foundation, later remarked: "Not only did _Seeing Taiwan_ let everyone know about the beauty of Taiwan, but it also revealed environmental issues. Through the medium of an aerial documentary, it was the first time we saw Taiwan in this way."[^11] By 2025, when director Hsu Ming-chun discussed the Taiwanese documentary market, he noted that the box office record for documentaries is still held by _Seeing Taiwan_, and even feature films struggle to achieve such results[^12].

To capture that large footprint in the paddy field seen in the film, the crew waited six days during the _meiyu_ (plum rain) season for the skies to clear. While he grew restless waiting, the elderly farmers simply ate, chatted, and sang karaoke; they had long since learned to live in peace with the whims of heaven, a lesson that taught him much[^4]. That paddy field is located in Yuli, Hualien. In March 2014, following the film's release, he returned to Yuli to express his gratitude to the local people[^14].

Wu Nien-jen, who provided the narration for the film, spoke in the summer of 2013 about how the mountainsides were covered with houses and B&Bs, as well as betel nut trees—sights that are impossible to see without aerial photography. He said the biggest problem with Taiwanese people is "pretending not to see"; they prefer not to know about anything terrifying, believing they are living well while only blaming the government when things go wrong. He added that one shouldn't think staying in European-style mountain villas or eating cabbage is refined; both you and I are "accomplices" in harming Taiwan[^7].

In a 2019 review, Australian critic Grant Watson captured this exact tone: the film repeatedly presents a beautiful scene, followed by how Taiwanese people have destroyed it, with the narration constantly rebuking the audience. By the standards of documentary filmmaking, he found it repetitive and dull, but as a political statement, he found it very persuasive[^13].

## After Watching the Film, Jiang Yi-hua Lists Sixteen National Land Issues

On November 13, 2013, Premier Jiang Yi-hua went to the cinema to watch _Seeing Taiwan_, where he promised Chih-pei-lin on the spot that the government would work hard to resolve the issues of national land destruction and environmental pollution depicted in the film[^15][^16]. He assigned Deputy Secretary-General Chien Taro to convene the National Land Conservation Task Force. During a meeting on November 29, the task force listed sixteen national land issues identified in the film, including the destruction of slope stability by high-mountain roads and residential communities located adjacent to dangerous slopes[^15].

Jiang Yi-hua stated that the government must show strength. The approach set by the Executive Yuan is that ministries responsible for central government matters will handle them immediately, while for matters under local jurisdiction, counties and cities will be urged to ensure businesses make improvements within a specified timeframe according to law; if businesses fail to comply by the deadline, the central government can carry out compulsory enforcement or demolition on their behalf[^15]. Initially, the task force met every two weeks; by the end of January the following year, they had held four meetings and conducted five field verification trips[^15][^16].

On February 26, 2014, after hearing reports from the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, the Council of Agriculture, and the Council of Indigenous Peoples, Jiang Yi-hua ruled that, in principle, no new high-mountain roads should be opened, nor should existing ones be easily widened. In the same meeting, the Council of Indigenous Peoples reminded officials that existing mountain roads are inseparable from the daily lives of tribal residents and serve as essential routes for disaster prevention, evacuation, and relief. Because mountain tribes naturally have access to fewer public resources than those in the plains, existing provincial highways must maintain normal service to ensure the basic livelihoods of the people are protected[^17].

Chih-pei-lin had previously told reporters from _Vision Magazine_ that while traveling to Qingjing in Nantou feels like seeing beautiful scenery, from an aerial view, one sees surrounding illegal land reclamation and loose soil and rock; he was deeply concerned about the life safety of those living there[^1]. Qingjing was among the areas targeted by the task force's first wave of crackdown on over-utilized zones[^18]. In 2013, the Ministry of the Interior announced seven illegal B&Bs in Qingjing located in geologically sensitive areas[^19]. In January the following year, Jiang Yi-hua requested the Ministry of the Interior to coordinate with the Nantou County Government to draft a handling plan for implementation before the flood season arrived[^16]. On May 29, 2014, the county government sent excavators to demolish "Qingjing New House," which consisted entirely of illegal structures that could not be granted retroactive permits. It became the first—and at that time, the only—B&B to be forcibly demolished; the other six were exempt from demolition as long as they obtained retroactive permits[^19].

Pan Chung-cheng of the Taoyuan Local Alliance was invited to the premiere in October 2013 and was profoundly moved by the aerial view of the Guanyin藻礁 (algal reefs) on the screen. In 2017, he wrote on Facebook that after the film brought images of the sewage in Guanyin before the public eye, the water quality of the Taoyuan coast gradually came under control; the "black sea," "red sea," and "purple sea" of those years are no longer visible, and since the previous year, residents living by the sea have begun to see oysters and sea melons[^20].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> A common saying is that _Seeing Taiwan_ changed Taiwan's national land policy. The records left by the Executive Yuan itself are more concrete than this sentence: what the film first brought about was a list of sixteen items, followed by one meeting and field verification after another. For every item on that list to become a demolished house or a high-mountain road that is no longer opened, someone had to pursue them one by one. Those pursuers sit within ministries and county governments, stand within courts, and some even live right beneath the mines.

## The Day the Helicopter Did Not Return from Scouting for the Sequel

June 8, 2017, was World Oceans Day, and Chih-pei-lin chose this day to hold the opening press conference for 《Seeing Taiwan II》. For the sequel, he intended to take the lens beyond Taiwan, flying over other countries and diving beneath the sea to capture marine ecosystems. He hoped audiences would understand that environmental issues know no borders; those living under the same sky and within the same ocean cannot remain bystanders[^21].

Two days later, on June 10, he boarded a Ling Tien Air helicopter to scout locations for the sequel. This was the third aerial photography mission of the day. The helicopter took off from Chihshang Township, Taitung County, at approximately 10:45 AM, scheduled to fly along Yuli, Ruisui, Fengbin, and Chenggong[^22][^23]. At around 11:54 AM, the helicopter crashed near the Xiejin Farm by the Changhong Bridge in Fengbin Township, Hualien County. All three people on board perished: Chih-pei-lin, aged 52; his assistant Chen Kuan-chi, aged 25; and the pilot Chang Chih-kuang, aged 53[^23][^24]. Chang Chih-kuang was originally a military helicopter pilot who joined Ling Tien Air in 2006, with total flight time exceeding 5,500 hours[^23].

On June 12, the Ministry of National Defense dispatched two C-130 transport aircraft to send the three individuals from Hualien back to Taipei. On June 24, President Tsai Ing-wen presented a posthumous commendation at the "Seeing Chih-pei-lin" memorial exhibition held at the Songshan Cultural and Creative Park; the honor was accepted by Chih's father, Chih Wei-hsin, on his behalf[^25][^26]. During her speech, President Tsai mentioned that during his lifetime, Chih-pei-lin often expressed gratitude to Chang Chih-kuang for using his best flying skills to accompany him on adventures: "We will always remember such a brave pilot."[^26]

President Tsai also read a passage written by one of Chen Kuan-chi's university classmates, who serves as the Facebook administrator for the East Rift Valley National Scenic Area Administration. After Chen's passing, the classmate wrote: "I hope the name Chen Kuan-chi is remembered not just as Director Chih-pei-lin's assistant, but as a name belonging to a young man who worked hard for this land and loved it dearly."[^26]

The Aviation Safety Investigation Commission (ASIC) conducted an investigation for one year and four months, releasing the final report on October 31, 2018[^27][^28]. This helicopter was not equipped with a flight data recorder, and most of the fuselage was destroyed by fire after impact. Investigators had no flight data to read and were unable to examine the wreckage or test the engines[^22]. What they could rely on was the aerial photography camera on board: the day after the accident, the investigation team worked with personnel from Abut Film to download the memory card, obtaining 72 video clips, which were then used to reconstruct the helicopter's flight path through the imagery[^23].

The report listed several conditions related to potential causes: the pilot may have been affected by antihistamines, had a high flight time due to resting only the minimum hours required by regulations, endured long periods of helicopter vibration, and flew in low-altitude mountainous environments under summer heat. These conditions may have caused him to be "in a state of fatigue or reduced physiological and psychological performance" at the time of the accident[^22].

The concentration of antihistamines detected in his blood was 24 ng/mL; these drugs are commonly used for allergic symptoms such as runny noses or itchy skin. The report stated that "this residual amount should be attributed to normal medication accumulation," and also acknowledged a lack of direct evidence to assess his operational and judgmental capacity at the moment of the accident, making it impossible to determine how much these conditions influenced his safe execution of the mission[^22][^29]. A commentary published by _The New News_ questioned including medication and fatigue as potential causes in the absence of direct evidence, noting that this did not align with the ASIC's past reporting styles[^30].

The report also noted systemic gaps: the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) had not provided guidelines for pilots and medical examiners regarding medication use, and the clock-in/out times on Ling Tien Air's field operation logs did not match the actual duty hours of the flight crew[^22]. Furthermore, according to regulations, this helicopter was not required to be equipped with a flight data recorder[^30]. In accordance with the Aviation Accident Investigation Act, this investigation "is not intended for punishment or the pursuit of liability"[^23]. The report concluded with 18 recommendations for improvement—9 for Ling Tien Air and 9 for the CAA—one of which requested that the CAA actively evaluate the feasibility of installing simplified flight data recorders for general aviation[^22][^28].

## Asia Cement's Gray-White Pit and the Four Tribal Members Who Sued

In the Taroko people's "Return Our Land" movement against Asia Cement (Asia Cement), Tien Chun-chou had already persevered for twenty years by 2017. She collected data along the way, uncovering forged land abandonment documents from the local district office and winning lawsuits to reclaim land titles for portions of the territory. When Tsai Ing-wen apologized to Indigenous peoples on behalf of the government in 2016, she also received Tien's petition[^32].

On March 14, 2017, the Ministry of Economic Affairs approved an extension of Asia Cement's Xincheng Mountain mining rights, allowing it to mine for another twenty years until 2037[^33][^34]. Once the news spread, a civil petition was launched to revoke the extension, and Chih-pei-lin also signed it. He had long been concerned about mining issues in eastern Taiwan and had previously provided aerial photographs to environmental groups free of charge[^11][^32].

In May of that same year, while scouting locations near [Qingshui Cliffs](/en/geography/qingshui-cliff) to film large whales and dolphins at sea, he captured a photo on his return trip from the "severed head" mountains of Taroko: where there should have been a vast expanse of green forest, a massive gray-white pit had been excavated. When he uploaded the photo, he lamented that Asia Cement "has dug even deeper than when I was filming _Seeing Taiwan_ five years ago"[^31].

![The Xincheng Mountain Asia Cement mine; a gray-white terraced mining area carved into the middle of a green hillside](/article-images/people/asia-cement-xincheng-quarry-2016.webp)

_The Asia Cement mine in Xincheng Mountain, Hualien, in November 2016, showing a gray-white terraced mining area carved into the middle of a green hillside. Photo: Simballin, CC BY-SA 4.0._

After his passing, the petition to revoke the extension surged to 210,000 signatures. On the afternoon of June 25, the "See Asia Cement, Rescue Taroko" march set off from the Executive Yuan. Led by 160 [Taroko people](/en/culture/truku-people) from Fushi Village in Xincheng, Hualien, over 8,000 people marched to Ketagalan Boulevard, where they collectively formed the shape of Taiwan and had it photographed from above. The organizer, the Citizen Connect foundation, stated this was to commemorate Chih-pei-lin. Tien Chun-chou—who is 75 years old, has survived two strokes, and uses a wheelchair—took the stage and said: "As long as I have one breath left, I will fight Asia Cement to the end!"[^32]

The four members of the Boshian tribe who filed the lawsuit all live within a straight-line distance of 500 meters from the mining area. Hsu Chin is a landowner within the mining area, Tien Ming-cheng is a cultivator of land within the area, and Cheng Wen-chuan and Pai Mei-hua are also among the plaintiffs[^33][^35]. In October 2017, they filed an administrative lawsuit with the Taipei High Administrative Court to revoke the extension order; thus, the controversy over the [Asia Cement Xincheng Mountain mine](/en/history/asia-cement-xincheng-mine) entered the courts[^34][^33]. On July 11, 2019, the first instance ruling revoked the extension. On September 16, 2021, the Supreme Administrative Court dismissed Asia Cement's appeal, making the ruling final. The reason was that before the Ministry of Economic Affairs approved the extension, it had failed to implement the consultation and consent procedures required by Article 21 of the _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_—meaning it had not consulted with the tribe or obtained their consent beforehand[^33][^34].

During the years the lawsuit was fought, the Executive Yuan's list of sixteen items had long since been returned to various ministries: the National Land Conservation Task Force ceased operations following the change in administration in 2016, and its originally assigned duties were taken over by individual ministries[^18]. In April 2018, Nantou County Magistrate Lin Ming-chen confirmed that a conditional lifting of the nearly six-year construction ban in Qingjing would occur, stating, "If even these simple agricultural facilities cannot be built, it causes unbearable hardship for our farmers." Lee Ken-cheng of the Citizen Connect foundation responded that case handling eventually turns into "a contest of who has the bigger fist or more influence?"[^37]. By March 2021, only one major illegal B&B in Qingjing under the supervision of the Construction and Planning Agency remained to be demolished; the county government stated a schedule would be arranged later[^38].

After the ruling became final, Lo Ti-yu-tao, chairman of the Boshian tribal council, came forward to explain that while he respected the members' right to express individual opinions, he did not wish to see people using the tribe's name to speak on its behalf. The tribe had not yet reached a consensus regarding Asia Cement, and an explanatory meeting would be held to consolidate the views of the tribal members. A report by the Central News Agency also noted that Asia Cement's operations in the area affect local employment and residential safety[^35].

According to Article 13 of the _Mining Act_, after a ruling, mining rights return to the status of "applying for an extension," during which time the operator still holds mining rights. The Ministry of Economic Affairs stated that mining operations could proceed as usual without stopping work, but it would require Asia Cement to complete the tribal consultation and consent process before re-evaluating the application[^36][^35]. Plaintiffs Cheng Wen-chuan and Pai Mei-hua said that for the more than 200 residents in the sixty-plus households living below the mining area, residential and land safety still has no foreseeable future. They described their situation after the lawsuit as follows: if the _Mining Act_ is not amended, there will never be a day of justice[^35].

## From Helicopters Rented from the Bureau of National Highway to a Satellite 561 Kilometers Away

At 2:44 AM Taiwan time on November 29, 2025, a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California, USA, delivering the first satellite of the [National Space Center](/en/technology/tasa-taiwan-space-agency) FORMOSAT-8 into a sun-synchronous orbit 561 kilometers above the Earth[^39][^40]. The satellite was named "Zeppelin." President Lai Ching-te stated that the name was chosen in the hope that "the spirit of Director Chih-pei-lin can extend into space, continuing to watch over Taiwan and observe the world"[^41].

More than thirty years ago, the Bureau of National Highway began renting helicopters for a young staff member who was originally in charge of procurement, allowing him to take aerial construction photos of national highways; the Director wanted him to focus on what he loved. What he loved eventually took him flying across the skies of Taiwan, resulting in a film with a nationwide box office exceeding NT$200 million[^1][^2][^10]. Along the way, there were helicopter pilots, assistants who flew with him, and Wu Nien-jen, who recorded the narration without charge[^7]. On the day the satellite launched, Chih's son, Chi Ting-huan—who once left a note on his father's desk asking about tuition fees—said in a video that his father always hoped to let everyone see what he saw, using the power of "seeing" to create and change[^39].

Chih-pei-lin often said during his lifetime that aerial photography cannot be accomplished by one person alone[^26]. The journey from seeing to changing is the same. He contributed his own effort by signing petitions for the revocation of mining extensions and providing aerial photographs to environmental groups[^11]. By 2017, Tien Chun-chou had already been fighting Asia Cement for twenty years to "return my land," and four tribal members living near the mining area took the extension dispute all the way to the Supreme Administrative Court[^32][^33]. The power of "seeing" that Chi Ting-huan spoke of must be caught by a group of people like this to truly become change.
