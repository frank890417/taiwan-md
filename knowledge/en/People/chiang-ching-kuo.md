> **30-second overview:** Chiang Ching-kuo became president in 1978 and announced the lifting of Taiwan's 38-year martial law on July 15, 1987 — two acts that made him simultaneously the helmsman of the authoritarian system and the decision-maker of political opening. This article does not write democratization as one person's gift, but places his reforms, state violence, and the society that kept acting after the Kaohsiung Incident on the same causal chain.

On May 20, 1978, Chiang Ching-kuo was sworn in as President of the Republic of China in Taipei; on July 15, 1987, Taiwan lifted 38 years of martial law; on January 13, 1988, he died in office. [^1] These three dates lined up look like a neat progressive timeline. But Chiang Ching-kuo was not someone standing outside the martial law system opening the door for Taiwan — he himself was one of the door's guards.

The historical puzzle he left is therefore not a popularity contest of "was Chiang Ching-kuo good or bad," but: why did a man who once wielded the authoritarian state begin, in his final years, to dismantle several pillars of his own rule? Even thornier, this dismantling did not start from a blank slate. He pushed open the crack, but the ones who shoved their bodies into the doorway so it could never close again were the wave after wave of people who risked speaking out during the martial law years.

> The lifting of martial law is worth remembering not because a strongman suddenly became kind, but because even a strongman had to face a society that had already grown up.

## A Man Who Took Over in Crisis

Chiang Ching-kuo assumed the Kuomintang (KMT) chairmanship and the premiership of the Executive Yuan in 1975, and became president in 1978. Research by Wu Nai-teh of Academia Sinica situates 1980s democratization in the context of the Kaohsiung Incident and the participants of mass and opposition movements, reminding readers not to over-attribute democratization to Chiang Ching-kuo alone. [^6]

This background matters because it changes the weight of the word "reform." For a ruler still intent on preserving his regime, localization, elections, social construction, and political opening did not necessarily equal relinquishing power; they could also be methods to keep the regime alive. _Retrospect Journal_'s analysis of this turning point notes that by the late 1970s Taiwan had lost U.S. diplomatic recognition and its international position had deteriorated; Chiang Ching-kuo faced not merely domestic governance but the question of whether the regime could continue to exist amid Cold War upheaval. [^8]

The Presidential Office's official biography uses more positive language to summarize his tenure: he promoted local construction, cultivated Taiwan's elite, narrowed urban-rural gaps, and pushed for parliamentary reform and democratization politically; the 1987 lifting of martial law was followed by permission to establish new newspapers and new parties, and in November 1987 he also allowed Taiwan residents to visit China for family reunions. [^1] This official narrative provides the skeleton of reform, but a skeleton is not the whole body.

Because before that, Taiwan's political space was still constituted by martial law, the party ban, the press ban, assembly restrictions, and the intelligence apparatus. These systems did not fall from the sky one day, nor did they all vanish because of a single martial law lifting order.

## Before the Lifting, Society Was Already Speaking

The Kaohsiung Incident (Formosa Incident) of December 10, 1979, is a scene Chiang Ching-kuo's late-year reforms cannot bypass. A 2007 Presidential Office retrospective treats the 1979 Kaohsiung Incident and the 1986 founding of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) as key factors leading to the lifting of martial law; the same document records that many political demands once treated as crimes later became part of Taiwan's democratic system. [^5]

This contrast is more worth remembering than "Taiwan's march toward democracy": a state that once treated the lifting of martial law, the lifting of the party ban, the lifting of the press ban, full re-election, and direct presidential election as evidence of sedition later had to write several of those demands into its own reform schedule. [^5]

On September 28, 1986, the DPP was founded. Martial law was still in effect; organizing a new party was a direct collision with the system's boundaries. Ten days later, Chiang Ching-kuo received _Washington Post_ publisher Katharine Graham and publicly stated for the first time that Taiwan would lift martial law. This temporal relationship is recorded in both the Presidential Office's later retrospective and the Chiang Ching-kuo Centennial Memorial Website, but their narrative stances differ: the former emphasizes pressure from democratic movements, the latter emphasizes Chiang Ching-kuo's role persuading comrades within the party. [^5] [^3]

This is not about choosing one "sole truth" between two versions. Together they point to one thing: the lifting had decision-makers, but no single decision-maker could manufacture that era alone.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Writing democracy as a leader's gift erases two kinds of people at once: those crushed by the state, and those who acted despite the risk of being crushed by the state.

## "The Times Are Changing" — That Line

The Chiang Ching-kuo Centennial Memorial Website preserves a frequently quoted remark. The website records that in 1986, to persuade party comrades to lift martial law, he said: "The times are changing, the environment is changing, the trend is also changing." [^3]

This line is short enough to print on a souvenir, yet cannot stand alone as proof that Chiang Ching-kuo had become a democrat. It is more like a key that unlocks the turn in his late political language; but the key opens a room that remains locked.

Hsueh Hua-yuan of the National Policy Foundation reminds us that lifting martial law was a key step in liberalization, but cannot be directly equated with democratization. Because democracy is not just canceling emergency orders; it also includes regular elections deciding who governs, guarantees of basic human rights, and the ability of the people to continuously change the government. [^2]

The Lowy Institute's retrospective also notes that after the 1987 lifting, the government quickly enacted the National Security Law, preserving many powers originally held by the martial law system; not until the 1992 constitutional reforms and the 1996 first multi-party direct presidential election did Taiwan pass several more critical institutional thresholds. [^7]

So "the times are changing" was not a happy ending, but the ruler acknowledging that old methods could no longer continue unchanged. It brought new possibilities, and also left new constraints.

## The Reformer Cannot Wash Away His Responsibility

Chiang Ching-kuo's historical evaluation most easily slides toward two extremes: one side writes him as the enlightened leader who single-handedly completed democratization; the other, because he was part of the authoritarian system, refuses to acknowledge that his decisions indeed changed the system's direction. Both writings are too easy.

Hsueh Hua-yuan's analysis directly states that during Taiwan's White Terror period, Chiang Ching-kuo bore considerable decision-making or administrative responsibility for the KMT authorities' human rights violations; simultaneously, his lifting of martial law indeed held critical significance, but lifting martial law and democratization cannot be conflated. [^2]

The Henry Liu (Liu Yi-liang) case makes this responsibility painfully concrete. The National Human Rights Archives records that in 1984, writer Liu Yi-liang was murdered in the United States, a case implicating Bamboo Union members absorbed by the Ministry of National Defense's Intelligence Bureau; in November 1984 Chiang Ching-kuo learned of the Intelligence Bureau's involvement, in January 1985 ordered investigation of relevant officials, and later under U.S. pressure agreed to allow U.S. personnel to investigate in Taiwan. [^4]

This history cannot be simplified into "Chiang Ching-kuo ordered an investigation, so he had nothing to do with state violence." On the contrary, it shows a president having to handle consequences between the security apparatus he led and international pressure. Chiang Ching-kuo's later political opening does not delete the intelligence agencies' violence from his governing responsibility; reform cannot retroactively demand that victims of the authoritarian era forgive him.

This is why "authoritarian reformer" is a more accurate — and more uncomfortable — label than "democratic hero." His actions had historical effects, but historical effects do not equal moral immunity.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> A regime starting to loosen its grip does not mean it never seized anyone; a person pushing open a door does not mean he did not help build the wall.

## From Lifting to Democracy, a Long Road Remains

On July 15, 1987, the martial law lifting order took effect. The week before, on July 7, the Legislative Yuan passed a resolution lifting martial law in the Taiwan Area; on July 14, Chiang Ching-kuo issued a presidential order designating midnight the next day as the effective time. This sequence matters: the lifting was not a dramatic moment completed the instant the president announced it alone on camera, but an institutional transition overlapping administrative orders, legislative procedure, and existing political pressure. [^9]

The dates are clear, the meaning is not linear. For many Taiwanese, it meant they could reorganize, publish, assemble, and speak of things previously forbidden; for the state apparatus, it meant replacing some emergency governance tools with new legal language. The Central News Agency's historical compilation lists the post-lifting opening of press and party bans, full legislative re-election, and constitutional amendments as follow-up reforms, but these reforms were not all completed on July 15 — they unfolded in stages. [^9]

The Lowy Institute describes the lifting as a starting point, not the endpoint of democratization. It notes that after the lifting, Taiwanese began dealing with truths forcibly silenced — the February 28 Incident, political victims, memory sites like Green Island and Jingmei — all places where public discussion later regrew. [^7]

This "speaking again" process was neither clean nor always gentle. It contains victims' memories, families' cross-generational silence, parties contesting historical interpretation, and society's ongoing debate over "who is qualified to represent Taiwan." But democracy was never about silencing debate; it is about letting debate proceed without the price of going underground, informing, or military tribunals.

Therefore, the lifting must be read in at least three layers. The first layer: when the order took effect. The second: when the press ban, party ban, legislative re-election, and constitutional reform gradually loosened. The third: whether victims could be seen, whether state violence could be questioned, and whether the people could truly change the government. Remembering only the first layer miswrites "lifting martial law" as "democracy completed." [^7] [^9]

Chiang Ching-kuo died in January 1988, never seeing the 1996 direct presidential election, never seeing party rotation. Pouring all later democratic achievements back into his late years is a common laziness of historical narrative. Erasing all his decisions between 1986 and 1987 is equally lazy. A more honest statement: he initiated a process whose endpoint he could not control, and Taiwan society continued pushing that process further after his death.

## Who Pushed Reform to the Next Station

Chiang Ching-kuo's reforms also have a scale issue easily overlooked: they changed the political entry point, but did not solve all political problems at once. New parties could form, but that did not mean power was equal. The press ban loosened, but that did not mean everyone could safely speak their history. The lifting took effect, but that did not mean victims immediately received full judicial and public acknowledgment. This subsequent work is precisely why the 1992 constitutional reforms and 1996 direct presidential election cannot be skipped. [^7]

Looking back from here at "Chiang Ching-kuo promoted democratization," it is best to break the verb apart: he approved, acquiesced, or led certain institutional turns. Social movements forced the state to acknowledge those turns had become inevitable. Later elections, constitutional amendments, transitional justice, and memory work turned those turns into relatively stable institutions. Different roles do not cancel each other out; they jointly constitute the causal chain of Taiwan's democratization.

This breaking apart is not to deliberately write the figure small, but to avoid writing history as a single biography. If Chiang Ching-kuo remains only an "enlightened" label, we cannot see the security state he inhabited. If democratic movements remain only hero stories, we also cannot see when and why loosening appeared inside the system.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Democratization is not moving credit from one person's hands to another's, but returning the question of "who gets to decide" to more people. Dates can confirm when orders took effect, but cannot alone answer who paid the price for freedom.

Official biographies often line up reforms as a string of policies: localization, local construction, lifting martial law, opening the press ban, opening the party ban. This arrangement helps confirm timing and institutions, but hides "who demanded first, who bore the cost, who remained excluded after reform" outside the sentence. [^1]

Conversely, human rights archives break state violence into individual names, agencies, and actions: who was surveilled, who was absorbed, who ordered investigations, who changed handling under international pressure. It does not automatically give complete answers, but forces the reader to put reform and responsibility on the same table. [^4]

These two materials cannot replace each other. The official page explains how decision-makers described themselves; the human rights archives let the governed re-enter the narrative. When overlapped, Chiang Ching-kuo is not written as a figure with only one voice.

## Not the Endpoint

The most worth writing about Chiang Ching-kuo is not whether he can be ranked on a "greatest contributors to Taiwan" list, but how his contradiction lets us see how a system turns.

An authoritarian government may choose opening because of international isolation, social movements, economic change, governing crisis, and leader judgment. But once opening occurs, it no longer belongs only to the leader. Journalists, tangwai (non-KMT opposition) figures, victims' families, lawyers, workers, farmers, women's and Indigenous movement actors — all will widen the crack into public space.

The Presidential Office's official narrative places Chiang Ching-kuo at the center of reform. Academic and human rights materials remind us that beside the center there have always been pressed bodies and voices. [^1] [^2] [^4] Reading these sources together yields not a prettier Chiang Ching-kuo, but an answer closer to Taiwan's experience: democracy is not a gift, nor a naturally ripened fruit, but a space that, once cracked open, many people together guarded, expanded, and redefined.

He pushed open the door, but was not democracy's master. The people outside the door made Taiwan into a place that could later change its masters.

This is the most difficult, and most useful, legacy Chiang Ching-kuo left Taiwan: a process, not a monument.
