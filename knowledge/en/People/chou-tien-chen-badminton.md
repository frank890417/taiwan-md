> **30-Second Overview:** Chou Tien-chen (周天成), born in Taipei in 1990, is Taiwan's leading men's singles badminton player. In 2014, he won the French Open, becoming the first Taiwanese player to claim a BWF Super Series men's singles title.[^2] In August 2019, his world ranking rose to second, the highest in the history of Taiwanese men's singles.[^1] He was the first Taiwanese player to defeat "world king" Lin Dan on the international stage.[^13] He has represented Chinese Taipei at three consecutive Olympic Games, reaching the quarterfinals all three times.[^2] In early 2023, a routine colonoscopy detected early-stage colon cancer; he underwent surgery and flew abroad for competition just days later, enduring a nearly year-long slump,[^3] only to reach the quarterfinals at the Paris Olympics at age 34 the following year.[^10] At 36, he remains a frequent fixture among the world's top-tier veterans.[^12]

![A shot of Chou Tien-chen hitting a save during the 2018 BWF Taipei Open, a typical moment of his grinding rally style](/article-images/people/chou-tien-chen-taipei-open-2018.webp)

## An Overly Flashy Child

Chou Tien-chen's badminton journey began with a simple wish from his parents: to provide an outlet for their hyperactive son to run, jump, and expend energy.[^1] Once his interest emerged, he transferred to Shuanglian Elementary School in the third grade, which had a professional badminton team.[^1]

His first coach, Chen Wen-ping (陳文平), did not have glowing things to say about the boy—noting his technique was too flashy and his lower body strength was insufficient.[^1] More troublesome was his temperament; he was competitive, self-important, often clashed with teammates, and was difficult to teach.[^1] At that time, no one could see a future world number two in this restless child who loved to show off.

The sixth grade was the turning point. That year, he won his first national championship and met the person who would rewrite his life: Cheng Yung-cheng (鄭永成), an Indonesian-born coach who had once been ranked fourth in the world.[^5] From Cheng-Yuan Junior High to Keelung Senior High, Cheng guided him through the years, slowly grinding that fiery temper into patience.[^5] Coincidentally, Cheng was also one of the foundational coaches for Taiwan's badminton queen, Tai Tzu-ying (戴資穎); the roots of Taiwan's "top ace and top lady" both connect to this same teacher from Indonesia.[^1] Chou Tien-chen later said he is most grateful to his coach for grinding away his childhood impulsiveness.[^1]

## Ten Consecutive Losses to World No. 2

In 2009, at age 19, Chou Tien-chen joined the CTBC Bank team, officially entering the national team system and beginning his professional career.[^1] However, the start was not sweet; Taiwanese men's singles had long been supporting players on the world stage. With no one to pave the way before him, he had to strike out on his own, match by match.

His most difficult period occurred in the first half of 2014. Facing top-tier opponents, he suffered ten consecutive losses, unable to break through the wall regardless of how he played, until he finally halted the losing streak at the Canada Grand Prix in July.[^6] Reflecting on it later, he noted that not only was his technique immature, but he also lacked tactical thinking on court, often simply hitting the shuttle back without purpose.[^6] He set a goal for himself: with every loss, he must take away a little bit of progress.[^6]

His turnaround from rock bottom came faster than anyone expected. In October of that same year, at the 2014 French Open, he became the first Taiwanese player to win a BWF Super Series men's singles title.[^2] In 2016, he ended a 17-year wait by winning the Taipei Open, the first time in many years a local player had claimed the title.[^2] At the 2018 Jakarta Asian Games, he took home the men's singles silver medal.[^2]

2019 was his peak. In the Indonesia Open final, he defeated Denmark's Anders Antonsen to claim his first Super 1000 title.[^1] In August of that year, his world ranking rose to second—a position no Taiwanese men's singles player had ever reached.[^1] The following year at the All England Open, with then-world king Kento Momota absent due to a car accident, Chou Tien-chen entered as the top seed. He reached the final but lost to Denmark's Viktor Axelsen, taking the runner-up spot.[^1] At the 2022 World Championships, he added a bronze medal to his collection.[^2] While playing for CTBC Bank, he also helped the Chinese Taipei team secure a bronze in the men's team event at the 2024 Thomas Cup.[^1]

## A Playing Style Called "Grinding"

Chou Tien-chen is not the type of player to kill an opponent with a single smash. His trademark is the "grinding" rally—patiently searching for gaps through multiple exchanges, utilizing delicate net play, and then using sudden attacking smashes to secure points.[^1] In his youth, he primarily relied on physical stamina to wear down opponents; later, he transitioned to a style based on tenacious defense with opportunistic attacks, hiding the timing of his strikes very well.[^16]

![Chou Tien-chen returning a shot at the 2022 Taipei Open, with low center of gravity and precise footwork](/article-images/people/chou-tien-chen-taipei-open-2022.webp)

This "grinding" style earned him a somewhat humorous nickname: "The Movie King" (影帝). On court, he often looks breathless and as if his stamina is failing, allowing opponents to take the first game, only to fight back in the deciding game. Because of this, Chinese fans called him the "Movie King." Chou Tien-chen clarified that it was actually his genuine physical exhaustion on court, not an intentional performance.[^14]

It was precisely through this resilience that in 2015, at the Taipei Open, he became the first Taiwanese player to defeat "world king" Lin Dan on the international stage.[^13] During his debut era, the men's singles field was lined with legends like Lin Dan, Lee Chong Wei, Chen Long, and Kento Momota. Chou Tien-chen would often battle opponents into a third game, only to lose by a hair's breadth.[^16] Lacking absolute, overwhelming talent, he used stability and an refusal to give up to grind his way into the world's top five.

## Appendicitis, Church, and a Slow-to-Mature Man

When he hit a low point in 2012, Chou Tien-chen was invited to a church by his physical therapist.[^15] The real turning point came the following year: before the 2013 India Open, he suffered severe abdominal pain but gritted his teeth to finish the tournament. It was only after the competition that he was diagnosed with appendicitis, which had already perforated several days prior.[^1] During his rehabilitation after that surgery, he was formally baptized as a Christian.[^1]

Faith became a part of him. He uses the gaps between international tours to visit local churches and has a habit of singing hymns before matches or during recovery periods.[^1] Chou Tien-chen says that faith accelerated his mental maturity, transforming him from a child who clashed with others easily into someone who can remain calm.[^15] While he may look strained on the court, he is relaxed off it, often chatting and joking with his team.[^1]

## Being His Own Coach

Starting in 2019, Chou Tien-chen made a decision quite rare among elite players: he stopped hiring a full-time coach. Instead, he became his own coach, personally arranging training and thinking through tactics independently on court.[^18] Even for major stages like the Olympics, he has occasionally competed without a coach, following an "unorthodox" preparation route.[^19] He summarized his source of strength in one sentence: "God is my coach."[^18]

This arrangement relies on extremely high self-discipline. During the COVID-19 pandemic, he lived long-term at the National Sports Training Center in Kaohsiung, preparing in a "bubble mode" isolated from the outside world; he noted that living at the training center actually allowed him to focus better.[^19] Commentators' evaluations of him almost always revolve around the same set of words: hardworking, self-disciplined, and humble. In a sport where talent is paramount, he turned "completing daily tasks" into his greatest weapon.

## A Routine Checkup Reveals Colon Cancer

In early 2023, while taking a break from competition to rest in Taiwan, Chou Tien-chen arranged a health checkup. The results of the colonoscopy caught him off guard: early-stage colon cancer.[^3]

He made the decisive move to undergo surgery for removal. To ensure no recurrence, the surgery was performed very deeply, and he even had to have a bioabsorbable clip left in his intestine to hold the wound.[^3] Fortunately, the cancer cells had not spread. He hardly allowed himself to stop; just days after surgery, he flew abroad for competition, and training continued as usual. However, his diet was heavily affected, and his physical condition struggled to recover.[^3] For nearly a year, he was eliminated in the first round several times, failing to reach even the quarterfinals for seven consecutive months. Outsiders began debating whether "he was getting old at 34," but only he knew that the problem lay with his major illness, not his age.[^3]

He chose not to speak of it publicly at first. It wasn't until he won the Germany Heilbronn Open in November 2023 that he shared this journey in his church for the first time.[^3] After winning the Thailand Masters in February 2024, he became champion again at the age of 34 and 27 days. According to reports at the time, this made him the fourth oldest men's singles champion in World Tour history, following Lee Hyun-il, Lee Chong Wei, and Lin Dan.[^11] Only after standing on the podium did he go public with his cancer diagnosis: "I made it through." He said this journey made him more certain of one thing: no matter what setbacks or accidents occur, as long as you hold on, you will always find an answer.[^3]

## Paris: The Quarterfinals Against the Wind

The Olympics have remained an unfinished chapter in Chou Tien-chen's career. In 2016, during his first attempt in Rio, he lost 0–2 to Malaysia's Lee Chong Wei in the quarterfinals;[^1] in 2021, he reached the quarterfinals again in Tokyo, facing China's Chen Long, where he won one game but lost the decider 14–21.[^1] In the summer of 2024, he stood on the Olympic stage for a third time.[^2] Having just recovered from cancer surgery, he let out a roar of defiance during the group stages to boost the Chinese Taipei team's morale, advancing to the round of 16 with an undefeated record.[^10] In his first knockout match, he faced the world number five and Japan's Kodai Naraoka. Playing steadily and accurately, he won in straight games, 21–12, 21–16, reaching the quarterfinals once again and matching his personal Olympic best.[^2] In the quarterfinals, he faced India's Lakshya Sen; after a 75-minute battle, he lost, and his dream of an Olympic medal slipped away once more.[^2]

To reach the quarterfinals in three consecutive Olympics, only four people in badminton history had achieved it before him.[^20] But the significance of this quarterfinal run was different from the previous two: this time, it was achieved by a man who, less than a year ago, was lying on an operating table having a tumor removed, fighting his way back to the world stage.[^4]

## The Record is Not Yet Written

Returning from cancer, Chou Tien-chen did not treat his comeback as a final curtain call. In 2024 and 2025, he completed back-to-back victories at the Arctic Open in Finland.[^8] In 2025, at age 35, he defeated the world king to reach the finals of the Indonesia Open, setting a record at the time as the oldest player to compete in a Super 1000 men's singles final at 35 years and 150 days.[^7] That same year, he set new age-related milestones in his world ranking, being described by the badminton community as a "role model for younger players."[^9]

![A recent image of Chou Tien-chen fighting hard during the 2024 Taipei Open; that year he also won the Arctic Open](/article-images/people/chou-tien-chen-taipei-open-2024.webp)

As of June 2026, 36-year-old Chou Tien-chen's world ranking remains high at number 6, making him a frequent fixture among the oldest players in the top tier of men's singles.[^12] In a sport where players are often considered past their prime by age 30, he has forcibly extended his professional lifespan by an entire cycle. While the new generation begins to view defeating him as a career milestone, he remains standing there, letting the young players chase him.

## Chou Tien-chen Off the Court

Chou Tien-chen integrated public welfare into his life planning early on. He has said that he wants to cultivate charity while he still has influence, as he is unsure if he will have the same energy after retirement.[^17] Over the years, he has contributed many times to children with rare diseases and rural education in Taiwan, all while maintaining a low profile: "Charity isn't done for show; whether there is media present or not, you just do what is right."[^17]

He does not run a fan page and rarely uses social media.[^1] For someone who has been world number two, reached the Olympic quarterfinals three times, defeated Lin Dan, and walked back onto the court from cancer, the lack of noisy packaging allows his achievements to speak more clearly. Chou Tien-chen has always walked a path of slow grinding, with no shortcuts of explosive talent: relying on patience, faith, and an unyielding spirit, he raises the height of Taiwanese badminton inch by inch.
