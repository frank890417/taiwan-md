# Dwagie: The Taiwanese Rap Schoolmaster Who Built a Training Room in Tainan

> **30-second overview:** Dwagie (大支), born Tseng Kuan-jung (曾冠榕), is a Taiwanese rapper from Tainan and the founder of Kung-Fu Entertainment (人人有功練音樂工作室). In the late 1990s, he met MC HotDog on the Master U message board, moving from underground demos and club performances into Magic Stone Records’ Great Circus music studio. His 2002 debut album _Lotus from the Tongue_ (舌粲蓮花, sometimes written 舌燦蓮花) is often treated as the first all-rap album in the Sinophone market, while “Taiwan Song” (台灣SONG) turned Taiwanese Hokkien accent into a sonic marker of Taiwan-centered identity. After 2003, he returned to Tainan and built Kung-Fu Entertainment, turning a recording and practice space into a southern hip-hop base. In 2011, _The People_ (人) brought together Deserts Chang, Freddy Lim, Wen Hsia, the 14th Dalai Lama, and others, pushing human rights, religion, animal rights, and political concern into the vocabulary of Taiwanese rap. By the time of _The Rappers_ (大嘻哈時代), he had become a judge and “schoolmaster,” handing the experience of Taiwan’s early underground scene to a new generation.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The Underground Years That Began on Master U

The first places where Taiwan’s hip-hop scene recognized Dwagie were message boards, record shops, and the subcultural corners of Tainan.

In a CNA interview, he recalled encountering West Coast U.S. hip-hop such as Snoop Dogg’s _Doggystyle_ in record stores. In Tainan, places like POP Artist Shop and East Assassin let him see how subculture could gather. Later, while studying in Taipei, he met MC HotDog on the Master U message board. Through meetups, basketball games, parties, and exchanging work, the two gradually became part of the core of Taiwan’s early underground rap scene.[^1][^2]

Taiwanese hip-hop did not yet have a mature industry. A Roomie excerpt from _Hip-Hop Kids: The Story of Taiwanese Rap_ notes that after 1998, Magic Stone Records noticed MC HotDog at a live performance at Taipei club @live, then set up the Great Circus music studio. Its members included MC HotDog, Dwagie, Xiao Si, 168, and Charles. Record companies were still figuring out how to produce, price, and promote rap; in those few years, underground demos, campus networks, live performances, and the mainstream recording industry briefly connected.[^2]

_MC HotDog performing in 2012. Dwagie and HotDog crossed paths in the Master U and Magic Stone era; their underground years form a key memory from before Taiwanese rap entered the mainstream market. Photo: Chiu Yu-feng, CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons._

In August 2002, Dwagie released his debut album _Lotus from the Tongue_. Roomie places it in Magic Stone’s final stage: the album was registered at the end of Magic Stone’s catalog list, and its promotional language called it “the first all-rap album in the Sinophone market.”[^2] The details of that claim vary across sources, but the historical position is clear: when Taiwan’s mainstream market still did not fully understand rap, Dwagie used a full album to put Taiwanese Hokkien, Mandarin, skits, rhyme experiments, local consciousness, and international hip-hop form together.

## The Sound of “Taiwan Song”

The most easily remembered work on _Lotus from the Tongue_ is “Taiwan Song.”

It is often simplified into a political statement, but the more important point is its method of sound. Dwagie uses Taiwanese Hokkien accent, Tainan inflection, and call-and-response shouting to turn “Taiwan” from an idea into a hearable tone. In the context of Taiwanese hip-hop and rap, “Taiwan Song” is both a song of local consciousness and a language experiment. Taiwanese Hokkien carries rap’s rhythm, aggression, and crowd-mobilizing force here; it is no longer just a borrowed emotional accent in Mandarin pop.[^2][^5]

In 2017, he was invited to perform “Taiwan Song” at the Brooklyn Nets’ “Taiwan Culture Night” home game, an event recorded by Taiwanese media and online sources as a representative moment for a Taiwanese rapper on an NBA arena stage.[^3] The scene captures Dwagie’s contradictory position: a song grown from Tainan inflection, Taiwanese Hokkien lyrics, and Taiwan consciousness became a sonic flag for an overseas Taiwanese cultural event in a U.S. professional basketball arena.

## Returning to Tainan to Open a Training Room

After his debut album, Dwagie did not simply follow the Taipei entertainment market. He returned to Tainan and built Kung-Fu Entertainment.

In the CNA interview, he said Kung-Fu Entertainment did not begin as a grand cultural plan. It was more like a place where people who liked hip-hop could record, practice, and learn rap. After 2003, this gathering and teaching space slowly grew into a southern hip-hop base. Summer rap classes, a recording room, events, and member networks made Tainan another production base for Taiwanese rap.[^1]

Kung-Fu Entertainment is distinctive because it combines education, performance, production, local gathering, and a relay point for newcomers. It is messier than a simple record label and more public than a personal studio. Taiwanese hip-hop later often called Dwagie “the schoolmaster,” a title that comes from seniority and generational status, but also from the way he tied rap teaching, recording-space work, and scene-building together over the long term.

Taiwanese rap is often imagined as a culture grown out of Taipei dorm networks, Riverside Live House, clubs, and campus societies. What Dwagie and Kung-Fu Entertainment add is the Tainan route: a group of people practicing in tea shops, recording rooms, classes, and events, carrying southern accents, Taiwanese Hokkien, and local experience into hip-hop.

## After The People, the Microphone Opened Toward Public Issues

The 2011 album _The People_ was an important turn in Dwagie’s creative path. CNA notes that the album included collaborations with Deserts Chang, Freddy Lim, Wen Hsia, the 14th Dalai Lama, and others. Its title track, “The People,” put religion, human rights, animals, and concern for life into the work.[^1][^3]

_The People_ was later nominated for Best Mandarin Album at the 23rd Golden Melody Awards and won Best Album and Best Hip-Hop Album at the 3rd Golden Indie Music Awards.[^3] Awards are not the most vivid part of Dwagie’s work, but they brought this rough-edged, highly political rap line, marked by Taiwanese Hokkien and Taiwan consciousness, into a position the mainstream music system could recognize.

PTS’s _Guess Who_ introduced Dwagie in the context of “speaking social truth with a microphone” and also recorded his long-term vegetarianism and concern for animals and vulnerable groups.[^6] In 2019, the Environmental Information Center reported that at a World Day for the End of Speciesism event, he led participants in reading an animal-rights declaration and performed songs about slaughter and leather goods.[^7]

These public issues make it hard to place Dwagie’s music back inside the entertainment market alone. He understands hip-hop as a sonic tool for intervening in reality. Human rights, Taiwan-centered identity, animal rights, and concern for vulnerable groups later all became part of his work and public image.

Political expression also carries costs. At the end of 2016, media reported that a list of Taiwanese artists allegedly banned in China was circulating; the authenticity of the list was not officially verified at the time. After being named on it, Dwagie responded that he had long talked about human rights, politics, and universal values, and that these were naturally at odds with the Chinese Communist Party’s position. He also said, “earning a little less money is fine.”[^8] That response is often cited because it states his position plainly: rap must preserve the freedom to speak, even if that compresses certain markets.

## Body, Family, and the Side Beyond “Hardness”

Dwagie’s stage image is easy to wrap in a few words: large build, heavy voice, direct lyrics, clear political stance. Those features are real, but if you only look at that side, you miss why he turned hip-hop into education and public advocacy.

PTS’s _Guess Who_ noted his family background: his father was a police officer, his mother a teacher, and his brothers also entered public-service work such as policing and firefighting.[^6] Placed inside his music, that background creates an interesting contrast. He performs hip-hop, a form often seen as rebellious, profane, underground, and anti-authoritarian, while the everyday occupational imagination around him was made of police officers, teachers, firefighters — roles inside systems of care and order.

PTS also recorded that he practiced judo as a student, then was injured, an embodied experience that shifted him from sports toward music.[^6] The stage name “Dwagie” (大支, literally something like “big branch” or “big one”) therefore carries a bodily feeling. Rap depends on words, but also on breath, lung capacity, rhythm, stress, and the pressure of a body sent into a live room. From “Taiwan Song” to “The People,” one of his most recognizable traits is this heavy but precise bodily rhythm.

That also helps explain why his later vegetarianism and animal-rights work did not come out of nowhere. The “hard” force was redirected. For Dwagie, rap can curse, declare a position, and speak for lives that cannot speak. That turn makes his public image hard to compress into one label: he is at once an aggressive rapper, a schoolmaster teaching students to write rap, an advocate appearing at animal-rights events, and a creator who puts religion and questions of life into his albums.

## Later Albums: Politics, Language, and Genre Experiments

After _The People_, Dwagie’s work did not stop at one formula. His discography includes _No Listening_ (不聽, 2013), _Hard_ (硬, 2016), _Dark Web_ (暗網, 2018), _Burning Rap Soul_ (燃燒の饒舌魂, 2019), _Music Politics_ (音政樂治, 2020), _Captain Taiwan_ (台灣隊長, 2021), and _Love_ (愛, 2023), moving across political rap, Taiwanese Hokkien, hardcore, social issues, and personal feeling.[^3]

During the _No Listening_ period, he collaborated with U.S. rapper Nas on the title track, creating a direct link between Taiwanese rap and an international hip-hop icon.[^3] _Hard_ was nominated for Best Mandarin Male Singer at the 28th Golden Melody Awards and won the Jury Award at the 8th Golden Indie Music Awards; _Dark Web_ was nominated at the 30th Golden Melody Awards for Album of the Year, Best Taiwanese Album, and Best Taiwanese Male Singer, among other categories.[^3] These awards do not need to be read as a simple success résumé. More important is the gradual expansion of institutional recognition. Dwagie’s work moved among Mandarin albums, Taiwanese-language albums, hip-hop genre awards, and mainstream singer awards, pushing Taiwanese rap against the boundaries of different categories.

In 2021, _Captain Taiwan_ brought Taiwanese Hokkien and the memory of public figures to the foreground. Existing research notes indicate that the album was mainly in Taiwanese and included songs paying tribute to figures such as Chi Chia-wei, Chien-Ming Wang, and Lee Teng-hui. This continued a habit Dwagie had built early: beyond writing the self, he also wrote people, events, and value conflicts from Taiwan’s public memory into songs. Taiwanese Hokkien here becomes a narrative language capable of carrying biography, politics, tribute, and argument.

## “The Same Seed” and The Rappers

After 2017, Chinese-language hip-hop exploded through Chinese talent shows, and Taiwanese rap received more attention. In the CNA interview, Dwagie used a metaphor that fits his own path well: “The same seed grows differently in different soil.”[^1]

The line responds to differences between the Taiwan and China markets, but it also returns to Taiwanese rap itself. When the seed of U.S. hip-hop enters Taiwan, its shape is remade by Taiwanese Hokkien, Hakka, Indigenous languages, north-south scene differences, identity politics after democratization, and the intertwined histories of independent music and social movements. Dwagie’s own route is what that seed looks like in Tainan soil.

In 2021, _The Rappers_ premiered. The show was produced by Sanlih and MTV and aired on TTV, MTV, MyVideo, and other platforms; Leo Wang, Dwagie, Razor Chiang, and Kumachan served as mentors or judges.[^9] For many young listeners, Dwagie was no longer only an OG name from early underground lists. He was a judge, teacher, and schoolmaster on screen. The shift was symbolic: Taiwanese rap, which once required message boards, campus networks, and underground demos to find fellow travelers, had grown enough to use television as an entry point for a new generation.

Dwagie’s role on the show was not limited to scoring. He represented a memory of early Taiwanese hip-hop: Tainan, Taiwanese Hokkien, political intensity, Kung-Fu Entertainment, and the long road from underground to mainstream. When younger rappers displayed flow, battles, language, and attitude on camera, Dwagie’s presence reminded viewers that talent shows were only a new entrance. Long before that, people had already been practicing Taiwanese rap in very small venues.

## “The Past Was Like This; the Future Is Still Unknown”

The title of CNA’s 2019 interview was “The Past Was Like This; the Future Is Still Unknown.” The phrase fits Dwagie well. It does not sound like an elder who has secured his position announcing the next project; it sounds more like someone who has spent twenty years doing the work and has begun to feel energy, time, and responsibility changing.[^1]

In that interview, he talked about younger singers, teaching, and the exposure brought by China’s hip-hop boom, but also about how long he could keep going. This is the realism of a middle-aged musician: the early underground scene was held up by passion, but later came a studio, students, albums, programs, and public issues, each adding weight. As the title “schoolmaster” becomes more stable, the creator himself also has to face a question: how do you keep writing your own songs while leading people, teaching people, and responding to public events?

This is also where Dwagie differs from the simple label “political rapper.” Political speech becomes news; teaching and scene-building are slow work. News events are easy to remember, while a recording room, a summer class, and a group of young people slowly becoming rappers are less likely to make headlines. What Dwagie leaves behind is a set of songs with clear positions, and also a way for a scene to continue: someone opened a door in Tainan first, so people behind him had somewhere to come in and practice.

## Tainan, Taiwanese Hokkien, Publicness

Dwagie’s importance does not rest on whether he will always represent the highest technical level of Taiwanese rap, nor on whether every political statement he makes wins agreement. His more stable historical position is that he tied three things together: Taiwanese Hokkien rap, the Tainan scene, and public issues.

He made Taiwanese Hokkien rap a declaration of subjecthood. He helped Tainan grow a southern base like Kung-Fu Entertainment. And he made it possible for a rapper to put human rights, animal rights, Taiwan-centered identity, and social issues at the creative core, rather than leaving them outside in the entertainment market.

In Taiwan’s hip-hop story, MC HotDog is often placed at the point where Mandarin rap entered the mainstream market; Sung Yueh-ting is often placed at the point of lyric craft and life narrative; Soft Lipa and KAO!INC. are often placed along the poetic and jazz-inflected route. Dwagie’s position is rougher and more public. He brought the microphone to Tainan, to the street, to international media, and beside political issues, making Taiwanese rap ask an early and harder question: what language, what accent, and what posture should this island use to speak itself?

That question also gives Dwagie’s work its friction. People who like him often see him as one of the few artists willing to sing Taiwan identity, human rights, and animal rights plainly. People who dislike him may find his politics too strong, his tone too hard, his positions too front-loaded. That split is itself Dwagie’s place in Taiwanese popular culture. He is hard to sort into pure entertainment, and hard to reduce to a single activist role. His songs contain stagecraft and rhythm, but also slogans, attacks, tributes, and conviction; his studio contains music production, but also teaching and scene cultivation.

So the best entrance to Dwagie is still music. Political events pass in waves, and public controversies keep changing, but the Tainan accent in “Taiwan Song,” the attempt to make a complete rap album in _Lotus from the Tongue_, and the southern scene slowly grown out of a Kung-Fu Entertainment training room are the steadiest parts he leaves in Taiwanese hip-hop history. They mark the process by which a Taiwanese rapper turned an imported form into a local sound, and the process by which Taiwanese rap moved from the underground into public language.
