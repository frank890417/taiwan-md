# Liao Hong-ji

## Thirty-Second Overview

Liao Hong-ji (born 11 November 1957), a native of Hualien, is Taiwan's most representative contemporary marine literature writer. In 1992, at age 35, he abandoned life on land to become a professional fisherman, and in 1996 initiated Taiwan's first civilian cetacean survey project. He successively founded the "Kuroshio Ocean Education Foundation" (1998) and the "Hualien Formosa Association" (2022), and through the "Kuroshio 101 Drift Project" and the "Sperm Whale π Project," pushed the Taiwanese perspective from the shoreline out into the deep ocean. His works not only document the life tensions of sea-faring people, but also attempt, through scientific surveys and environmental education, to rebuild a profound "marine culture" on an island where "seafood culture" prevails [^1] [^2] [^10].

## The "Counter-Intuitive" Choice to Flee the Land

Liao Hong-ji's literary path began with an escape from the land-based system. A graduate of Hualien Senior High School, he once worked as a purchaser for a cement company and also ventured to Indonesia to engage in shrimp farming. However, anti-Chinese movements in Indonesia and workplace interpersonal troubles on land made him yearn for a spiritual outlet. In 1992, in an era when Taiwanese society generally regarded the ocean as a dangerous frontier, he chose in the prime of life to discard stable work and return to his hometown of Hualien to become a grassroots fisherman. This act, viewed at the time as "not doing one's proper job," became the most important turning point in Taiwan's marine literature [^3] [^13].

📝 Curator's Note: Liao Hong-ji's transformation proves that the most profound Taiwan stories often lie not in the bustling urban centers, but in the marginal waves forgotten by mainstream society.

He was not merely fishing at sea, but dialoguing with the ocean through fishhooks. Beyond early experience on sailfish boats, he also sailed on distant-water squid-jigging vessels, executed a circumnavigation project, and even traveled on container ships along European routes. These rich experiences spanning coastal and deep-sea waters freed his writing entirely from the sterile groanings of the literati, presenting instead a vitality replete with the stench of fish and the temperature of sea salt [^13].

### The "Mother's Test" from Family

The greatest resistance Liao Hong-ji faced in choosing a life at sea came from his family. In traditional family values, fishing is high-risk work with lower social status. His father repeatedly urged him to give it up, and even at Liao's new book launch, speaking as a reader, raised his hand to ask: "Mr. Liao Hong-ji, when you go out to sea, do you ever think of your parents?" This question profoundly revealed the tension between his pursuit of an ocean dream and family affection. Yet it was precisely this persistence toward the ocean that ultimately let him find his true "home" [^13].

## A Cultural Revolution from "Seafood" to "Ocean"

In the 1990s, most Taiwanese people's understanding of marine life stopped at "seafood." Liao Hong-ji realized that without establishing an emotional connection between islanders and the ocean, conservation would remain empty talk. In 1998, he founded the "Kuroshio Ocean Education Foundation" and began systematically training interpreters, transforming traditional fishing and hunting culture into ecological observation culture [^5] [^6].

This revolution was not smooth sailing. In the early days of promoting whale-watching, he faced skepticism from academia about "ecological disturbance," and pressure from animal rights groups concerned that commercial whale-watching boats would disrupt cetacean behavior. In practice, he also had to balance commercial operations with ecological education, confronting practical challenges such as "noise affecting ecology." Liao Hong-ji insisted that "cultivating ecological awareness is making friends"; he believed that only by letting people personally witness whales and dolphins leaping in the sea could a genuine resolve to protect the ocean truly grow [^3] [^13].

## Kuroshio Drift: Recovering the Island's Great Pulse

In August 2016, Liao Hong-ji launched the "Kuroshio 101 Drift Project." He and his team designed an unpowered square raft floating approximately 45 centimeters above water and 20 centimeters below, setting off from the waters off Dawu, Taitung, entrusting themselves entirely to the Kuroshio Current. This experiment, lasting approximately 100 hours and drifting to the waters off Su'ao, Yilan, was fundamentally meant to verify his inner conviction: Taiwan should not be an island with its back turned to the ocean, but a starting point connected to the pulse of global ocean currents [^7] [^11].

During the drift, he released 99 glass floats containing messages, symbolizing dialogue with the ocean. This grand voyage not only produced the book _Kuroshio Drift_, but was also recorded in the documentary _The Man and His Sea_. He discovered that when speed slows down, the fish and birds at sea grow larger, and the world becomes quiet and vast. This "slow-speed" observation let him redefine Taiwan's territory — territory should not stop at the coastline, but extend with the Kuroshio into the boundless Pacific [^10] [^13].

📝 Curator's Note: Drifting is not going with the flow; it is the highest freedom after relinquishing control — precisely the oceanic character that island Taiwan lacks most.

## Late-Life Concern: The Sperm Whale π Project

Entering his later years, Liao Hong-ji still has not stopped sailing. In 2022 he founded the "Hualien Formosa Association" and promoted the "Visiting the Pacific Sperm Whale π Project" (2023-2025). This project originated from a promise to a sperm whale named "Hua Xiao-xiang." Through systematic surveys, he discovered that from March to October each year, sperm whale pods arrive in batches in Hualien waters, which is very likely a calving and nursery ground for them, confirming the importance of Taiwan's eastern waters as a "marine oasis" [^8] [^9] [^13].

## Literary Achievements and Social Contributions

Liao Hong-ji's publications are abundant; among them _The Sea-Faring People_, _Whale Life Whale World_, _Drifting Prison_, and _From the Deep Sea_ are collectively known as the "Marine Tetralogy," classics of Taiwan's marine literature. His literary achievements have been widely recognized, winning the China Times Literature Award and the Wu Cho-liu Literature Award, and in 2019 he received the 41st Wu San-lien Award in the prose category. In 2024 he was further awarded the Ministry of Education's "Lifetime Contribution Award for Social Education," affirming his long-term dedication to marine education [^10] [^13].

He has served as writer-in-residence at National Taiwan Ocean University, and taught at Tzu Chi University and National Dong Hwa University, continuously leading the younger generation toward the open ocean. To him, the ocean is a book that cannot be finished writing in a lifetime, and he is merely the courier striving to translate the language of seawater into text, attempting to build a bridge that will never be dismantled between the concrete jungle and the azure sea [^1] [^12].
