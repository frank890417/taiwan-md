> **30-second overview:** Liao Tianding, born in 1883 in Qingshui, Taichung, was killed in 1909 around Guanyin Mountain in Bali by Yang Lin, aged 27.
> Japanese police files leave a record of theft, robbery, murder, and pursuit; folk songbooks, storytelling, temples, and games rewrite him as a righteous bandit who robbed the rich to aid the poor.
> This article neither whitewashes him nor treats legend as verdict. It asks instead: why would an island preserve a criminal's name in its own language?[^1] [^2]
>
> **Introduction:** To read Liao Tianding, don't rush to decide hero or criminal. First watch how the same name swaps four identities across police files, Taiwanese songbooks, the Bali cemetery, and digital games.

## First Look at That Wanted Order

November 1909, around Guanyin Mountain in Bali. The Japanese police treated Liao Tianding as a man who had to be caught. The wanted order preserved by the Taiwan Governor-General's Office Police Bureau compresses a real, living person into an image for identification, tracking, and reward. This picture bears no labels of righteous bandit, chivalrous outlaw, or anti-Japanese hero — it is first and foremost a state document for hunting a criminal.[^9]

The "Report on the Pursuit of Liao Tianding" held by the National Archives Administration, Taiwan Historica, confirms one unromantic but crucial fact: Liao Tianding was not a figure invented by later unofficial histories. The Taipei Prefecture magistrate reported to the Governor-General's Office how police investigated his hideouts, contacted accomplices, and finally, in the encirclement at Profound Mountain Tail (尖山尾) on Guanyin Mountain, Yang Lin struck him dead with a hoe.[^1]

1883: Liao Tianding was born in Qingshui, Taichung. 1909: he died in Bali, aged 27. These two years form a skeleton, yet cannot explain why his name has lived over a hundred years.[^2]

| Surviving Materials                       | What They Tell Us                                                                            | Conclusions They Cannot Directly Support                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Taiwan Governor-General's Office archives | He existed; police pursued him; he died around Bali                                          | Cannot therefore write him as an anti-Japanese hero           |
| Postwar songbooks and storybooks          | Folk culture wrote him as a chivalrous, righteous bandit who robbed the rich to aid the poor | Cannot treat songbook plots as point-by-point historical fact |
| Films, television, and games              | Each generation re-summons him with its own media                                            | Cannot treat adaptations as biography                         |

Placed together, these three material types give Liao Tianding a three-dimensional outline: **police recorded a public-order problem to be eliminated; the people preserved a name onto which they could project grievance.**

## He Was Not Hero First, Criminal Later

_Taiwan Panorama_ (光華雜誌), drawing on Qingshui household registers, Japanese newspapers, and Governor-General's Office archives, assembles a less romantic life. Liao Tianding's father died when he was eight; his mother remarried. He first lived with his grandmother, then was raised by an aunt. Around age 18 he began offending. At 20, the Taichung District Court sentenced him for three thefts; during imprisonment he escaped twice.[^3]

At 22, he conspired with Zhang Fu of Banqiao to rob a tea merchant, involving NT$3,000, and accidentally wounded an accomplice while resisting arrest. At 23, he and accomplices repeatedly burglarized wealthy homes; a small pistol and Japanese saw were found on him. These details do not make him likeable, but they remind us: Liao Tianding was not a pure hero first misunderstood by the masses, then smeared by officials.[^3]

In August 1909, he stole a police pistol, ammunition, and saber, becoming a top priority for Japanese police. Then came the Lin Ben-yuan family robbery, the shooting of detective Chen Liang-jiu, and the robbery of village head Li Hong's house in Wugoukeng. From summer to November, several cases stacked up in short order, pushing a repeat offender to the colonial police's number-one target.[^2] [^3]

Here a familiar label needs putting back in place. Liao Tianding is often called an "anti-Japanese hero," but surviving official files do not classify him as a political criminal or member of an anti-Japanese organization. The Taiwan Historica's phrasing is closer: under Japanese colonial rule, he was a habitual thief and murderer, not an anti-Japanese bandit acting with political aims.[^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Writing Liao Tianding only as an "anti-Japanese hero" seems to elevate him, but actually hands him over to another convenient myth. The truly hard part is that he may have been simultaneously criminal, resister, and a figure later generations used to voice society's grievances.

"Rob the rich to aid the poor" cannot be treated as his ledger either. Official files prove cases, pursuit, and death. Whether he regularly distributed loot to the poor comes mainly from later oral accounts, songbooks, temple legends, and storytelling. The National Museum of Taiwan History's collection notes therefore adopt a restrained formulation: his theft, robbery, and murder records are historical background, while "anti-Japanese hero, righteous bandit" is an image gradually shaped by legend.[^4]

## Archives Are Not Transparent Windows

Official documents matter, but they are not transparent windows. The "Report on the Pursuit of Liao Tianding" is a police report written for superiors; the wanted order is a tool for police and public to identify the target. They tell us how the government described Liao Tianding, but do not automatically tell us how residents of Qingshui, Dadaocheng, or Bali understood him.[^1] [^9]

The _Taiwan Nichinichi Shinpō_ (臺灣日日新報) occupies a more delicate position. _Taiwan Panorama_ notes that Liao Tianding appeared in the social news pages over a dozen times; as the case escalated, coverage reached half or nearly full pages. These reports may have served the official aim of making the public fear him and not harbor a fugitive, yet they also let his name spread through streets and alleys faster than ordinary criminals'.[^3]

Indeed, some people then provided him food, shelter, or tools, and were arrested by Japanese police for it. These records cannot prove everyone saw him as a righteous bandit, but they do prove the pursuit was not a two-player game between police and fugitive. Local residents, village heads, accomplices, newspaper readers, and the colonial government all co-produced the story's early versions.[^3]

## Why the Common People Saved Him a Place

The answer is not only "he stole from the rich." More crucially, he made power look clumsy in everyday stories for the first time: police could be mocked, police pistols could be stolen, wealthy mansions were not forever safe. Researcher Li Ji-hua argues that Liao Tianding's actions may not have had a clear political platform, yet carried a rebellious character — targeting only Japanese or wealthy Taiwanese, and enjoying teasing "A-běn-á" (Japanese policemen).[^3]

The colonial government used newspapers to mold him into a ferocious bandit; the public read from the same papers "the authorities can't catch him either." His heroization did not require proving him morally flawless. It was more like little people retelling a bad news story until a breath of resistance surfaced.

Scholarship also reminds us that the Liao Tianding legend is not a fixed old tale. _A Study of the Morphological Changes in the Liao Tianding Legend_ divides materials into three themes — anti-Japanese, loyalty/affection, and myth — and observes how they kept shifting from the Japanese era through postwar to post-martial-law. In other words, every retelling adjusts "what kind of person deserves to be called a hero" for its own era.[^8]

| Period               | Main Media                                                    | Liao Tianding in the Story                                                                              |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Japanese era to 1945 | Police files, Chinese-language newspapers, folk oral accounts | Habitual thief to be pursued, also a name that embarrassed colonial power                               |
| 1945–1987            | Songbooks, films, storytelling, local worship                 | Righteous bandit who robbed rich to aid poor, anti-Japanese hero, chivalrous underworld figure          |
| 1987–present         | Academic research, publishing, games, digital communities     | Re-examined, re-translated, and re-handed to the next generation as a historical figure to operate with |

This table is not a "progress ladder" drawing the legend ever closer to truth. It is more a media map reminding us that each era loads its own questions onto Liao Tianding. The Japanese era asked who challenged the police; postwar asked who could represent resistance; post-martial-law began asking how heroization itself happens.[^8]

## The Last Night: History Has No Single Version Either

November 1909. Liao Tianding hid around Guanyin Mountain in Bali. The official "Report on the Pursuit of Liao Tianding" records that Yang Lin gave police a tip; police surrounded the area; Liao Tianding was finally struck dead with a hoe. Taiwan Historica's compilation gives the death date as 18 November; the stele preserved at Hanmin Temple (漢民祠) is engraved 19 November.[^1] [^2]

As for why Yang Lin betrayed him, at least two explanations survive. One says for the reward; another says to save the Yang family. _Taiwan Panorama_ notes neither can be lightly taken as final truth. What is certain: Yang Lin did cooperate with police, and Liao Tianding died not under Japanese police guns but in an encirclement where acquaintance and police overlapped.[^3]

This is a later commemorative site of his death, not proof that every legendary detail has been historically verified.[^11]_

This ending has no clean closure like a hero film. No decisive duel that cleanly separates good and evil, no will explaining motives for everyone. Only a pursuit report preserved by the colonial government, a tombstone later worshipped, and a Yang Lin who keeps switching positions across versions.

## After Death, He Truly Began to Transform

After Liao Tianding died, the story did not stop — it entered another lifeline. Taiwan Historica records that Bali was initially just a small cemetery. After a Liao Tianding film premiered in 1956 and became a media focus, locals built Hanmin Temple in 1958, layering cemetery, worship, and film memory onto the same ground.[^2]

![八里廖添丁石像](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/25/%E5%BB%96%E6%B7%BB%E4%B8%81%E7%9F%B3%E5%83%8F_%28cropped%29.jpg)

_Figure: Liao Tianding statue at Bali's Tianding Park. Original image from [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%BB%96%E6%B7%BB%E4%B8%81%E7%9F%B3%E5%83%8F_%28cropped%29.jpg), photo by Outlookxp, CC BY-SA 4.0.[^12]\_

The _New Song Liao Tianding_ (《新歌廖添丁》) collected by the National Cultural Memory Bank writes his story in seven-character lines, sung in Southern Min (Tâi-gí / Taiwanese). The collection note presents the shift directly: in the Governor-General's eyes a vicious criminal, Liao Tianding, through rural oral telling and operatic rendering, became the anti-Japanese hero and legendary righteous bandit Taiwanese understood.[^5]

This does not mean the folk "didn't know" he killed. A closer reading: the folk did not stuff all of one person's acts into a single drawer. He could have crimes, and also be a spokesperson for an unjust era. A hero is not a medal on a judgment sheet, but the people's way of redistributing responsibility and dignity in story.

By the 1990s, storytelling, radio, television, and dance gave him new bodies again. _Taiwan Panorama_ records Wu Le-tian's long-running telling of Liao Tianding, and how audiences understood him as "righteous bandit," loyal, standing in for those who dared not speak. The same report warns that the story kept adding new characters and dramatized episodes the more it was told, growing ever more like a "great hero."[^3]

## From Taiwanese Songbooks to Game Screens

Liao Tianding's next rebirth was not in a temple, but on screens. _Openbook_ notes that the 2004 Flash game _The Legend of Tianding_ (《神影無蹤廖添丁》) became many players' childhood memory. The 2021 _The Legend of Tianding_ (《廖添丁—稀代兇賊の最期》) then used Taiwanese text, voice, manga style, and Dadaocheng scenes to hand this name to a new generation.[^6]

When English game media _Game Developer_ interviewed the dev team, the team explained they referenced books, photos, and Japanese-era Dadaocheng architecture, but frankly admitted the game prioritizes playability, not treating every battle as historical reenactment. This admission matters. The game does not turn archives into animation; it lets players walk once through the space between historical materials, legend, and imagination.[^7]

So, climbing with a waist-cloth, running across rooftops to escape Japanese police in the game does not mean sources prove every move. It means later generations needed an operable Liao Tianding. Songbooks let people sing his story; storytelling let people hear his story; games let people use their own hands to carry him through Dadaocheng's street corners once.

## Keep the Name, Not Whitewash Him

What Liao Tianding most deserves to be remembered for is not the binary "was he Taiwan's Robin Hood?" That metaphor is easy to grasp, but quickly flattens Taiwan's own history. Robin Hood is someone else's template; Liao Tianding's materials come from Qingshui, Taipei, Dadaocheng, Bali, colonial police, Chinese-language newspapers, Taiwanese songbooks, and a cemetery that keeps being rebuilt.

Surviving materials show a line that cannot be omitted. Victims and violence cannot vanish because the legend sounds good; Liao Tianding's crimes cannot be rewritten as pure justice because later generations sympathize. But equally impossible to erase is why people, after a criminal's death, would erect a stele, build a shrine, compose songs, tell stories, make games.

Perhaps Liao Tianding left Taiwan not a hero's résumé, but a persistently prickly question: **When official files leave only charges, why do the people still preserve a criminal's name?**

The answer lies not in exonerating him, but in understanding how memory works. What people preserve may not be Liao Tianding himself, but the self that once badly wanted to see power embarrassed and had no other way to say it. When we read his name today, what is truly summoned is not just a 1909 fugitive in the Bali mountains, but how Taiwan sang a criminal's crimes, resistance, fear, and hope into a song that never finishes.
