# Chef Sheng-kai: From Steamed Egg Videos to Frozen New Year's Cuisine—The Trust Cost of Culinary Influencers

> **30-Second Overview:** Huang Sheng-kai (黃聖凱) left school at sixteen to enter the catering industry. At twenty-four, using his savings and loans, he opened his first Swatow (錵鑶) Japanese restaurant in Yonghe. Later, a roughly 100-second instructional video on steamed eggs transformed him from a restaurant owner into a culinary influencer, allowing him to integrate home cooking videos, his restaurant, frozen foods, and e-commerce into a single brand. What is truly worth remembering is not the inspirational headline of "from small chef to chairman," but rather that when kitchen skills are amplified into commodities, trust is amplified along with them. [^1] [^2] [^3]

## Learn to Carry Trays Before Learning to Earn Trust

Chef Sheng-kai's story did not begin online. According to an interview by _Business Weekly_ (周刊王), he was not fond of studying while attending Songshan Industrial & Commercial Vocational High School and left school at sixteen to work as a waiter in a Chinese banquet hall. During busy periods, he might work sixteen hours a day, trading time for experience before transitioning into Japanese cuisine under a master chef. This experience is more than just a "hardship" backstory; it explains why his later culinary content does not merely demonstrate dishes in front of a camera, but reflects an understanding of ingredients, processes, service flow, and customer expectations from the front lines of catering. [^2]

At twenty-four, he pooled approximately NT$2 million from savings and loans to open the first Swatow in Yonghe District, New Taipei City. Reports note the shop had only sixteen seats, with monthly revenue of about NT$400,000 and a profit margin of roughly ten percent during its early stages. To determine his culinary style, he observed the local community demographics and chose to offer creative _omakase_ sushi rather than simply replicating traditional Taiwanese-style Japanese cuisine found in surrounding shops. [^2]

There is an easily overlooked detail in this entrepreneurial history: Chef Sheng-kai did not open a shop because he already had fans; rather, he accumulated a sense for costs, target audiences, and products through physical catering first. When videos later brought traffic, that traffic was not starting from zero but was being funneled into an existing catering brand. [^1] [^2]

📝 **Curator's Note:** "The tray-carrying boy who became a chairman with NT$400 million annual revenue" is a useful news headline, but it is not a complete understanding of the person. Attributing success solely to hardship obscures more practical abilities: identifying target markets, testing products, controlling processes, and knowing when to translate restaurant experience for the home kitchen.

## One Steamed Egg Video Changed How People Watch

Chef Sheng-kai's foray into video was not the result of a mature media plan. When a friend asked why he only made Japanese cuisine if he knew how to cook, it prompted him to begin translating his professional restaurant skills into demonstrations understandable for average households. An interview with _World Catering Trends_ (世界餐飲趨勢) noted that he deliberately used common household cookware and heat levels, simplifying processes so viewers had the opportunity to actually recreate them at home. [^1]

The catalyst that brought massive viewership to his channel was a roughly 100-second video titled "Change Your Ugly Steamed Eggs." Reports from _Business Weekly_ and _Next Magazine_ (鏡週刊) both view this video as a turning point. After the video went viral, Chef Sheng-kai realized through fan comments that basic techniques taken for granted by professional chefs were not necessarily familiar to many households, leading him to launch the "Swatow Home Cooking Classroom." [^2] [^3]

The format of this channel is very similar to a miniature version of a home kitchen. Ingredients can be obtained at markets or convenience stores, cookware does not need to be restaurant-grade, videos are usually short, and demonstrations do not pretend the audience has professional equipment. Consequently, the value of the culinary content shifted from "watching something impressive" to "feeling confident enough to try it." [^1] [^2]

Chef Sheng-kai also used authenticity as a brand identifier. In interviews, he stated that things like rice grains splashing during stir-frying or food dropping while plating are things that can happen in an ordinary home kitchen and do not need to be edited out for the sake of visual perfection. This choice makes him appear less like a chef who only displays finished products and more like someone bringing restaurant techniques into home conditions. [^1]

## From Cooking Videos to a Food Brand

When "Swatow Home Cooking Classroom" accumulated a large following, Chef Sheng-kai did not treat it merely as an advertising billboard; instead, he extended the culinary techniques featured in his content into products. _Business Weekly_ reported that he integrated frozen and shelf-stable foods, cookware, and e-commerce channels into his business layout, even planning to push food products into Hong Kong and Macau markets. [^2]

An interview with _World Catering Trends_ explained this transformation more clearly: a restaurant is limited by seating, location, and on-site labor, whereas a food brand can use frozen, shelf-stable, retail, and home delivery methods to bring the culinary experience—originally only available in-store—into the consumer's home. This represents a shift from "cooking a table of food" to "designing a reproducible product." [^1]

This is where the difference between a culinary influencer and a general catering celebrity lies. A celebrity chef's influence may stop at restaurant reservations and media exposure, but a culinary influencer can string together demonstrations, comments, products, and delivery into a single consumption path. Viewers first learn a dish, then buy a piece of cookware or a meal kit, and finally use "Chef Sheng-kai says this dish is good" as a preliminary guarantee of quality. Content, therefore, is no longer just content; it is the gateway to product trust. [^1] [^2]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Traffic is not proof of product quality. It can make a new product visible faster, but it cannot replace recipes, labeling, preservation, logistics, or quality control. Cooking videos build expectation; food brands must use physical goods to fulfill that expectation.

## The New Year's Food Incident: When Video Demonstrations Meet Delivered Meal Boxes

The 2020 New Year's cuisine controversy is a crucial piece of material for understanding Chef Sheng-kai, as it made the gap between content branding and food responsibility visible. _Next Magazine_ reported that consumers claimed the New Year's meals they received differed from the promotions and video demonstrations, with some demanding refunds. Swatow apologized afterward, while Chef Sheng-kai filed a lawsuit against the manufacturer to seek compensation. [^5]

Another report by _Next Magazine_ recorded that Swatow claimed consumer refund amounts exceeded NT$10 million, while the manufacturer argued that the recipe and cost of the New Year's meals should be mutually understood by both the content creator and the producer; they further countered that promotional packaging created unrealistic consumer expectations. The court ruling was reported to find that part of the defects in the New Year's meals were the responsibility of the factory, which ultimately paid Swatow NT$660,000 for consumer refunds. It is important to distinguish between different levels here: consumer complaints, the claims made by both parties in litigation, and the scope of responsibility confirmed by a court ruling are not the same thing. [^6]

In a response published by _EBC News_ (東森新聞), Chef Sheng-kai apologized again for the New Year's food lapses that year and stated that some of the costs listed in the judgment were not the total production costs, noting that the case would still be handled by lawyers. _NOWnews_ interviewed New Year's food judges and suppliers to discuss dishes, portions, logistics, frozen storage, and marketing costs, pointing out that whether a price is reasonable cannot be judged by a single cost figure alone. [^4] [^7]

The most important takeaway from this incident is not the easily viral label of "influencer selling overpriced New Year's food," but rather how brand responsibility was broken down into several practical issues: whether the dishes in promotions matched the received products, how the OEM and the brand verified quality, how consumers obtained refunds, who monitored logistics and preservation, and whether a creator's face and voice induce higher trust in consumers than general commodities. [^5] [^6] [^4] [^7]

## The Reality of Culinary Influencers is More Than Just a Filming Style

Chef Sheng-kai's content emphasizes lack of excessive polish, a type of "authenticity" that is very useful in cooking tutorials. It allows viewers to see the limitations of a home kitchen and lowers beginners' fear of failure. However, when the same person begins selling frozen foods, authenticity cannot stop at rice grains and the sound of spatulas in a video; it must enter product specifications, preservation, and after-sales processes.

This is the most easily overlooked turning point in culinary content. Viewers can accept a mistake in an instructional video, but they do not necessarily accept quality issues in a paid product. The former is a sense of intimacy during viewing; the latter is responsibility during a transaction. The former makes the creator feel like "one of us," while the latter requires the brand to prove it is more capable of controlling food than an average household. [^1] [^5] [^6]

_Next Magazine_ also noted that following the New Year's controversy, Chef Sheng-kai continued to launch frozen and shelf-stable foods, cookware, and catering brands. This does not mean the controversy did not happen, nor does it mean the brand has been forgiven by everyone. It simply shows that the recovery of an influencer brand does not end with a single apology; it must be continuously verified through the next product, the next explanation, and the next consumer experience. [^3]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Chef Sheng-kai's case is not suitable for being written as a simple success or failure. What is more worth observing is how the same "down-to-earth" language can simultaneously build trust while increasing the degree to which a brand is expected, questioned, and held accountable.

## He Turned the Kitchen into Media, and Brought Media Back to the Dining Table

Chef Sheng-kai's position in Taiwan's influencer history is not just due to follower counts or the scale of his catering business. He made a very specific conversion visible: professional restaurant skills, once translated through short videos, can enter the home kitchen, and then return to the consumer's table through commercialization.

This path seems natural, but in reality, every step changes the nature of responsibility. A restaurant chef primarily faces dine-in customers; a cooking tutorial creator faces viewers; a food brand faces consumers involved in payment, preservation, delivery, and returns. Chef Sheng-kai moving from the kitchen to a platform, and then from a platform to a food brand, is equivalent to placing himself within three different evaluation systems. [^1] [^2] [^6]

Therefore, writing about Chef Sheng-kai cannot just be about "knowing how to eat" or "knowing how to sell." What is truly interesting is that he placed the craftsmanship of catering, the intimacy of social media videos, and the scalability of food products on the same table. That table can be very lively, but it can also suddenly become very quiet when quality issues arise. The more successful a brand becomes, the less it can rely solely on the creator's personal charisma, and the more it must let viewers see how products are made, verified, and corrected.

## Conclusion: After a Steamed Egg Video, There is an Entire Chain of Responsibility

Chef Sheng-kai's story began with a steamed egg video, but it should not stop at "overnight fame." He first accumulated skills on the catering front lines, then used home cooking videos to lower the barrier to entry, and finally converted viewership into restaurant, food, and e-commerce brands. This is a very characteristic influencer path in Taiwan, as it places neighborhood catering, home kitchens, social platforms, and holiday consumption within the same commercial cycle.

However, when content becomes a commodity, a creator's voice is no longer just a recommendation. It participates in the consumer's imagination regarding portions, quality, and reasonable pricing. The New Year's food incident reminds us that the hardest thing for an influencer brand to maintain is not exposure, but the distance between a promise and the physical object. Chef Sheng-kai is worth documenting not because he provides a wealth-building template anyone can copy, but because his case asks a very clear question: **When the audience believes in a person's skill, what must a brand use to prove that this belief has not been wasted?** [^5] [^6] [^4]
