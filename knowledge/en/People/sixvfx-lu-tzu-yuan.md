> **30-second overview**: Six Fingers Abyss didn't learn to please the algorithm before starting VFX. Lu Tzu-yuan encountered the all-English After Effects interface in high school, breaking down the originally hard-to-enter software into tutorial and experimental videos. When VFX became his channel's language, he moved the same skillset into freelance work, the sixvfx VFX tutorial site, and the Infinite Design Academy. This is a story of a Taiwan creator turning behind-the-scenes processes into public curriculum.

In 2011, Lu Tzu-yuan was still exploring what to do in the future. He began self-learning from the English-interface After Effects, later putting his learning process and video experiments online. Public interview records note that he would break current events into visualizable ideas, also using Notion to list props, draw storyboards, then complete videos in stages with Premiere and After Effects. [^2]

The counterintuitive aspect of this character story isn't "a young person succeeds via the internet." What's more worth recording: Six Fingers Abyss turned the work most easily overlooked by audiences—VFX, storyboarding, props, editing, and teaching—into the channel's own subject. When he laid out the software interface, shooting process, and post-production time before audiences, they saw not just the result, but how the result was made.

📝 **Curator's note:** Most influencer stories start from the on-camera persona. Six Fingers Abyss's entry point is behind the camera—those technical jobs usually not written into end credits instead became his most recognizable language.

## How an All-English Interface Became a Starting Point

Publicly released interview records from the National Tainan University of Applied Sciences (Nan Ying University) Department of Fine Arts in 2024 record that Lu Tzu-yuan had self-taught Adobe After Effects in high school. The page also segments channel growth by time points: ~3,000 subscribers in January 2016, 100,000 in January 2019, 1 million in January 2024. These figures are records compiled by the school page at that time and should not be repurposed as current unchanging channel statistics. [^2]

From the book introduction compiled by Fanggezi, Lu Tzu-yuan was not a model student pushed along by school in his early days. The author describes him being questioned by classmates with "isn't it just editing a video, adding subtitles?"—this mockery instead prompted him to face the all-English software interface. This is a secondhand article recounting book content; this article treats it as book narrative, not rewriting it as Lu Tzu-yuan's original words from another interview. [^3]

He later didn't treat mastering the software as a personal secret. The sixvfx VFX tutorial site and Infinite Design Academy turned "I can do it" into "others can follow along." The Apple Podcasts program introduction also places him in a parent-child career dialogue context, discussing how families face dreams, skills, and uncertainty when a child wants to become a YouTuber. [^4]

📝 **Curator's note:** Self-learning is most easily told as a myth of individual willpower. But the truly Taiwan-flavored part here is that he left behind the walls he hit, letting others take fewer detours.

## VFX Isn't Decoration, But a Content Philosophy

In creative interview materials, Six Fingers Abyss says he treats VFX as embellishment, with the video's main axis still on the content to be conveyed. He also mentions that when a topic can't be completed after thinking for over two days, he seeks alternatives, because once a video goes live it exists permanently. [^2]

Put these two sentences together and you see a work ethic not quite like a "show-off tech influencer." VFX quantity isn't the goal; it must serve what the video wants to say. The creator's technical ability must therefore submit to content's rhythm. This is also why his works can simultaneously approach tech teaching, life experiments, and entertainment, not just stay in the narrow category of software tutorials.

The 2024 Nan Ying University interview adds another time slice. The school page records his YouTube subscribers at 1.5 million and Instagram followers at 2.48 million on April 26, 2024. The page also preserves the interviewer and interviewee's discussion on market, entrepreneurship, and value accumulation. These figures should stay in their dates, because a creator's scale changes but the source's time doesn't. [^2]

## From Creation to Teaching, Then to Team

Public interview materials discuss that Six Fingers Abyss's team coordinates remotely, and has planned tools like auto-editing and auto-subtitling, hoping to let creators spend less time on trivial tasks. His direction is no longer just one person filming, but trying to handle the workflows creators encounter daily. [^2]

When a YouTuber starts building an education platform, tools, and a team, the question shifts from "what to film next" to "where does a creator actually spend their time daily." Six Fingers Abyss's direction raises at least three work questions: which processes need human judgment, which repetitive tasks can go to tools, which knowledge is worth organizing into courses. This makes his channel not just a work showcase, but a set of answers to content labor.

In a 2022 podcast episode, the program introduction describes Six Fingers Abyss as founder of sixvfx and Infinite Design Academy, linking his self-directed learning experience with "Your Dream Might Be a Bit Risky." This is the program's guest introduction, not an independent financial audit; therefore this article only uses it to support career identity and program theme, not expanding the program description's revenue claims into precise business conclusions. [^4]

The Fanggezi article compiles several methods from the book content: using competitions, client feedback, portfolios, and search results to turn skills into services others can recognize. These are the author's summaries of book content, and material for understanding how Six Fingers Abyss brought technique to market, but not a success formula all Taiwan self-learners can copy. [^3]

📝 **Curator's note:** Teaching skills to others doesn't necessarily make you lose your advantage. Six Fingers Abyss's case instead shows teaching can turn a personal skill into a community, a service, and a professional language.

## The Cycle of Works, Courses, and Community

Six Fingers Abyss's creative path has another easily overlooked detail: works aren't the endpoint, but the entry point to the next learning question. Parenting World Flipped Education discusses self-directed learning and error through his experience. Fanggezi places the book's compiled competitions, client feedback, portfolios, and search results in the context of "how to make skills visible." Put the two sources together and a cycle appears: first produce works, then find problems from feedback, next organize problems into teaching, finally let teaching return to new works. [^1] [^3]

This cycle also explains why his content isn't just showcasing VFX. Showcase only proves a one-time result. Teaching must break results into steps, acknowledge where failure easily occurs, and give another person the chance to redo it on different devices, at different times, with different experience. The Nan Ying University interview places this continuous value creation in entrepreneurship and market context, not just software operation. [^2]

📝 **Curator's note:** True publicness doesn't lie in everyone becoming Six Fingers Abyss, but in whether one person's expertise, once organized, can let the next person find the entry with a little less effort.

## After Traffic Arrives, the Story Doesn't End

In October 2024, DailyView reported Six Fingers Abyss received "Annual Most Valuable Internet Leader YouTuber." The award uses online interaction, positive buzz, and related data as selection context. This award proves he won that year, not directly that he's Taiwan's most important or influential creator. [^5]

SETN's same-day report adds that Six Fingers Abyss spoke at the ceremony about short-form video and overseas markets, describing traffic expansion with the claim of a single video reaching 150 million views. This is the interviewee's statement in the report; this article retains attribution, not writing a single video's peak views as the channel's average performance. [^6]

Mirror Media's same-day report also lists Six Fingers Abyss as the award winner, mentioning he won at age 27. It and SETN's report together show the award event was indeed covered by multiple media, but both revolve around the same ceremony and cannot be miscounted as two independent wins. [^7]

Six Fingers Abyss's traffic growth thus has two different faces. One is subscribers, views, and awards—quantifiable by platforms or media. The other is what he was already doing early on: breaking videos into prop lists, storyboards, editing, VFX, and teaching, then organizing personal experience into knowledge others can use. The former makes him seen; the latter makes him worth writing into Taiwan's internet culture history.

📝 **Curator's note:** Traffic isn't the end of a creator's story. For Six Fingers Abyss, the real question is whether technique can still be taught, the team can still function, and content can still explain one thing clearly after view counts grow large.

## After a Video Goes Live

In a 2020 interview, Six Fingers Abyss used a concrete metaphor: videos exist permanently after going live; looking back at unsatisfactory works is like having something in your body that's uncomfortable whether you cut it out or not. This isn't simple perfectionism, but platform content's special temporality. Traditional assignments handed in, teachers or clients might only see at one time point. YouTube videos get searched, recommended, rewatched; creators can hardly pretend they only exist on the day of publication. [^1]

Therefore, his editing workflow never piles VFX at the end. First rough cut in Premiere, check if the video flows smoothly, then use markers to separate segments needing VFX, finally enter After Effects per topic. This workflow splits creativity and engineering into two phases, also letting team members know when to join. To audiences this might be just a few-minute video. To producers it's a project needing script, props, storyboards, editing, and post-production to coordinate with each other. [^1]

He also doesn't call "having ideas" innate talent. In the interview, Six Fingers Abyss says he practices finding things in life and current events that can turn into visuals, but also admits not everything can become a video. Sometimes he just feels particularly moved by a topic, then finds excitement in the production process. This account is closer to creators' actual work than "inspiration explodes daily": inspiration needs habit, also needs abandoning unsuitable approaches. [^1]

There's another layer to technical education here. When Six Fingers Abyss breaks VFX into watchable steps, he effectively lowers the psychological cost of first attempt for many Taiwan students unfamiliar with post-production software. In the Nan Ying University page's preserved interview, he tells students to continuously create value and find positions where they can exert their value in the present. This shouldn't be treated as an inspirational slogan, but understood back in his work experience: value isn't abstract talent, but something gradually becoming "no one else can do this but you." [^2]

## From Student Status to Market Scene

The Nan Ying University interview also preserves his retrospective on student status. He mentions that even when failing in school, one might still have time to readjust, so he views the schooling phase as a trial period where one can set goals and bear costs. This idea is easily interpreted as demanding students earn money early, but his talk actually simultaneously discusses direction, expertise, and value accumulation, not treating income as the sole metric. [^2]

Fanggezi's compilation of "Your Dream Might Be a Bit Risky" breaks this path into several observable actions: join competitions, keep client feedback, organize portfolio, let others find you via search. These suggestions share a commonality: they require creators to turn "I think I can" into externally verifiable works. For design and VFX work, a portfolio isn't an add-on, but a menu letting stranger clients know what problems you can handle. [^3]

But portfolios also bring new pressure. When skills are packaged as services, creators must respond to client constraints, deadlines, and budgets. When services go on YouTube, creators must also respond to audience expectations and platform rhythm. Six Fingers Abyss later doing videos, tutorial sites, online academies, and tool development simultaneously is precisely because a single work can hardly bear all jobs. Some need to see results, some need to learn steps, some need faster editing. Different products handle different needs, all built on the same VFX and imaging capability. [^1] [^3] [^4]

The Apple Podcasts program puts this experience into parent-child dialogue: child says they want to be a YouTuber, should parents support or oppose. As guest, Six Fingers Abyss isn't invited to guarantee this path succeeds, but to discuss how to find direction, how to self-learn, and how having no dream doesn't mean being immediately judged as behind. This moves his story out of the single "influencer inspiration" framework into the question of how Taiwan families imagine new work. [^4]

📝 **Curator's note:** Six Fingers Abyss's story doesn't just answer "how to get popular," but a harder question: when schools, families, and markets haven't yet prepared a name for a new profession, how does one explain their skills to others.

## Another Career Imagination for Taiwan Creators

Six Fingers Abyss's importance lies not only in making VFX videos, nor only in once winning an internet reputation award. He lets a path usually cut into different professions appear on one person: self-learner, freelancer, YouTuber, lecturer, published author, education platform founder, and tool developer.

All-English software takes time; content creation is affected by platform distribution; teamwork needs management; education products must also face the market. Six Fingers Abyss himself says in interviews that creators must first understand audience and topic, then decide how to revise works. These conditions show self-directed learning isn't excluding school, but putting feedback, trial-and-error, and market into the learning process. Writing his experience as "just persist and you'll succeed" instead erases the technical barriers and uncertainties he encountered.

His work method offers a set of observable steps. Start from a software interface, break down what you don't understand. Then organize freelance-leftover problems into teaching, turn audience questions into works, turn works into courses. Finally, courses return to new creative needs. This is a work sequence accumulated over years by a creator, and his way of turning personal skill into public resource. [^1] [^2] [^3]

In 2024 award reports, Six Fingers Abyss said he once gained subscribers rapidly due to short-form video and overseas markets. This reminds readers that platform changes can indeed rewrite a creator's scale, but also reminds us not to retroactively attribute all of one person's early technical accumulation to their later traffic code. [^5] [^6]

📝 **Curator's note:** What Taiwan internet culture deserves to preserve isn't just who's most popular. More worth preserving is someone turning originally hidden-behind-the-scenes capabilities into public resources others can learn, use, and continue rewriting.

## Family, School, and New Work Narratives

Six Fingers Abyss's story doesn't only happen in front of computers. Apple Podcasts places him in the parent-child dialogue "child wants to be a YouTuber, should parents support or oppose." This arrangement matters because it pulls internet creation from personal interest back to family decision: does the child need a word of support, or first need to understand skills, time, income, and risk? The program page can confirm discussion theme and guest identity, but can't provide a universal answer for all families. [^4]

The Nan Ying University interview puts the question back to school and career. The school page's recorded talk includes continuously creating value, finding positions to exert value in the present, and thoughts on facing market and entrepreneurship. If these words are only extracted as inspirational quotes, they lose their original work context. Put back into Six Fingers Abyss's experience, they refer to a repeated adjustment method: first break down software you can't do, then get feedback through works, finally decide which capabilities are worth teaching others. [^2]

📝 **Curator's note:** Influencer articles often write school as a place only useful after creators leave. Six Fingers Abyss's materials show schools, families, platforms, and markets are actually simultaneously naming new professions, just each using different language.

## Put Traffic Data Back in Their Dates

2024 media reports show another turning point in Six Fingers Abyss's career. Early materials focused on After Effects, self-learning, and teaching. Later reports discuss short-form video, Instagram follower growth, overseas markets, and single-video high view counts. This isn't before-after contradiction, but the result of the same imaging capability entering different platforms and facing different distribution logics. [^5] [^6] [^8]

However, traffic numbers alone can't explain a creator's value. Award reports record a specific year's selection; media-cited view counts also have their statistical time and narrative purpose. Taking "videos generate hundreds of millions of views annually" directly as long-term income or stable influence exceeds what sources support. A more cautious reading: Six Fingers Abyss once transplanted mature VFX capability to short-form video and overseas content, and this pivot was seen in 2024 public reports. [^5] [^6] [^8]

📝 **Curator's note:** What truly needs preserving isn't an eternally rising traffic curve, but how a creator re-explains their technique, audience, and work methods when platforms change.
