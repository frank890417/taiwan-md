> **30-Second Overview:** Shih Chun-rug (born 1944) was raised in a family of incense makers from Lukang. His father passed away from overwork when he was three, and his mother raised him by selling duck eggs, stationery, and patriotic lottery tickets. Rising through the ranks from an engineer in the Department of Electronic Engineering at National Chiao Tung University, he pooled NT$1 million with his wife and several partners in 1976 to found Acer, leading it to become one of the world's top five PC brands. He also mapped out the "Smiling Curve," a concept written into textbooks and later studied by international economists. He is also one of the few tech godfathers in Taiwan willing to stand before the camera and discuss his own failures: DeGe Semiconductor once set a record for the largest loss by a single company in Taiwan, and Acer lost over NT$20 billion in 2013 alone. Now in his eighties, with over ten stents in his heart, he is still active in venture capital and developing AI avatars. What he has cared about most throughout his life are the intangible things that take a long time to yield results: talent, brand, and what he calls "the Kingly Way."

September 1981, San Francisco, USA, at the venue of the 30th Western Electronic Show[^1].

A small computer with a plastic shell sat on a table. It had a peculiar appearance: when closed, it looked like an ordinary book that could be easily slipped onto a bookshelf[^2]. That year, the exhibition featured over 500 exhibitors from 14 countries[^3], and about 20 American distributors were gathered around this unassuming little device, eagerly inquiring about distribution rights[^4].

It was called "Micro-Professor I," produced by a Taiwanese brand named Multitech. The man who created it was Shih Chun-rug, who was 36 years old at the time. For many Taiwanese people in the fifth or sixth grade back then, this book-shaped machine was the first time they had ever touched a "computer designed and made by Taiwan itself."

## A Computer That Looks Like a Book When Closed

To truly understand the significance of this "book computer," one must look back in time.

In the 1970s, Taiwan was focused on OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturing). People assembled and processed goods for others to earn modest profits; few dared to dream of "creating their own brand and selling it to the world." Shih Chun-rug was precisely the kind of person who wanted to do just that. After earning his bachelor's degree in Electronic Engineering from National Chiao Tung University in 1968 and his master's degree in 1971[^5], he dove headfirst into the cutting edge of the era: microprocessors. He first developed Taiwan's first desktop calculator at Universal Electronics, then moved to Rongtai Electronics, where he led a team to create Taiwan's first handheld electronic calculator and the world's first electronic pen watch[^6].

In 1976, at the age of 31 (most media reported him as 32, following the traditional Taiwanese practice of calculating age by _xusui_), he teamed up with his wife, Yeh Tzu-hua, and partners including Tai Chung-ho, Lin Chia-ho, and Huang Shao-hua. With NT$1 million, they founded a small company originally named "Multitech"[^7]. He gave himself a very scholarly title: "The Gardener of Microprocessors"[^8]. A gardener is not a hegemon or a conqueror; his goal was to slowly plant and nurture an entire industry.

![Acer's 1981 "Micro-Professor I" (MPF-I), featuring a book-like design that can be placed on a bookshelf when closed](/article-images/people/mpf1-microprofessor-1981.webp)
_The 1981 "Micro-Professor I" (MPF-I). This Z80-based microcomputer training kit featured a book-shaped casing, becoming the first impression of "locally made computers" for many Taiwanese people._

The Micro-Professor I was the 1981 creation of this small company—a training kit designed to teach people about microprocessors. It went on to live an unexpectedly long life: in 1993, Acer sold this product line to a company in Southampton, UK, and that company continued to produce it in small batches until 2021[^9]. Spanning approximately 40 years from 1981 to 2021, this small computer made in Taiwan quietly sold in a foreign land for nearly half a century.

The 1981 WESCON exhibition was a microcosm of a turning point. A small factory that started in Hsinchu arrived with a book-shaped machine and was swarmed by foreign distributors asking about agency rights among more than 500 exhibitors—this was the first time the ambition of Taiwan's tech industry to "not be content with just being an OEM" was seen by the world. And standing behind that machine, Shih Chun-rug would spend the next forty years answering a question he himself had posed: just how far can a Taiwanese brand go?

## The Mother Who Sold Duck Eggs, and the Child Who Wanted to Build a Brand

To understand why Shih Chun-rug was so obsessed with "making his own things," one must return to Lukang.

He was born on December 18, 1944, in Lukang, Changhua, into a family of incense makers led by a woman named Shih Mei-yu. The roots of this incense shop can be traced back to an old shop opened by an ancestor in Jinjiang, Fujian, in 1774; his father, Shih Ch'i-shen, was the sixth generation of successors[^10]. However, this family background could not protect him as he grew up: his father passed away due to overwork when Shih Chun-rug was only three years old[^11].

The one who held the family together was his mother, Shih Chen Hsiu-lien. A native of Puli, Nantou, she resolutely refused to remarry after her husband's death, raising her son single-handedly. When the family divided its assets in 1953, she received a storefront. She made a living from that small shop selling duck eggs, stationery, groceries, and _aiguo jiangquan_ (patriotic lottery tickets), even taking on knitting work to earn a little extra[^12]. The _aiguo jiangquan_ were lotteries issued by the government during Taiwan's martial law period; it was common for small business owners of that era to supplement household income by selling them.

> 💡 **Did you know?** Shih Chun-rug later grew Acer into one of the world's top five PC brands, with its market value once exceeding NT$100 billion. However, according to some reports, the true seed money for his career was the startup capital his mother took from that small duck egg shop and lent to him and his wife. A widowed mother in Lukang selling duck eggs and lottery tickets sponsoring a son who would go on to break into the global computer market—this thread itself is the most concrete embodiment of the "self-made" generation in post-war Taiwan.

A child who grew up watching his mother meticulously manage every penny chose, upon reaching adulthood, the least meticulous path: building a brand. Doing OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturing) is stable—you make money as soon as you take an order. Building a brand requires massive investments in R&D, marketing, and time, with no guarantee of success. Shih Chun-rug insisted on the latter. He often said that Taiwan "does not lack talent, only stages"[^13], and what he wanted to build was that very stage.

Acer grew steadily. It went public in 1987 and officially changed its name to Acer[^14]. By the 1990s, it had become a globally recognized PC brand: revenue surpassed NT$50 billion in 1993 and NT$150 billion in 1995, at one point climbing to become the seventh-largest PC manufacturer in the world[^15]. A child emerging from a family that started from a small factory in Hsinchu and was sustained by a mother selling duck eggs truly placed Taiwan's name on the rankings of the global computer market.

## A Curve Thought of for Thirty Years and Still Being Refined

What Shih Chun-rug is most famous for is actually a curve.

In 1992, during Acer's first major restructuring, Shih Chun-rug proposed the "Smiling Curve" [^16]. The concept was simple: lay out the upstream, midstream, and downstream of an industry on a horizontal axis—R&D and patents on the left, assembly and manufacturing in the middle, and branding and marketing on the right; the vertical axis represents the level of added value. When drawn, the middle is low and the two ends are high, resembling a smiling face. His official explanation was as follows:

> "In 1992, when Acer was undergoing its first reorganization, the 'Smiling Curve' allowed us to understand where added value was distributed. At that time, I used it to communicate with our global colleagues, explaining that assembly had become the lowest value-added part of the computer industry. I persuaded Acer employees that we should abandon assembly in Taiwan and concentrate our efforts on specialized fields with higher added value." [^17]

This curve was originally just a strategic metaphor he proposed in his own book to persuade the company to undergo internal transformation. It was not a peer-reviewed academic theory; there was no original paper and no statistical testing. Interestingly, however, its subsequent life far exceeded that of a mere business slogan: entering the 2000s, international research on Global Value Chains (GVC) began to seriously adopt it as an analytical framework, with economic journals in Oxford and Canada successively publishing papers "measuring the smiling curve" and "re-testing the smiling curve" [^18]. A line drawn by intuition from an entrepreneur who started as an engineer became a subject of constant debate and refinement within the international field of economics.

Precisely because it was large and influential enough, the discussions surrounding it never ceased. There is a counter-argument known as the "Musashi Curve": in 2004, Suehiro Nakamura of Sony's Nakamura Institute in Japan investigated 400 Japanese manufacturing companies and argued that the highest profits actually lay in the middle "manufacturing" segment, which is the exact opposite of the smiling curve [^19]. There has also been criticism within Taiwan; in 2014, Chan Yi-chien of Chih-Jung Venture Capital wrote that Taiwan had misused the smiling curve, getting stuck in "design, development, and channel management," while failing to grasp the true "meridians" of branding: marketing and data [^20]. By the end of 2025, a commentary in _Asia Times_ even singled out Shih Chun-rug and Acer, using the economic concept of "Dutch Disease" to argue that outsourced manufacturing is actually the "most difficult, technically demanding, and hardest to replicate" segment of the entire value chain [^21].

There is a common point of comparison often discussed here: what TSMC does is precisely the "middle manufacturing" categorized as having the lowest added value in the smiling curve, yet it has grown into Taiwan's "Protector of the Nation" (a semiconductor giant) and one of the world's most valuable semiconductor companies. If one places TSMC on that curve, it falls into the lowest middle section—this is one way to read it, and a very natural question arises: does that curve need an update? (It should be noted that the framework "TSMC is a counter-example to the smiling curve" is an observational perspective, not a conclusion proposed by any specific scholar or paper.)

The first person to respond positively to this question was actually Shih Chun-rug himself. On the official website of the Chih-Jung Foundation, he personally added a crucial clarification:

> "Do not misunderstand the 'Smiling Curve' as an instruction to abandon manufacturing. Although the added value is relatively lower, the scale of manufacturing is large, and its accumulation still yields benefits; the concern is merely oversupply." [^22]

He even stated that misinterpreting the smiling curve as "do not manufacture" might be "one of the reasons for the massive outflow of production industries in Taiwan over the past 20 years" [^23]. This was his way of correcting a misinterpretation he feared most: he never called for Taiwan to abandon manufacturing; he only reminded everyone not to get stuck in the segment with the least bargaining power.

![Diagram of the Smiling Curve: The horizontal axis from left to right is R&D, manufacturing, and marketing; the vertical axis is added value; the curve forms a smiling face shape with a low middle and high ends](/article-images/people/smiling-curve-1992.webp)
_The appearance of the Smiling Curve: R&D on the left, manufacturing in the middle, and marketing on the right, with added value on the vertical axis. Low in the middle and high at both ends, like a smiling face—this is a diagram Shih Chun-rug drew for Acer employees in 1992, which was later used by international economists for research._

By 2019, at age 75, he was still modifying this curve. During his New Year's greetings at home, he proposed the "New Smiling Curve Six-Dimensional View," expanding the original single-line framework into three axes: upstream/midstream/downstream, added value, and field/sector, plus three dimensions: time, tangible/intangible, and direct/indirect [^24]. This new version is so complex that he admitted himself it is "very hard to draw, and I haven't drawn it yet" [^25]. There is a moving contrast here: the line in 1992 could be explained in a single stroke; the things he has realized thirty years later are so difficult they can barely be drawn.

Beyond that curve, in his later years, Shih Chun-rug planted another even larger tree called the "Kingly Way" (_Wangdao_). He defined it as "the way of leading organizations large and small," not the way of an emperor; its core consists of three things: creating value, balancing interests, and sustainable management [^26]. The most central set of concepts under the Kingly Way is what he repeatedly discusses: "explicit value" and "implicit value." Explicit value is tangible, direct, and present—to put it bluntly, whether you are making money; implicit value is intangible, indirect, and future-oriented—things like brand, talent, culture, and trust, which are things that only show their results much later [^27]. He often laments, "the whole of Taiwanese society cares too much about explicit value" [^28].

From a personal computer to a smiling curve, and then to an entire philosophy of the Kingly Way, the shape of Shih Chun-rug has gradually transformed. He began as an engineer making computers, became an entrepreneur, and finally, grew into a thinker who is constantly questioning: "What exactly is value?"

## The First Member of the 5-Billion Club

If one were to speak only of brands, theories, or philosophy, this man would seem too smooth and too perfect. But what truly makes Shih Chun-rug remembered and respected by the people of Taiwan is quite the opposite: he is one of the few tech godfathers in Taiwan willing to step forward and publicly discuss his own failures.

And his failures were not small at all.

In 1989, Acer entered into a joint venture with the U.S.-based Texas Instruments to establish DeGe Semiconductor, a company specializing in DRAM memory[^29]. This move was poorly timed. The DRAM industry is characterized by intense cyclicality and frequent oversupply; DeGe hit a market crash, losing over NT$5 billion in both 1997 and 1998, totaling nearly NT$10 billion over those two years. In 1997 alone, DeGe set the record for the "highest loss by a single company" in Taiwan[^30]. Ultimately, Acer had to accept the loss and sell DeGe to TSMC: in 1999, TSMC first acquired a 30% stake for approximately NT$5.47 billion, and in July of the following year, the entire company was merged into TSMC through a stock swap, marking the end of DeGe's history[^31].

How does Shih Chun-rug discuss this period? He does not evade it; instead, he uses it for self-deprecation. He said:

> "Back when DeGe lost 5 billion, the media called me the '5-Billion Club'... and I was always the first one to break into it."[^32]

When a corporation sets records for losses, he describes himself as the "first member of the club," with a tone of rare composure. When people criticized him online at the time, his response was: "Back then, someone on the internet called me a pig; I said pigs are actually quite smart."[^33] From the lesson of DeGe, he later distilled a frequently cited maxim: "To fight repeatedly despite defeat is a great spirit, but do not fight battles you cannot afford to lose."[^34]

DeGe was not the only pitfall. When Shih Chun-rug retired at age 60 in 2004, he handed Acer over to the Italian Gianfranco Lanci. By aggressively pushing shipment volumes, Lanci once propelled Acer to become the world's second-largest PC brand[^35]. But driving volume came at a cost: European channels became clogged with unsold inventory, leading Acer to recognize a loss of approximately US$150 million in 2011[^36]. In March of that year, the board decided to part ways with Lanci, and as the founder and largest shareholder, Shih Chun-rug held the deciding votes in this showdown[^37]. Acer paid an exorbitant severance package of NT$1.284 billion, a figure that set a record in the PC industry at the time[^38]. For the full year of 2011, Acer lost NT$6.601 billion[^39].

![Shih Chun-rug attending a brand forum in 2007. He is regarded as one of the few godfather-level entrepreneurs in Taiwan willing to publicly discuss his failures](/article-images/people/stanshih-brandforum-2007.webp)
_In Taiwan, it is difficult to hear godfather-level entrepreneurs speak openly about their defeats. Shih Chun-rug is a rare exception—he lays his failures out in the sunlight and even treats them as a philosophy._

The true bottom came in 2013. The various Western brands that Acer had spent years acquiring through massive mergers and acquisitions (such as Gateway, Packard Bell, and eMachines) failed collectively: in the third quarter alone, the company recognized NT$9.943 billion in intangible asset impairment, losing NT$13.1 billion in a single quarter. Combined with inventory losses in the fourth quarter, the annual loss surged to NT$20.579 billion, making it Acer's worst year in 38 years[^40].

Facing these accumulated defeats, Shih Chun-rug said something in a 2022 interview that has since been repeatedly quoted:

> "I am the biggest loser (as he holds the most Acer shares), but I have never regretted it."[^41]

He even increased the figures. He stated that these losses were the "necessary tuition fees for Acer to become a top-tier global multinational," and even remarked, "I didn't just pay 20 billion; I paid hundreds of billions"[^42]. This "hundreds of billions" was his own rhetorical expression during the interview—no audit report has calculated it that way—but the composure of someone who could lose his fortune and still speak with a smile was real.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> In Taiwan's business culture, "failure" is usually something to be hidden—biographies of big bosses mostly write about their wins; defeats are either left unmentioned or blamed on the economy or subordinates. Shih Chun-rug did the opposite: he laid out his defeats one by one in the sunlight and personally refined them into a philosophy. What commands respect is that he demonstrates something very rare: treating "admitting defeat" as a capability—a capability used to adjust and eventually win. He once said, "My personality makes it easy for me to admit defeat; if something is wrong, I will adjust immediately." In an environment accustomed to "saving face" at all costs, this level of composure is, in itself, a remarkable example of leadership.

## Returning to Clean Up His Own Mess

At the moment Acer hit rock bottom in 2013, Shih Chun-rug could have easily walked away.

He had long since retired. He was 68 years old that year, having been retired for nearly a decade; he had already had several stents placed in his heart, and he could have lived out the rest of his life with dignity. His philanthropic work, his venture capital investments, and his "Kingly Way" philosophy were all on track. Acer's NT$20.5 billion loss was a matter for his successors, not him.

However, in November of that year, Chairman Wang Chen-tang and General Manager Weng Chien-jen both resigned to take responsibility for the company's operations[^43]. Shih Chun-rug made a decision that surprised many: he returned to the fold. On November 21, he reassumed the chairmanship and concurrently took on the role of President, launching Acer's third transformation[^44]. A founder who could have retired with his reputation intact chose instead to go back and clean up a mess for which he held institutional responsibility.

He later offered this somewhat helpless self-deprecation regarding that period: "If I keep returning, am I expected to come out of my coffin?"[^45] He also said something even more profound: "Work can be outsourced, but responsibility cannot."[^46]

The first major task he undertook upon his return was finding the right successor. In 2014, he recruited Chen Chun-sheng from TSMC to serve as CEO. Rather than following Lanchi's old path of fighting for market share through price wars, Chen led Acer toward e-sports (Predator, Nitro) and educational Chromebooks, while also establishing a dedicated R&D unit[^47]. This pivot pulled Acer back from the depths. By 2021, Acer's revenue climbed back above NT$300 billion, with a net profit after tax of NT$10.8 billion and earnings per share of NT$3.63—all hitting recent highs. The group's market value also rose from over NT$50 billion before Chen Chun-sheng took office to nearly NT$100 billion[^48].

It is worth noting that Shih Chun-rug adhered to one principle throughout: passing leadership to the talented, not to his children. From Lanchi and Wang Chen-tang to Chen Chun-sheng, Acer's management leadership has been handed over three times, and each time it went to a professional manager rather than his own son[^49]. His eldest son, Shih Hsuan-hui, actually joined the company in 2011 following Acer's acquisition of iGware and was formally appointed as a director in 2019. However, that position was defined as a "shareholder supervisory seat representing the family" rather than management rights; the baton of the CEO remained in the hands of professional managers like Chen Chun-sheng[^50]. While outsiders hold varying views on how to balance passing management to the talented and passing equity seats to one's children, Shih Chun-rug himself was very clear: his son joined the board to "become a long-term and stable supporting force for the company's professional management team"[^51].

## Invisible Value

The places where Shih Chun-rug truly placed his bets throughout his life were not the figures appearing in financial reports, but rather the "invisible" things he often spoke of.

The most concrete of these is talent. He frequently said: "My greatest contribution to Taiwan is providing a stage and training many talents."[^52] This was no mere platitude. In Taiwan's early years, almost no company was willing to spend money on employee training because those who were well-trained would often turn around, start their own businesses, and become competitors. But Shih Chun-rug's attitude was: "Once employees finish their training, they will set up their own doors and become competitors, but I don't care."[^53] As a result, Acer became Taiwan's technology industry's famous "Whampoa Military Academy for talent"; waves of people brought out by Acer later dispersed throughout Taiwan's entire electronics industry, spreading their influence far and wide.

Another invisible area is the arts. This man who built computers immersed himself in Taiwan's cultural circles in his later years: from 2011 to 2016, he served as the chairman of the National Culture and Arts Foundation[^54]; starting in 2021, he took over as the chairman of the Cloud Gate Dance Theatre Foundation[^55]; he is also the honorary chairman of the Taiwan Premium Brand Association and the president of the Wan Sheng Orchestra Supporters Association[^56]. His explanation for this reflects his worldview: "Culture and art create implicit future value; I want to make this value more substantially visible to the public."[^57]

And when discussing "invisible value," one cannot avoid his relationship with TSMC. In 2000, Morris Chang invited Shih Chun-rug to serve as an independent director of TSMC. He served for 21 years until stepping down in 2021, during which time he also served as the chairman of TSMC's remuneration committee[^58]. Most of the staggering number of TSMC shares he held were obtained through stock swaps when DeGe Semiconductor was sold to TSMC[^59]. At a public event in early 2026, when someone asked him exactly how many TSMC shares he owned, his answer was characteristically himself: "So many that even I don't know how many I have."[^60]

However, he never treated these shares as assets for speculation. He said: "I feel that stocks are like real estate; I don't trade stocks, I just take the dividends and hold them long-term."[^61] This was not just talk. In 2021, he donated 500 TSMC shares worth five years of cash dividends to National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, explicitly stating that he was only donating the dividends and not the principal—in his words, "not touching the 'mother money'"[^62]. Even in his donations, he was practicing his logic of "taking the implicit and leaving for the long term."

> 💡 **Did you know?** Despite spending a lifetime building a brand, Acer's market capitalization has never been considered massive. On the same trading day of July 6, 2026, TSMC's market cap was approximately NT$64.44 trillion, while Acer's was approximately NT$99.97 billion—a difference of over six hundred times[^63]. Interestingly, Shih Chun-rug never avoids this gap. He once said in English: "If we are talking about making money, we are not good as TSMC." But he immediately followed up with the second half: regarding contributions and influence on the high-tech industry, he believes Acer plays a very important role[^64]. He had long used his own framework of "explicit vs. implicit" to find an answer for this gap: making money is explicit, while talent and influence are implicit, and throughout his life, he has always bet on the latter.

He even put this choice bluntly in a single sentence: "If my lifelong ambition had been only to make money, I would be making much more money today than they are."[^65] There was no bitterness in those words, only a man who knew clearly what he was pursuing. What he wanted was never the most beautiful number on a financial report.

## An Avatar Named Adan

In 2026, Shih Chun-rug is 81 years old.

His body has already been through a great deal: he has over ten stents in his heart and has survived myocardial infarction, strokes, and two critical medical emergencies[^65b]. Yet, he has absolutely no intention of stepping down. He told reporters quite bluntly: "Currently, I am enjoying life. Although it might look like I face many challenges to others, a life without challenges is boring."[^66] He even joked half-seriously that, thanks to AI technology, he is confident he can live to be 120[^67].

One of the most unique works of his later years is that he "made" himself: an AI avatar named "Adan."

His team fed all of his Chinese and English books and articles into a knowledge base. Shih Chun-rug himself personally participated in several training Q&A sessions to create an AI version of himself capable of answering questions. In January 2026, Adan officially launched on the GPT Store, available for free worldwide[^68]. He even released a new book, 《領導人的覺醒時刻：Stan 哥與 AI 對話》 (_The Leader's Moment of Awakening: Conversations with Brother Stan_); ninety percent of its content was written by that AI avatar. It includes 30 Q&As, one of which asks: "How do you want others to remember you?"[^69]

Reflecting on the meaning of this is quite moving. A man who spent his entire life talking about "implicit value," "intangibility," and "the future"—those invisible things that take a long time to calculate—has, in the end, turned himself into an invisible existence that can continue indefinitely. When the engineer who made the _Micro-Professor I_, the entrepreneur who proposed the "Smiling Curve," and the former chairman who lost hundreds of billions yet spoke with a smile are no longer here, an avatar named Adan will still be somewhere in the cloud, slowly answering questions from strangers, over and over again.

From that small computer in 1981—which looked like a closed book and was surrounded by American distributors asking questions—to this 2026 AI avatar placed in the GPT Store for anyone to query, forty-five years have passed. Forty-five years ago, he wanted the world to see a computer made in Taiwan; forty-five years later, he wants the world to remember a certain way that Taiwanese people think.

He plans to "retire" a second time at age 85[^70], handing over his position as chairman of for-profit enterprises to continue focusing on philanthropy. But looking at him these past few years—traveling to Japan to present his new "Kingly Way" white paper, launching an AI avatar, and accepting interview after interview—that retirement date will likely be pushed back yet again.

The child who grew up in Lukang watching his mother sell duck eggs has, in the end, remained that same gardener who wants to plant an entire garden.
