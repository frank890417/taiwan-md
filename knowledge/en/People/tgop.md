> **30-second summary:** Starting in 2011 on YouTube, this group once turned the chemistry of seven performers into life quotes shared by Taiwanese audiences. In 2023, they announced a hiatus for the following year. The outside world first heard "disbandment," but what the members themselves said was rest, change, and a return to what's essential. This gap reveals the hardest question early Taiwanese celebrity groups faced: when a channel is simultaneously friends, a company, a stage, and a source of income, can feelings alone sustain long-term collaboration?

![Recording studio console and instruments, illustrating the production foundation needed behind internet group works. Photo by Intermediapost, CC BY-SA 3.0, source: Wikimedia Commons file page: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Intermediapost_Recording_Studio.jpg.](/article-images/people/intermediapost-recording-studio.webp)

In 2011, Showhand, Showplus, Mars, Dongzi, Yinsheng, Nick, and Shitou began posting videos on YouTube. A 2025 follow-up report still described their start as "posting on YouTube in 2011," noting that a year after their hiatus, they still ranked in the top five on a Taiwanese influencer chart cited by that report[^1]. This was not a channel suddenly boosted by algorithms, but a group of performers gradually converting rehearsals, division of labor, and the chemistry between friends into rhythms in front of the camera.

Their most recognizable works rarely required expensive sets. Waiters, roommates, family, and colleagues were all characters the audience already knew. In 2017, member Zheng Yinsheng said during a team production: "The reach rate was so low I couldn't eat" and "Even if we're on the verge of bankruptcy, we still work hard on the videos!"[^2]. The jokes on screen were light, but the costs behind the scenes were heavy.

## The Seven, First Turning the Channel Into a Stage

In 2011, YouTube didn't yet have today's clear creator job titles. Dongzi later recalled that after receiving a call from Showplus, she joined the team and worked as a liaison, agent, producer, publicist, company administrator, and HR. Free Times cited her social media post, writing that she wasn't only in front of the camera but also managed how a small production team operated[^3].

This detail is crucial. What the audience sees is a group of people on the same screen, but behind the scenes, actors, screenwriters, producers, agents, and administrators all existed simultaneously. The channel was like both a theater troupe and a company. Every time they said "let's all film together," someone had to schedule time, book locations, negotiate collaborations, handle personnel, and divide revenue.

A 2024 TVBS report described the channel's scale with 3.57 million subscribers and 336 videos, listing seven members. The report also revisited the period when _Chao Hao Fan Chao Series_ gained attention in 2016, noting that the members once prioritized making good content before revenue sharing[^4]. This made "together" not just an emotional slogan but a work method of deferring personal compensation.

The problem is, a work method can sustain things for a while but doesn't necessarily sustain everyone's life. The same TVBS report mentioned that the market once rumored a commercial video fee of 1.3 million yuan, but after splitting among seven people, each got less than 200,000 yuan. This figure should be understood as the distribution scenario described in the report, not an estimate of the channel's annual revenue[^4].

## The Cost of One Video, Hidden Behind Laughter

![A cameraman using a Steadicam, illustrating the often-overlooked on-site work in group videos. Photo by Fryfilm2000, public domain, source: Wikimedia Commons file page: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:John_E_Fry_Steadicam_Operator_UK.jpg.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/16/John_E_Fry_Steadicam_Operator_UK.jpg)

In July 2021, after a partial relaxation of restrictions, this group gathered for the first time to film. Free Times reported that actors and crew wore masks throughout, with Yinsheng and Showhand even wearing goggles. Everyone tested negative before filming, following the Ministry of Culture's epidemic prevention guidelines[^5]. The team told the camera: "We can still film under full epidemic prevention! Nothing can stop us (should be fine)."[^5]

That "should be fine" sounds just like their tone, reflecting the reality of the creator industry. Works must maintain a light tone, but the set requires adding one procedure after another. The epidemic made group filming harder, yet the team still tried to preserve the feeling of "everyone on the same screen."

In 2022, Showhand and Showplus announced a temporary pause on posting after their father had a stroke during a livestream, first dealing with family matters. Free Times recorded that the two later updated that their father had regained consciousness, writing: "Really, really thankful—having you guys is great."[^6] The channel here was not just a place to release works but also a public space where members explained life changes to their audience.

## The Summer of "It's Time to Change"

In August 2023, Showhand announced through the official Facebook page of "These People TGOP" that after months of discussion, the team decided to take a break at an appropriate time next year. Yahoo! News cited the announcement and specifically preserved the team's reminder to the outside world: a hiatus does not mean disbandment, nor does it mean no more works will come[^7].

Showhand said in the announcement: "Our group has decided it's time to take a rest. Next year, at an appropriate time, we'll pause the 'These People' channel."[^7] He also said this decision hopes to move everyone in a better direction, returning to a pure and close "heart."[^7]

Here is the first contrast. The outside world easily translates "stopping updates" as "disbandment," but the team repeatedly asked the media to use more precise wording. To them, a hiatus was not a sensational ending but a way to temporarily put the channel back from a daily-running machine to a place where it could be rethought.

Several months later, the media began asking about the real reasons. In January 2024, Initium Media reported that producer Shitou discussed the hiatus on his "Makers" channel. He said: "Not continuing means we got tired of it—personally and as a group, everyone was exhausted!" The same report also quoted him saying "the company had operational issues"[^8].

These two statements cannot be compressed into one answer. Burnout is a human state, operational issues are a company state. They can drive each other but are not the same thing. Shitou also mentioned the possibility of releasing an album or moving toward the entertainment industry, but facing the short-video market, they felt "we want to move but can't afford to"[^8].

ETtoday's coverage of the same interview preserved the phrases "it must be about money" and "the company had operational issues," and recorded that members had discussed repeatedly, eventually forming the decision to pause from the idea of "wanting to take a rest."[^9] The common thread among different media was not announcing a single truth for the group but letting readers see that the hiatus involved money, burnout, company, platform, and the next step all at once.

## After the Last Video, What Remains

At the end of January 2024, this group broadcast their final project. On February 15, Dongzi posted a group photo, reviewing her journey from liaison, agent, producer to HR, writing: "All along, 'These People' was my sky, my everything. Watching 'These People' grow strong, I had to grow up too."[^3]

She also said: "A hiatus doesn't mean an end, because we're all still making our way in this circle!"[^3] This statement didn't guarantee the channel would definitely return, but left a boundary for the shared work experience: the channel can stop, but the members' lives won't all hit pause.

In March 2025, Free Times followed up on the state a year after the hiatus, reporting that Xiaogong had established a personal studio, mainly filming short videos. This split was symbolic. The seven once put all their energy into one channel; after the hiatus, creative energy began returning to individual people[^1].

This also shows why "These People" deserves to be written as a character story, not just placed in an influencer ranking. Rankings leave behind positions; TGOP leaves behind a working memory of Taiwan's internet culture: a few people without traditional TV fixed time slots found their own actors, wrote their own scenarios, handled their own companies, and then let the audience bring the lines from the videos back to classrooms, offices, and homes.

## The Platform Changed, and So Did the Group's Time

Early videos from this group relied on appearing together. The humor was built on members knowing each other well, and the audience thus remembered each person's expression, voice, and position. This model had the advantage of high recognition but the disadvantage that every project required scheduling multiple people at once. As long as someone had drama, music, family, or personal channel work, what seemed like a simple shoot turned into a scheduling negotiation.

In an interview, Shitou mentioned that the team once discussed releasing an album or moving toward the entertainment industry but worried about costs and market changes. This shows how platform shifts changed content organization. Short videos required faster responses and higher update flexibility, making long-term role-playing and group chemistry-based production methods face new rhythms. ETtoday summarized this state as "wanting to move but can't afford to"—it's not an industry term but the hesitation of a group facing a new market[^9].

If one person films, changing direction only requires adjusting their own work. If seven people film together, changing direction affects everyone's income, time, and identity. This is the hidden cost of group channels. The audience only sees updates or pauses; members face the reality that everyone has to reschedule their work.

### Revenue Sharing Is Not Just a Math Problem

The most noteworthy part of TVBS's report about the 1.3 million yuan commercial video and the seven-person distribution scenario was not the amount itself but how it let the audience see that the group's income had to pass through division of labor. Actors, producers, agents, administrators, and company expenses might all occur in the same collaboration. As works grew bigger, the rules for distribution had to become clearer, otherwise "doing it together" would slowly turn into "bearing it together."[^4]

Dongzi's review of handling liaison, production, public relations, and HR shows that the group wasn't complete just by having seven people in front of the camera. The more behind-the-scenes work, the more systems were needed. She mentioned in her pause article that as company staff gradually increased, many things had to start being regulated by systems[^3]. Looking at this recollection alongside Shitou's comments about company operational issues is enough to understand why the pause wasn't just creative burnout.

### The Audience's Memory Lives Within the Group

When family events occurred in 2022, Showhand and Showplus first paused posting. This decision would be remembered by the audience because the channel had long kept members' work and lives visible. The audience supported them, not just supporting a brand but the individuals already recognized in front of the camera. This sense of closeness could form long-term loyalty but also increased the public pressure on members facing private changes[^6].

Dongzi said "These People was my sky, my everything"—a line from a long-time worker reflecting on life. It's moving but also heavy. When a group carries work, friendship, growth, and the opportunity to be seen, a pause naturally feels like losing a life anchor. She then said everyone would continue forward on their own journeys, perfectly explaining that the end or pause of a group should not be simplified to who left and who stayed.

## Why the Audience Remembers These Seven

One easily underestimated design of this group's content was that it turned the relationships Taiwanese audiences encounter every day into recognizable character arrangements. How family members reply, how colleagues shift responsibility, how waiters face customers, who friends look at first in awkward moments—these scenarios didn't need a grand worldview but required actors to be very familiar with tone, pauses, and reactions to each other. When seven people were on screen together, the punchlines were often not single lines but who caught whose mistake first.

This performance style made the audience feel like "I know them." The 2017 report mentioned that even in economic hardship, the team still prioritized their work, showing that their recognition didn't come only from star-like individual exposure but from the rhythm accumulated through fixed collaboration[^2]. In 2021, during epidemic filming, the team still wanted to maintain the feeling of multiple people on screen, concretizing the production cost of this rhythm[^5].

Because roles and members were tightly bound, once the group paused, the audience lost not just an update column but a set of fixed viewing relationships. Some were used to watching Showhand and Showplus's interactions; others remembered Mars, Dongzi, Yinsheng, Nick, and Shitou's positions in different scenarios. These memories didn't necessarily require the seven to reunite but would make any solo work carry the shadow of the original channel.

When creator groups reach this stage, the real question is no longer whether they can make another viral video but whether each person can establish new work anchors outside of shared memory. The 2025 report recording Xiaogong establishing a personal studio is a concrete example of this transition[^1]. The group's remaining abilities didn't disappear but dispersed from one set to different studios, different channels, and different performance forms.

## Reunderstanding "Together"

This group's story had no clean climax. They started filming in 2011, gained more attention through _Chao Hao Fan Chao Series_ in 2016, tried to gather for filming under epidemic restrictions in 2021, paused due to family events in 2022, announced a hiatus for the next year in 2023, and completed their final project in 2024. Each node pushed "together" forward and made its costs clearer.

They didn't stop because they no longer had an audience. At least according to the chart cited in the 2025 follow-up report, they still had recognition and rankings a year after the hiatus[^1]. The truly hard-to-answer question was how a channel could simultaneously take care of seven people's creative directions, income needs, life changes, and the next platform.

Therefore, a pause looks like disappearance but is actually closer to splitting apart. The identities of channel, company, friends, actors, producers, and individual creators were re-separated, and each person had to decide what to take with them and acknowledge that some things couldn't be maintained by chemistry alone.

Showhand said in the 2023 announcement that he hoped the group would "return to everyone in a different way"[^7]. The most worth preserving in this statement is not what it predicted but that it acknowledged the form had to change. The seven who rose to fame together didn't necessarily need the same channel to prove they were once together.

The last object this group left for Taiwan's internet culture was not a video or the number 3.57 million, but the screen where they once appeared together. It shows that what's hardest to maintain is never the heat but letting different people, under the same name, still have reasons to move forward together.
