> **30 Second Overview:** Born in 1988, Wu Chia-Wei was an amateur before playing "Brother Jie." He appeared as a guest actor in the online group "This Group" and was cast in a short educational film by the Ministry of Education—which featured a "perpetrator"—after receiving a casting notice from Zhan Rongchen. _If I Had Known Boys Could Be Sexually Assaulted Too_, uploaded on June 16, 2013, became a sensation, achieving nearly 800,000 views in July, setting a record for Taiwanese government public service announcements[^1]. He was shouted at by passersby as "No Brother Jie" to the point of wanting to flee and initially rejected the role, yet ten years later, he starred in Taiwan's first meme musical featuring "Brother Jie"[^4].
> In an anti-sexual assault short film, it is not the victim who becomes the most famous, but the perpetrator.

In 2012, the Ministry of Education of the Republic of China commissioned the National Educational Research Institute to produce a 17-minute and 31-second gender equality education public service announcement titled _If I Had Known Boys Could Be Sexually Assaulted Too_[^2]. The plot involves Awei, a male raised across generations who runs away from home, meeting a middle-aged man calling himself "Brother Jie" at an internet cafe. He is lured home with food and beer and is ultimately sexually assaulted[^2]. The film aims to remind society that boys can also be victims of sexual violence.

What people truly remember about the film was Brother Jie's line: "Awei, let Brother Jie see."

When Wu Chia-Wei played that role, he was not yet the YouTuber Ironbull. He later gained fame across the strait through the "Brother Jie" meme, accumulating over 300 million views[^4], and it took him nearly ten years to accept that he was "Brother Jie."

---

## Three Days: The Role of a Perpetrator

In 2012, Wu Chia-Wei's life goals had little connection to the public service announcement. He previously worked at haunted houses and did backstage work for stage plays; his last full-time job was in publishing for two years[^6]. At that time, his only acting experience was as a guest actor in the online group "This Group." Zhan Rongchen said the Ministry of Education was casting actors for a short public service announcement, and the entire group submitted resumes. Ironbull was selected to play Brother Jie[^5].

The casting process itself was full of surprises. He was originally cast as "the teacher," but the script was suddenly taken away. The director asked him if he wanted to take on the educational theme of "boys can also be victims of sexual assault," leaving him only three days to prepare[^6]. Upon hearing that he was playing a "perpetrator," he agreed without hesitation. At that time, no one foresaw what this video would spark.

The filming location was in Keelung, utilizing Warmwarm High School, Xinfeng Street, and Dingnei No. 15 Park[^3]. Wu Chia-Wei portrayed the middle-aged man—who seemed enthusiastic but harbored ulterior motives—in a way that was chilling, especially the line: "Awei, let Brother Jie see, come, come, let Brother Jie see"[^1].

This was likely not the outcome any gender equality education expert could have anticipated.

## Ten Years of Being Called "No Brother Jie"

The video was uploaded to YouTube on June 16, 2013, and nearly reached 800,000 views within a month, setting a record for Taiwanese government public service videos[^1]. The Ministry of Education probably did not expect that a film reminding people "boys can also be victims of sexual assault" would become the hottest meme material in Taiwan.

Lines such as "No Brother Jie," "Listen, let me see," "You are brave," "I think you don't understand at all," and "This Binbin is just inferior" were screenshotted, adapted, and turned into memes, becoming popular from Taiwan to the mainland. In 2017, the video sparked a new wave of excitement on bilibili; in 2019, it was even more prevalent, with a single video on bilibili reaching as many as 27 million views[^4]. Big data statistics show that in just one year, from November 2022 to October 2023, the "No Brother Jie" meme series generated 17,048 online mentions[^10]. As of June 2023, cumulative views on YouTube exceeded 3 million[^1].

For Wu Chia-Wei, this was not glory; it was a nuisance.

"No matter where I go, everyone calls me Brother Jie," he later recounted in an interview, saying he felt overwhelmed when people shouted "No Brother Jie" at him on the street, and even someone riding a motorcycle came up to shout it[^5]. Even more absurdly, some viewers used the threatening SMS phone number that Brother Jie sent to Awei to call his actual mobile phone; others showed up to ask him to "play Brother Jie one more time"[^3].

> **✦** "I actually regretted it at the time. I initially rejected the role of 'Brother Jie' because I am an actor, and I was afraid of being typecast or having my acting career limited."[^6]

He once heard people shouting "No Brother Jie" on the street but wasn't sure if they were calling him; he said, "Being laughed at requires courage. I didn't have that yet to handle everyone's gaze"[^5]. In 2018, he married flight attendant Tingting and continued to run the channel "IronbullTingTing" after marriage[^2].

The turning point came from a sentence spoken by Huang Licheng (Brother Ma Ji) to Chou Junwei (Little Brother Ma Ji) on a show: "It doesn't matter what others think of you; this is your past, and you must accept it to become better"[^5]. When Ironbull later recounted this story, he said that he decided to calmly accept being Brother Jie—"Later I realized that through this role, I could bridge the distance with the audience, so I accepted it calmly."[^7].

In 2021, he and Huang Yaowei were invited by the game company Garena to film an advertisement for the mobile game _Free Fire_, recreating a classic scene. "I never thought this meme could be commercialized like this."[^7].

## WanzaiGame: Playing Stubbornly

Another part of Ironbull's history is written in the map of Taiwan's early gaming streaming community.

In 2013, Wu Chia-Wei co-founded the gaming streaming group "WanzaiGame" with Huang Zhisen (Huasen), and Ironbull named the channel. They initially considered names like "Yu Xi Wang" or "Wan GAME," fearing plagiarism, so they changed it to "WanzaiGame," meaning "playing stubbornly"[^9]. The channel officially launched in 2014, with early members including Ironbull, Huasen, Sheng Jieshi, and Nick. They gained popularity through the series "Things Little Kids Do"[^9]. At that time, Taiwan's gaming streaming community was just starting out; the four members experimented with a very Taiwanese style of comedy between live streams and walkthrough videos using a humble setup—one phone per person and one account each.

Ironbull has a vivid memory of their first commercial collaboration. Around 2014, the three members worked on a Shell Coin promotional video for a total fee of five thousand NTD; after deducting prop costs, none received more than a thousand. "When we got paid, we thought, let's be bolder! Let's take a taxi to film this one!"[^6].

Sheng Jieshi and Nick eventually left the group due to personal plans and differing views on filming concepts. In 2019, the four members briefly reunited for a video, breaking rumors of internal discord[^8]. On February 9, 2021, Ironbull unexpectedly announced the dissolution of WanzaiGame, when the channel had 712,000 subscribers and accumulated 153 million views[^9].

In the year of dissolution, Ironbull considered leaving the online community. He thought about returning to a stable job and even considering becoming a construction vehicle operator, "feeling that driving big trucks was cool"[^6]. In the end, he discussed it with Tingting and agreed to run his personal channel for two more years; if it failed, he would look for a job. The audience still supported him, so he stayed[^6]. His current advice to new creators is very practical: first secure a stable living through a main job, then operate YouTube as a hobby, and do not invest large amounts of money in equipment from the start[^6].

## Ten Years as a Gamer Streamer with WanzaiGame

After the short film became popular, Wu Chia-Wei did not immediately become a full-time creator. What truly made him rely on "content creation" for a living was the gaming streaming channel "WanzaiGame," established at the end of 2014 with Huasen and others. Ironbull named the channel. They initially considered names like "Yu Xi Wang" or "Wan GAME," but finally chose a name with a strong Tâi-gí flavor[^6]. WanzaiGame was known for game walkthroughs and comedic streaming, becoming an early representative face of Taiwan's gaming streaming community alongside Sheng Jieshi and Nick. The channel peaked at 712,000 subscribers[^6].

On September 1, 2020, WanzaiGame unexpectedly announced its dissolution. At the time, the channel had 712,000 subscribers and over a thousand videos[^6]. There was no dramatic fallout behind the dissolution, but for Wu Chia-Wei, this loss was more tangible than "Brother Jie": at least the meme gave him something to do; after the channel disbanded, he had to figure things out again.

In those years, he tried many jobs. In an interview, he revealed that he considered becoming a construction vehicle operator, "feeling that driving big trucks was cool"[^6]. In 2021, he co-founded a channel with Sheng Jieshi called "Sheng Wanzai"; in 2023, he reformed "WanzaiGame2" with Junchen, Ashin, and Huasen, but the collaboration ended after only one year. Huasen later continued the brand in a new format, which was referred to by outsiders as "WanzaiGame 2.0"[^8]. For someone who had been trapped by memes, these years instead showed him something: outside of Brother Jie, Wu Chia-Wei was struggling through ordinary life.

## Memes Become a Common Language

The status of "No Brother Jie" in Taiwanese internet culture is no longer just a joke; it has become almost a dialect. Netizens use it to respond to almost any context: if a friend recommends a bad movie, you reply with "I think you don't understand at all"; someone brags about a new car, and the comment section automatically floods with "This Binbin is just inferior"[^11]. Analysis from Key Opinion Platforms pointed out that the 2021 wave of popularity came back to Taiwan from the mainland. At that time, Taiwanese netizens began fact-checking the filming locations in the video, confirming that the shooting sites were around Warmwarm High School and Dingnei No. 15 Park in Keelung. The act of "No Brother Jie" fact-checking itself became a form of internet subculture[^11].

The penetration power of this meme even seeped into mainstream media daily life. In March 2023, under a news post about double Mercedes vehicles, netizens commented with "I think you don't understand at all," causing the meme to peak in online mentions that month[^10]. Statistics from LINE TODAY and the big data observation site DailyView show that whenever social news features protagonists who are "preachy" or "showing off," the comment section automatically awakens Brother Jie, as if he were a standby character on the internet[^10]. Big data statistics show that in just one year, from November 2022 to October 2023, the "No Brother Jie" meme series generated 17,048 online mentions[^10]. As of June 2023, cumulative views on YouTube exceeded 3 million[^1]. A public service announcement produced by the government twelve years ago is still providing material for Taiwanese news comment sections—this long-tail buzz cannot be bought with any advertising budget.

Some scholars have also raised serious questions about the educational effectiveness of this film. Li Jia-lin from the Institute of Social and Cultural Studies at National Taiwan University criticized as early as 2013 that the video not only failed to achieve gender equality education goals but also reinforced stereotypes like "middle-aged men = sexual assault perpetrators," linked HIV patients with sexual assault victims, and suppressed youth sexual expression[^3].

From a meme studies perspective, the cross-strait dissemination path of "No Brother Jie" is textbook material. Around 2017, the video first fermented on bilibili; mainland Chinese netizens re-edited and re-sang it with simplified Chinese subtitles, turning the lines into a common meme library; starting in 2021, it went viral from the mainland back to Taiwan, forming a "export-to-domestic sale" meme cycle[^11]. A Taiwanese public service announcement produced in 2012 and released in 2013 completed an accidental cultural export and return through cross-strait online communities. The video also spawned popular phrases like "Let Me See," "Escaping Jie," and "No Escape from Jie" in the mainland[^15]. The official page of the National Educational Research Institute still retains this video, available for viewing under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 license[^2]. The government did not remove it; the internet made it into something else.

## Memes Take the Stage

From December 1 to 3, 2023, the New Taipei City Arts Center hosted a musical that no one thought would exist—_Hao You~ No Brother Jie!!_, promoted as "Taiwan's first meme musical"[^10]. Ironbull (Wu Chia-Wei) and Huang Yaowei reprised Brother Jie and Awei. Chang Changmian, who played Teacher Shuhui in the film, continued her role, and a new character was added by the scriptwriter[^4].

The origin of this play is complex. In 2018, Huang Yaowei, who had already become a theater actor, conceived the idea of adapting it into a musical but was stuck on funding[^4]. In 2021, YouTuber Xiaoweiba (Cheng Yuxiang) and Wu Jiru adapted the short film into a three-minute online musical, inviting Ironbull and Huang Yaowei to lend their voices, causing the video to go viral again[^4]. Lin Dahan, the founder of Shell, saw commercial potential and decided to invest. Huang Yaowei recommended the theater company "Cidian Creation Workshop," known for original Chinese musicals[^4]. The director Gao Tianheng and scriptwriter Wang Mutian (who was previously part of the Mandarin dubbing team for _The Simpsons_) wrote over a hundred meme-related lines in the first draft[^13].

Adapting a public institution's public service announcement requires passing through licensing. Gao Tianheng first obtained permission from the National Educational Research Institute: "The public sector is very willing to let us adapt it, but the premise is that the work must not violate good morals."[^13] The entire play centers on the "story behind the meme," using song and dance to weave together internet memes and subculture, featuring over ten songs[^13]. The musical brought background characters in the original film (convenience store clerks, park passersby), who had almost no lines, onto the stage, giving these decade-long supporting actors their own narrative for the first time.

![A worksheet from a 2012 Taiwanese gender equality education lecture on sexual harassment, sexual assault, and domestic violence—*If I Had Known Boys Could Be Sexually Assaulted Too* was a public service product under the Ministry of Education's gender equality education system during that era](/article-images/people/taiwan-gender-equality-worksheets-2012.webp)
_Cloud Network of Baozhong High School / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0_

The ambition of the stage version was not just restoration. The musical features fifteen songs in total, including an overture and a finale. The stage design recreates the original scenes using simple props and projection screens, and the program booklet is deliberately formatted like a social media page, giving the audience a feeling of "logging into a webpage" before entering the theater. New characters symbolizing online celebrities, such as "Yanzhi Nan" and "Lin Qinqinzi," were added, extending the meme's ten-year span into an overall observation of the internet ecosystem through plots involving live streaming, hacking, cyberbullying, and fraudulent inducement[^14].

![Musical audience seats—the familiarity of the audience with the meme lines is the market basis for *Hao You~ No Brother Jie!!* to go on stage](/article-images/people/wicked-lyric-theatre-audience-2023.webp)
_Christopher Sherlock / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0 (Illustrative)_

In March 2024, Qiu Yifeng, a resident critic from the National Performing Arts Center, reviewed the show: "The ensemble actors are physically fluid, and their singing ability is perfect; the overall performance is steady and sufficient; unfortunately, the lyrics were 'trimmed to fit the plot,' with no lyricist credited."[^14]. Director Gao Tianheng made a joke during the curtain call at Zhongshan Hall in Taichung that made the audience roar: "If you understand 60 percent, it means your internet addiction is quite serious; if you understand 90 percent, wow! Then you are truly hopeless!"[^14].

Scriptwriter Wang Mutian said that most of the stories behind memes carry a sad element, but when they become memes, they bring joy to others—this contrast is the core premise of the entire play[^13]. From February 24 to 25, 2024, the company toured Zhongshan Hall in Taichung, with ticket prices ranging from 300 to 2,300 NTD[^16]. The official website of Zhongshan Hall in Taichung precisely positioned this play: "Taiwan's first meme musical, an artistic creation collaboratively made by the internet generation." Memes are new poetry of the exhausted generation on the web, and the theater turns it back into live poetry[^16]. Director Gao Tianheng was previously known for works like _Kulu Life_ and _Jingniang Small Tavern_, and this marked his expansion into the musical theater field[^16]. In 2025, the company brought an upgraded version to the Globe Theater at the Taipei Performing Arts Center, inviting comedian He Long (from _He Long Night Show_) to participate. The production team listed on the OPENTIX official page included composer Xiaoweiba and Zhang Qingyan, choreographer Zhang Qingjia and Gu Hongshan, among more than ten people, and noted "Supported by | Ministry of Culture."[^17]. Huang Yaowei, who played the young victim in the film, is now a theater actor and has participated in the CCTV new media film _No Fire_[^3].

For Wu Chia-Wei, this performance was particularly meaningful. Ten years ago, he was an amateur, taking a chance for a public service announcement without knowing how the audience would view him; ten years later, he stood under the theater lights, with the people who watched his meme growing in the audience. The meme carved his name into Taiwanese internet history, and the musical returned that name to the stage—for an actor who feared being typecast for over a decade, this was the most dignified reconciliation.

## Memes Grow Into a Universe

The ten years of derivative works have elevated "Brother Jie" into a self-contained derived universe. Game companies used it in advertisements; Garena's _Free Fire_ invited Ironbull and Huang Yaowei to co-star in an ad short, directly incorporating the meme lines[^7]. YouTubers cut it into a three-minute online musical, causing another explosion by having him perform[^4]. The theater brought it onto the stage, becoming "Taiwan's first meme musical"[^4]. In March 2026, the mainland Chinese game team Loser Studio released the first-person horror game _No Escape from Jie_ on Steam for 66 NTD. Players were invited by Brother Jie to enter his house only to discover it was a scam; they had to continuously explore and evade his pursuit inside the house, finding refuge in a closet to survive[^15].

Advertisements, online music, theater, and horror games—the meme material from one public service announcement grew into four completely different media forms over ten years. For cultural researchers, this is a rare sample: a character evolving from a perpetrator in a Ministry of Education proposal to a common internet language, and finally becoming a consumable, investable, and performable cultural asset. Wu Chia-Wei is the only "origin" within this universe—the starting point for all derivative works, which cannot escape his amateur audition more than ten years ago.

## Tingting, Children, and IronbullTingting

On January 1, 2018, Wu Chia-Wei married flight attendant Tingting. After marriage, the couple ran the channel "IronbullTingting," documenting their daily life and parenting[^2]. As of December 2025, the channel had approximately 262,000 subscribers, 22.51 million views, and 254 works[^12]. The content ranged from couple's daily life to outings with children, showing audiences the family side of "Brother Jie" after shedding his role. Between 2025 and 2026, the focus shifted clearly to family: the series "Flying with Flight Attendant Tingting" concluded after episode ten because Tingting was pregnant. In an August 2025 video, he publicly revealed his status as a "rich second generation": Grandma owns land in Kaohsiung, and the whole row of houses belongs to the family[^18]; in July 2026, they filmed a special episode titled "Opening Love Letters from Ten Years Ago," revisiting their romance that started through flight attendants[^18]. Furthermore, he also established an official Bilibili channel called "Ironbull Jie" to actively manage this meme's origin[^12].

After his son was born, Wu Chia-Wei's life challenge advanced from "accepting Brother Jie" to "protecting the child." He admitted in an interview that his biggest concern is his three-year-old son being teased by peers as "Your dad is Brother Jie."[^5] This statement actually echoes his situation ten years ago: a role is a burden when it is yours, and traffic/fame when it is given to you by the audience; what he wants now is not to let that same gaze fall on his child. From rejection, to acceptance, to vigilance—this was the third lesson Brother Jie taught him, and the lesson this public service announcement never intended to teach.

## The Lesson the Public Service Announcement Didn't Finish: Memes Took Over

To be honest, the effectiveness of _If I Had Known Boys Could Be Sexually Assaulted Too_ as a gender equality education film is controversial. Li Jia-lin from the Institute of Social and Cultural Studies at National Taiwan University criticized in 2013 that the video not only failed to achieve gender equality education goals but also reinforced stereotypes like "middle-aged men = sexual assault perpetrators," linked HIV patients with sexual assault victims, and suppressed youth sexual expression[^3].

Yet, the video survived in a way completely outside of its original proposal. It became a common language: Taiwanese netizens used it for memes; young people on the mainland created derivative works on bilibili[^3]; game companies used it in advertisements[^7]; the theater brought it onto the stage[^4]; and even gaming platforms released an original horror game based on it[^15]. For "the actors" like Wu Chia-Wei and Huang Yaowei, the decade of meme popularity represented more than just traffic—Huang Yaowei said: "People discuss it because they like this character; I actually enjoy it now. The 'No Brother Jie' meme has become a part of my life."[^7].

And Wu Chia-Wei's ten-year timeline is clear: from rejection, to acceptance, to vigilance. After becoming a father, his greatest fear was not the meme fading away, but that it was too alive—so alive that he worried his son’s classmates might call him "Brother Jie" when he grew up.

The life force of memes often outlasts their original intent. The lesson the Ministry of Education wanted to teach, the students did not fully absorb, but at the moment Brother Jie said "Let Brother Jie see," more than ten years later, countless people still remember it, and it has grown into a "Brother Jie Universe" spanning advertisements, games, theater, and horror games[^15]. This perhaps reminds us: in Taiwan, the life force of a character depends not only on who they want to be, but also on who the audience decides them to be—and Wu Chia-Wei spent ten years learning to coexist with the audience's decision.
