# Yang Yung-wei

> **30-Second Overview:** Yang Yung-wei (born September 28, 1997), a Paiwan indigenous judoka. He won the silver medal in the men's 60kg category at the 2021 Tokyo Olympics, marking the first Olympic judo medal in Taiwan's history. In November of the same year, he rose to number one in the International Judo Federation world rankings, becoming Taiwan's first world number one in judo. His gold medal at the 2023 Hangzhou Asian Games proved his success was no flash in the pan.

July 24, 2021, Tokyo Budokan. When Yang Yung-wei lost by a narrow margin to Japan's Naohisa Takato in the men's 60kg judo final, the image of him gently rubbing the silver medal against his cheek spread across the world. In that moment, this 23-year-old Paiwan youth had rewritten the history of Taiwanese judo—it was the first Olympic judo medal in Taiwan's history.

## 1997: The Birth of a Paiwan Warrior

Yang Yung-wei was born on September 28, 1997, and is a proud member of the Paiwan people. Contrary to many reports mentioning "Taitung," he is actually from Taichung, but the warrior spirit of the indigenous peoples flows through his blood. There is a saying among the Paiwan: "A brave heart cannot be defeated," a phrase that perfectly encapsulates Yang's life in judo.

His introduction to judo began in elementary school. Unlike many athletes whose journeys are defined by "natural talent," Yang's path was shaped by coincidence. At the time, his school PE teacher noticed that although he was small in stature, he possessed incredible explosive power and suggested he try combat sports.

"I had no idea what judo even was at first," Yang later recalled. "I just knew it involved throwing people around, and it felt fun." This "fun" beginning unexpectedly opened a new chapter for Taiwanese judo.

## 2017: Making a Name in the Asian Junior Championships

The year 2017 was when Yang Yung-wei truly began to emerge in the judo world. That year, at just 19 years old, he won Taiwan's first Asian Junior Judo Championship gold medal in the 60kg category.

This junior gold medal was significant. Taiwanese judo has long been at a second or third-tier level in Asia; to defeat athletes from judo powerhouses like Japan, South Korea, and Mongolia at the youth level requires exceptional technique and mental fortitude.

Yang's technical characteristics were first displayed during the Asian Junior Championships: his _uchimata_ (inner thigh throw) was precise, powerful, and executed with extreme speed. More importantly, he demonstrated competitive intelligence beyond his years—knowing when to attack, when to defend, and when to exhaust his opponent's stamina.

## 2021: From Tokyo Silver to World Number One

For Yang Yung-wei, the Tokyo Olympics were "the first Olympics, and unforgettable for a lifetime." As the first athlete in Taiwan's judo history to reach an Olympic final, the pressure was immense.

His opponent in the July 24 final, Naohisa Takato, was a mainstay of the Japanese national team competing on home soil. The match was exceptionally intense; Yang created multiple attacking opportunities, but they were all neutralized by the experienced Takato. Ultimately, Yang lost by a slim margin in golden score (overtime).

"I wanted the gold medal," Yang choked back tears before the cameras after the match, a sentiment that moved all of Taiwan. Yet, the historical significance of this silver medal was irreplaceable—Taiwan judo finally had an Olympic medal.

Even more surprising was that in November 2021, when the International Judo Federation released the men's 60kg rankings, Yang Yung-wei had ascended to number one in the world. This marked the first time a Taiwanese athlete reached the top of the world rankings in judo, proving that his Tokyo Olympic silver was no accident.

## 2023: Proving His Strength at the Hangzhou Asian Games

"One good result might be luck; consistent good results are strength." Yang Yung-wei proved this with his gold medal at the 2023 Hangzhou Asian Games.

In the men's 60kg final at the Hangzhou Asian Games, Yang faced a Kazakhstani opponent. This time, he did not let the gold slip away, winning the championship with a clear advantage. This Asian Games gold was not just an addition to his medal collection; more importantly, it represented a psychological breakthrough—proving he has the ability to seize opportunities at critical moments.

Notably, the technique Yang displayed in Hangzhou was more mature than during the Tokyo Olympics. His groundwork (_ne-waza_) had improved significantly, and his tactical application was more flexible. This capacity for continuous improvement is a vital trait of elite athletes.

## Technical Analysis: The "Judo Artist's" Signature _Uchimata_

Yang Yung-wei's judo style can be described as "technical." He is not a power-based athlete, but rather a "judo artist" who wins through technique.

His signature move is the _uchimata_, which has proven effective time and again on the international stage. The _uchimata_ requires exquisite timing—attack too early and the opponent will see it coming; attack too late and you miss the opportunity. Yang's mastery of timing is nearly perfect, often completing throws at the exact moment his opponent is most vulnerable.

In addition to his standing techniques, Yang's groundwork is quite solid. In judo matches, if a standing technique fails to decide the outcome, the fight moves to the ground. Yang's pinning and _shime-waza_ (strangling techniques) are of a high standard, allowing him to maintain an advantage in ground combat.

## "Judo Ears": The Professional Athlete's Medal

Attentive viewers may notice that Yang Yung-wei's ears have a unique shape—this is the "occupational hazard" of judoka: "judo ears" (also known as cauliflower ear).

Long-term judo training leads to repeated friction and impact on the ears, causing the cartilage to deform. To an ordinary person, this might look like a defect, but for a judoka, it is a symbol of honor—proof of the sweat and effort poured into training.

Yang never hides his judo ears; instead, he takes pride in them. "These are my medals," he says, "reminding me of how much I have sacrificed for my dream."

## The Japanese Fan Phenomenon: Cross-Border Charm

After the Tokyo Olympics, Yang Yung-wei became unexpectedly popular in Japan. The term "Taiwanese athlete" even became a trending topic on Japanese Twitter, as countless Japanese netizens were won over by his skill and humble attitude.

This phenomenon is fascinating: in Japan, the birthplace of judo, a Taiwanese athlete has garnered a massive following. One Japanese netizen commented: "Yang's judo is beautiful; it has the spirit of _budo_," while another wrote: "I hope he keeps working hard; I want to see him win gold next time."

Yang's Instagram follower count skyrocketed after the Tokyo Olympics, with a significant proportion being Japanese fans. He occasionally posts in Japanese to show respect for Japanese judo culture, an attitude that has earned him even more international support.

## Daily Training: The Cost of Sacrificing Youth

Becoming a world-class athlete requires a price most people cannot imagine. Yang's day begins at 6:00 AM and ends after training at 8:00 PM. While his peers are out shopping, singing KTV, or dating, he is sweating in the _dojo_.

"I have missed out on much of my youth," Yang admitted, "but I have no regrets. Judo has given me so much more."

This sacrifice involves not just time, but also physical health. Judo training is extremely grueling, and injuries are a daily occurrence. Yang's body bears countless scars, each one a mark of his training.

## The Social Significance of Indigenous Athletes

As a successful indigenous athlete, Yang Yung-wei's significance transcends sports. While the indigenous population in Taiwan accounts for less than 3% of the total population, they are highly represented in the field of athletics. Yang's success once again proves the talent and potential of indigenous peoples in sports.

More importantly, Yang has become a role model for indigenous youth. His story tells his people: regardless of your background, as long as you have dreams and perseverance, you can shine on the world stage.

Indigenous legislator Yi Cheng once said: "Yang Yung-wei is not just the light of Taiwan, but the light of the indigenous peoples. He has shown the world the excellence of Taiwan's indigenous people."

## Commercial Value and Endorsements

His Olympic silver medal made Yang Yung-wei a highly sought-after spokesperson. From sports brands to telecommunications companies, major corporations have sought to invite him for endorsements. However, Yang is quite cautious when choosing endorsements, prioritizing whether a brand's philosophy aligns with his own values.

Taiwan Mobile has been an important partner, tailoring advertisements around his image as a "Judo God." These commercial collaborations provide the financial support that allows Yang to focus on training without worrying about his livelihood.

Yet, Yang always remains clear about his priorities: "First and foremost, I am an athlete; only then am I a spokesperson." This professional attitude allows him to maintain his athletic integrity within commercial partnerships.

## 2024 Paris Olympics: The Path to Defense

Yang Yung-wei is currently preparing for the 2024 Paris Olympics. As a Tokyo Olympic silver medalist and former world number one, he carries even greater expectations and pressure.

"This time, I want the gold," Yang said, "not to prove anything, but to fulfill the wish I had back then."

From a technical standpoint, Yang already possesses the strength to win gold. The key lies in psychological adjustment—how to perform at his best under even greater pressure. This will be his greatest challenge.

## The Mission to Promote Judo

Yang Yung-wei is well aware of his influence and actively engages in promoting judo. He frequently visits schools and _dojos_ across the country to instruct young athletes and share his training insights and competitive experience.

"I hope Taiwan can have more judoka shining on the international stage," Yang said. "It shouldn't just be me."

Under his influence, the number of judo practitioners in Taiwan has grown significantly. Many parents have begun enrolling their children in judo, hoping to cultivate the next Yang Yung-wei.

## A Modern Interpretation of the Budo Spirit

Judo is not just a sport; it is an embodiment of the _budo_ (martial arts) spirit. "Maximum efficiency with minimum effort," "utilization of mental and physical energy," and "mutual welfare and benefit" are the core philosophies of judo. Yang is an excellent practitioner of these principles.

Regardless of whether he wins or loses, he bows to his opponent in respect. This respect for the opponent embodies the core values of martial arts culture. In a highly competitive era, Yang uses his actions to interpret what it means to engage in "gentlemanly competition."

## Future Outlook: From Athlete to Coach

Yang Yung-wei has already begun thinking about his plans after retirement. "I hope to become a coach and train more Taiwanese judoka," he said, "passing down the techniques and experience I have learned."

This idea is meaningful. Taiwan's judo community needs more coaches with international experience like Yang to cultivate more world-class athletes. His path as a coach may have an even more profound impact than his career as an athlete.

Yang Yung-wei's story tells us: the power of dreams is infinite. A young man from the Paiwan people, through persistence and hard work, climbed to the pinnacle of world judo. He has not only rewritten the history of Taiwanese judo but has also used his story to inspire countless young people to chase their dreams bravely.

From his silver medal in Tokyo to becoming number one in the world, and from his Asian Games gold to his expectations for the Paris Olympics, Yang Yung-wei's journey in judo continues to be written. No matter what the future holds, he has already proven: Taiwan's indigenous warriors have the ability to shine on the world stage.
