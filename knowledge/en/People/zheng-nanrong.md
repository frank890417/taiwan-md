# Cheng Nan-hsung (鄭南榕)

When the 1947 February 28 Incident broke out, a family in Hankou Street, Taipei, huddled inside their house, trembling. The father, Cheng Mu-sen (鄭木森), was a "Waishengren" (mainlander) from Fuzhou, and people were being hunted in the streets. It was a Taiwanese neighbor next door who hid them, saving the family's lives.

Seven months later, the couple's eldest son was born and named Nan-hsung.

Forty-one years later, this "Waishengren boy" who had once been saved by Taiwanese people ignited gasoline in his own magazine office to fight for the right of all Taiwanese to speak.

> **✦** His death stemmed from a precisely calculated conviction: if even death is not feared, those who are alive have no reason to remain silent.

## A Person Without a Place

The first half of Cheng Nan-hsung's life was a story of constant searching for identity.

He grew up in Luodong, Yilan, where his father ran a barbershop at the Chih Hsing Paper Mill cooperative. On his first day of elementary school, he was mocked by classmates for being "Waishengren" and got into a fight. Afterward, he and his younger brothers identified as _Benshengren_ (local Taiwanese), used Taiwanese as their mother tongue, and joined the "Benshengren camp" to fight other mainland children. He began experiencing the absurdity of provincial identity at the age of six.

He ranked first in his entrance exams for Yilan Senior High School and later entered Chien-Kuo High School. His father hoped he would attend Taipei Industrial Vocational School, which offered a five-year graduation track, but Cheng insisted on attending Chien-Kuo. For the university entrance exam, he abandoned his preferred liberal arts path to choose the Department of Engineering Science at National Cheng Kung University—because his family needed him to earn money quickly to support his younger brothers.

After one year, he realized it wasn't what he wanted. He took a leave of absence, retook the exams, and entered the Department of Philosophy at Fu Jen Catholic University. He later transferred to the Department of Philosophy at National Taiwan University. There, he visited the house-arrested Professor Yin Hai-kuang (殷海光) [https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%AE%B7%E6%B5%B7%E5%85%89] every week, reading Hu Shih, Li Ao, and classical liberalism. He found a conviction: China's history could not produce a free and democratic society; Taiwan had to walk its own path.

However, he refused to take the mandatory "Thought of the Father of the Nation" course required by the Kuomintang (KMT), so he never received his graduation diploma.

After being discharged from the military, Cheng Nan-hsung worked in textile exports, sold solar water heaters, imported Swiss lozenges, and ran a book and periodical distribution business. All failed. It wasn't until he was thirty-four that he found his battlefield: magazines.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Cheng Nan-hsung's wife, Yeh Chu-lan (葉菊蘭), is a Hakka from Miaoli. Her father vehemently opposed the marriage due to provincial identity issues. Cheng pursued Yeh through letters, messengers, and even telegrams; they eventually married via notary in a Taipei court, only receiving family recognition a year later. A boy mocked for his origin married a girl opposed because of hers—their lives were spent fighting the cages of identity.

## Twenty-Four Licenses

On March 12, 1984, _[Freedom Magazine](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%87%AA%E7%94%B1%E6%99%82%E4%BB%A3%E5%91%A8%E5%88%8A)_ was launched.[^1] The founder was Cheng Nan-hsung, the director was Li Ao, the president was Chen Shui-bian, and the publisher was Lin Shih-yu. There was only one slogan: "Strive for one hundred percent freedom."

But Cheng Nan-hsung did something no one expected: before the launch, he collected university diplomas everywhere and applied to the Government Information Office for **24 magazine licenses**.[^1]

He knew the government would ban him. If one issue was banned, he would immediately publish the next using a new license, while keeping the word "Freedom" in the title. He eventually used many of them. Every time the government issued a ban, he simply switched to another license and continued publishing. Each license had its purpose calculated well before the launch.

During the martial law era, _Freedom_ specialized in publishing what the government forbade: political reform, human rights protection, ethnic equality, and Taiwan's future. It also provided space for environmental movements and Indigenous rights advocacy, making it one of the few media outlets willing to cover diverse social movements. Every issue was a dance on the edge of a blade. Cheng Nan-hsung was prosecuted many times, but he never backed down. He said, "For someone with deep ideological training like me, running a magazine is the most appropriate way to practice my faith."

In 1987, _Freedom_ did something unprecedented—it publicly initiated activities to commemorate the February 28 Incident. It was the first time in forty years that a public media outlet dared to discuss February 28.[^2]

## That Constitutional Draft

In December 1988, Cheng Nan-hsung published the _Draft Constitution of the Republic of Taiwan_ authored by [Hsu Shih-kai (許世楷)](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A8%B1%E4%B8%96%E6%A5%B7) in the 254th issue of _Freedom_.[^3]

At that time, advocating for Taiwan independence was considered sedition and was punishable by death.

Cheng Nan-hsung knew exactly what this meant. However, he believed that if the people of a country were not even allowed to "discuss" that country's future, then that country's freedom of speech was a sham. What he wanted to defend was the legitimacy of **the discussion itself**—whether Taiwan independence was right or wrong was a separate issue.

On January 21, 1989, prosecutors indicted Cheng Nan-hsung on suspicion of sedition and summoned him to court.[^2]

He refused.

He locked himself inside the magazine office in Taipei's Songshan District and announced to the world:

**"The Kuomintang cannot catch my person; they can only catch my corpse."**

## April 7th

He remained self-imprisoned for seventy-one days.

In the early morning of April 7, 1989, [Hou Yu-ih (侯友宜)](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BE%AF%E5%8F%8B%E5%AE%9C), then the criminal division chief of the Zhongshan Precinct, led a team to storm the magazine office.[^4] Cheng Nan-hsung retreated into the editor-in-chief's office, locked the door, and ignited the gasoline he had prepared.

He was forty-one years old when he died. His daughter, Cheng Chu-mei, was nine.

Seven days later, another social activist, [Chan Yi-hua (詹益樺)](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A9%B9%E7%9B%8A%E6%A8%BA), committed self-immolation in front of the Presidential Office during Cheng Nan-hsung's funeral procession.[^4]

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> Hou Yu-ih, who led the raid, later became the Mayor of New Taipei City. This history remains one of Taiwan's most sensitive political memories—the names of the enforcer of state power and the martyr live simultaneously in the daily life of this island.

## After the Fire

Cheng Nan-hsung's death dropped a bombshell on Taiwanese society.

Many who had previously remained silent began to speak. Intellectuals, lawyers, teachers—those who had kept quiet for years out of fear saw that one person could give his life for the "right to speak," and suddenly felt they had no reason to remain silent.

His wife, Yeh Chu-lan, did not break. She walked through the door her husband had opened with his life—entering politics, she served as a legislator, Minister of Transportation, Chairperson of the Council of Indigenous Peoples, Vice Premier of the Executive Yuan, and from 2006 to 2007, Secretary-General to the President.[^5] Their daughter, Cheng Chu-mei, grew up to work for foundations, guarding her father's spiritual legacy.

This family's journey became a rare case in Taiwan's democratic movement of "the privatization of public loss"—where personal loss was transformed into the political energy of an entire generation.

> **⚖️ Controversial Perspectives**
> Cheng Nan-hsung's choice is still interpreted differently today. Supporters view his self-immolation as a precisely calculated declaration of conviction, believing he paid the highest price to ensure that freedom of speech could not be ignored. Critics argue that self-immolation was an extreme personal choice that sought goals through radical means, potentially obscuring paths for reform through institutional struggle; they also note that Hou Yu-ih was executing a legal arrest warrant and his actions should not be simplified as "violence." These two interpretive frameworks coexist in discussions of Taiwan's democratic history, and readers may judge for themselves.

On December 22, 2016, the Executive Yuan officially designated April 7 as "[Freedom of Speech Day](<https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%A8%80%E8%AB%96%E8%87%AA%E7%94%B1%E6%97%A5_(%E8%87%BA%E7%81%A3)>)."[^6] The [Cheng Nan-hsung Memorial Museum](https://www.nfreedommemorialfoundation.org/) in Taipei (located at the former magazine office site) preserves his final office, with burn marks still visible on the walls.

## The Legacy of an Activist Thinker

Cheng Nan-hsung liked to call himself an "activist thinker." This term describes him accurately: he did not just think about freedom; he lived freedom as a practice.

His core belief was extremely simple: **All viewpoints, including those you disagree with, must be freely discussable.** Independence and unification can be discussed, systems can be questioned, and governments can be criticized. Because "forbidding discussion" is itself a form of violence—more fundamental than the suppression of any specific content.

In today's Taiwan, people can criticize the president online, hold protest signs in the streets, and debate any issue in the media. This atmosphere of freedom is so commonplace that many have forgotten it once required a life in exchange.

What Cheng Nan-hsung left behind is a question that continues to be debated: Why is someone willing to trade their life for the right of others to speak? Whatever your answer may be, the fire he ignited in 1989 truly changed how Taiwanese people perceive the weight of freedom of speech.
