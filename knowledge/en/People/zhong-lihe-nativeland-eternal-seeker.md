# Zhong Lihe: Meinong 1915, the Pen-Farmer Who Fell in a Pool of Blood

> **30-second overview:** Zhong Lihe was born December 15, 1915, in a Hakka family in Meinong, Kaohsiung.[^1] He went to Northeast China in 1938 to make a living, returning to Taiwan in 1946.[^1] Major works: _Lishe Farm_ (novel), _The Native Place_ (short story, written 1959), _Impoverished Couple_.[^1] Called "the Pen-Farmer Who Fell in a Pool of Blood," often revered as the "Father of Taiwan Nativist Literature" (this title is contested; Lai He is the recognized father of Taiwan New Literature).[^2] On August 4, 1960, he coughed up blood while revising a manuscript and passed away, aged 44.[^1]

## Born in a Hakka Village in Meinong, Kaohsiung

Zhong Lihe was born December 15, 1915, in a Hakka farming family in Meinong, Kaohsiung; his given name was Zhong Lianhe.[^1] Meinong preserves a rich Hakka culture; its mountains, waters, and language became the primary material for his later writing.

After graduating from Meinong Public School, he was admitted to Tainan Normal School but could not complete his studies due to family financial difficulties, returning home to farm. By day he labored; by night he read extensively the works of modern writers such as Lu Xun, Mao Dun, and Lao She.

This self-taught literary training forged his writing foundation: no academic structural consciousness, but a linguistic sensibility repeatedly honed from life. His Hakka syntactic traces, the rhythms of rural labor — none were smoothed away by the anxiety to "write proper literature"; instead they became his most distinctive linguistic fingerprint.

## 1938: To the Northeast; 1946: Return to Taiwan

In 1938, at age 23, Zhong Lihe went to Northeast China (Dalian, Shenyang, Beijing) to make a living, remaining until he could return home after the war in 1946.[^1] Eight years away from his hometown gave him a deeper apprehension of the "native place"; this experience later became the source material for many of his short stories.

During those eight years in Northeast China, Zhong Lihe lived on "the motherland's" soil as a _bensheng ren_ (native Taiwanese / pre-1945 resident), yet discovered he was neither regarded as Chinese nor able to return to Taiwan. This doubly marginal position gave him a visceral, embodied experience of the question of "belonging," which later transformed into his repeated interrogation of the concept of the "native place."

## _Lishe Farm_: A True Depiction of Taiwan's Rural Villages

_Lishe Farm_ is Zhong Lihe's most important novel.[^1] Set in a Hakka farming village, it depicts the survival predicament of peasants on barren land. Zhong Lihe himself came from a farming family and knew rural poverty firsthand; the peasant figures in his writing are not idealized, and the work is regarded as an important precedent in Taiwan nativist literature.

_Lishe Farm_'s significance in Taiwan literary history lies not only in its earliness, but in its rural writing that lacks the outsider gaze of urban intellectuals. He did not "go down to the countryside for a look" then write for urban readers; he grew up on that land, writing his own reality. This internal perspective later influenced the scale of writing in Taiwan nativist literature.

## _The Native Place_: Short Story Written in 1959

_The Native Place_ is a short story Zhong Lihe created based on his own experience of leaving home and drifting.[^1] Written in 1959, it is a major work from the final phase of his life. In the story, "native place" refers to a spiritual home; the address Meinong is merely the starting point.

As a spiritual concept, the "native place" in Zhong Lihe's writing possesses a unique dialectic: he spent eight years in "China" yet felt no belonging; only after returning to Meinong did he realize that the true native place is emotional attachment to land and language, not geographic location. This reverse discovery gives his "native place" concept a unique philosophical depth in Taiwan literature.

Zhong Lihe revised manuscripts on his sickbed; there is a famous saying attributed to him: "I must finish writing my life, then die" (this statement awaits verification from primary documents).[^3]

Regardless of the exact source of this phrase, it accurately describes a reality: in the last years of his life, his body already severely eroded by tuberculosis, he still wrote, still revised. Writing was his race against time; in the final years as tuberculosis consumed his life, he kept writing, kept revising. This attitude later became the primary reason he is respected by later generations.

## 44 Years Old, Collapsed on a Manuscript

On August 4, 1960, Zhong Lihe suddenly coughed up blood while revising a manuscript; rushed to hospital, he did not survive, aged 44.[^1]

(Note: Some articles open with "a brief 45-year life" — this is contradictory; December 1915 to August 1960 is actually under 45 years; the correct age is 44.)

He is called "the Pen-Farmer Who Fell in a Pool of Blood."[^1]

This title circulates because it simultaneously describes the manner of his death and the attitude of his life: at the final moment, he chose to continue doing what he considered most important. This attitude itself became the spiritual core inevitably mentioned when later generations speak of Zhong Lihe.

## Title Clarification: Father of Nativist Literature (Not Father of Taiwan Literature)

Zhong Lihe is often honored as the "Father of Taiwan Nativist Literature," but "Father of Taiwan Literature" or "Father of Taiwan New Literature" usually refers to **Lai He** (1894–1943) — two different titles.[^2]

In 1976, the "Zhong Lihe Cultural and Educational Foundation" was established, holding the "Zhong Lihe Literature Prize" annually to discover new literary talent.

That a literature prize established in the name of a writer who died in 1960 has continued into the 21st century demonstrates that Zhong Lihe's literary spirit possesses considerable enduring symbolic power in the Taiwan nativist literature lineage. His name has become the vehicle for a certain standard: attention to land, writing truth, uncompromising toward mainstream formats.

**Common framing → More precise reading**: Zhong Lihe is often described through a "tragic" framework — poverty and illness, early death at 44, falling in a pool of blood. But this framework easily obscures the power of the works themselves. His literature was never a monument frozen in suffering; it is the honest record of a creator with clear critical consciousness confronting Hakka rural reality. Tragedy was his circumstance, not his literature.

> 🎙️ **Curator's Note**: If Zhong Lihe's 44-year life leaves only the image of "the Pen-Farmer Who Fell in a Pool of Blood," we have taken away the most important thing about him as a writer: how lucid his observational perspective was, how honest his language was, what concrete literary foundations his works contributed to the establishment of Taiwan nativist literature.
>
> He and Lai He represent two different headwaters of Taiwan literature: Lai He's anti-Japanese spirit is political; Zhong Lihe's rural writing is social. The two lines later converged in the 1970s nativist literature debate, but he was already gone; his name was invoked as the source of that tradition — this is the position later generations gave him.
>
> A 44-year life left only a few books, but those few books established a standard: nativist literature was never romantic nostalgia for a past countryside, but honest recording of real life. This standard, every time later generations invoke his name, they are confirming it remains valid.

From a Hakka farming family in Meinong, to drifting in the Northeast, back to the land of Meinong, to revising manuscripts in a pool of blood — Zhong Lihe's life is an extreme sample of "how literature equals life": he traded his body's last strength for the completion of his works.
