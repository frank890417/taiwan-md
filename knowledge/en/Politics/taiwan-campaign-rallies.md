Every election eve, Taiwan's streets undergo a remarkable transformation. Deafening loudspeakers, multicolored campaign flags, and those "stage trucks" (Stage Truck) that can metamorphose from ordinary freight trucks into neon-flashing stages in mere minutes — together they construct Taiwan's globally unique "election campaign culture"[^1]. This is not merely a political contest for votes, but a "democratic carnival" fusing grassroots aesthetics, social psychology, and legal norms.

From a sociological perspective, Taiwan's campaign rallies have long transcended simple policy pronouncements, evolving into a highly ritualized process of collective resonance[^7]. In these occasions, what voters seek is often not cold policy data, but emotional resonance and a sense of collective belonging[^15].

## Stage Trucks and Campaign Vehicles: The "Transformers" of Grassroots Culture

If one must pick the most representative visual symbol of Taiwan's campaign rallies, "stage trucks" and modified campaign vehicles surely top the list. These seemingly ordinary freight trucks, upon reaching their destination, need only deploy hydraulic systems and simple operations to unfold within minutes into luxurious stages equipped with top-tier sound systems, LED screens, and dazzling lighting[^3].

Photographer Shen Chao-liang (沈昭良) profoundly documented this uniquely Taiwanese mobile stage culture in his renowned _STAGE_ series. He notes that the development of stage trucks originated from the frequent needs of Taiwanese folk temple festivals (_baibai_ 酬神) and weddings, funerals, and celebrations[^2]. Early performance stages were mostly temporarily erected, time-consuming and laborious. As times evolved, operators seeking greater mobility and visual impact converted trucks into electronic keyboard vehicles, ultimately developing today's highly automated hydraulic stage trucks.

![Figure 1: In early street campaign activities, party propaganda vehicles and supporter processions wove through urban streets together. This image records the dynamic streetscape of Taiwan's street-level political mobilization, with vehicles carrying large installations and amplification equipment, exhibiting a high degree of grassroots participation and fluidity.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b6/908_Taiwan_Republic_Campaign_people_and_Fuso_FM_truck_20060318.jpg)

Early political mobilization on Taiwan's streets was often accompanied by large modified trucks and endless parade processions. This practice of temporarily converting vehicles into propaganda platforms broke the spatial constraints of traditional political rallies, allowing political appeals to penetrate directly into every street and alley. The design of these stage trucks and campaign vehicles brims with intense local Taiwanese colors — vivid color schemes, exaggerated lighting effects — delivering not only strong visual impact but also reflecting the grassroots society's yearning for "heat and noise" (_re nao_ 熱鬧) and "pomp and circumstance" (_pai chang_ 排場)[^3].

## Democratic Snacks: Political Mobilization on the Tip of the Tongue

At Taiwan's campaign sites, besides the political speeches on stage, the "democratic snacks" below are equally indispensable souls. Most famous among them is the "democratic sausage"[^18]. This vendor culture originating from 1980s street movements saw vendors pushing charcoal-grilled sausage carts following protest crowds, providing physical sustenance to participants and becoming an informal emblem of the democratic movement[^18].

![Figure 2: A "democratic sausage" stall photographed at a protest and campaign rally site. White smoke rises from the charcoal grills as vendors serve grassroots delicacies to the masses amid the clamor of political gatherings. This "charcoal-grilled aroma at the edge of protest" has become a unique olfactory and visual memory in the history of Taiwan's democratic movement.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c5/318_-_%E6%B0%91%E4%B8%BB%E9%A6%99%E8%85%B8.jpg)

Democratic sausage stalls often nestle right against protest or campaign crowds. The sausages sizzling on charcoal grills and the drifting oil smoke are not merely sources of physiological sustenance; they carry the shared lived experience of Taiwan's civil society in street protests and electoral mobilization[^18]. Besides sausages, another food with high political symbolism is "election fried rice noodles"[^17]. In central and southern Taiwan, when candidates hold small policy presentations, they often prepare large vats of fried rice noodles and meatball soup to treat neighbors. This "rice noodle gathering" (_mi fen chang_ 米粉場) culture has wafted through Taiwan for over 30 years; it is not only to fill voters' stomachs but also a display of human sentiment and a mobilization tool[^17].

## Campaign Propaganda Vehicles: Mobile Democratic Broadcast Towers and Noise Anxiety

Shuttling through alleys, "campaign propaganda vehicles" (colloquially "battle trucks" _zhan che_ 戰車) constitute another political landscape. These modified vehicles typically mount massive loudspeakers, repeatedly broadcasting candidates' platforms, campaign songs, or beseeching slogans[^9]. Although viewed as a necessary means to reach the grassroots, the noise they generate often sparks social controversy.

According to Article 54 of the _Civil Servants Election and Recall Act_, parties and candidates using loudspeakers must not create noise, or they will be fined by the environmental protection authority under the _Noise Control Act_[^10]. However, due to the high mobility of propaganda vehicles, law enforcement agencies often face technical difficulties in actual measurement and ticketing[^11]. This tug-of-war between the "voice of democracy" and the "peace of daily life" reflects the ongoing negotiation of citizens' rights boundaries in Taiwan's democratic transition.

![Figure 3: Panoramic view of a large campaign rally in Kaohsiung's Fengshan District. Tens of thousands of supporters gather on a plaza flashing with lights, waving flags in hand, embodying the irreplaceable "theater effect" and collective resonance of Taiwan's physical campaign rallies.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3d/The_campaign_rally_of_Han_Kuo-yu_in_Fengshan%2C_Kaohsiung.jpg)

At large campaign rallies, tens of thousands gather in the night, collectively constructing powerful political energy through synchronized chants and waving flags. This "theater effect" produced by physical assembly is something digital social media can hardly fully replace.

## Election Trinket Economics: From Fans to Hipster Design

Another hallmark of Taiwan's elections is the dazzling array of "election trinkets" (_xuan ju xiao wu_ 選舉小物). From the most practical tissues, ballpoint pens, and lighters of yesteryear to the indispensable plastic fans in sweltering weather, these trinkets are not only propaganda tools but also form a unique "election industry chain"[^12].

As the voter structure grows younger, election trinkets have evolved from mere giveaways into "brand merchandise." Candidates now launch design-conscious hipster (_wen qing_ 文青) products — eco-bags, sports towels, even plush toys — attracting supporters to purchase at their own expense, transforming political identity into lifestyle display[^12]. Interestingly, part of this industry chain was once outsourced to China to lower costs, but in recent years, due to geopolitical risks, many orders have flowed back to domestic Taiwanese manufacturers, forming another form of "roots staying in Taiwan" (_gen liu Taiwan_ 根留台灣)[^12].

## "Dong Suan" and the Magic of Language: Emotional Catalysts on the Campaign Stage

In campaign occasions, language is not only a tool of communication but a catalyst of emotion. Most widely known is the term "_dong suan_" (凍蒜). This word, a Minnan homophone for "elected" (_dang xuan_ 當選), has become an indispensable slogan in Taiwan's elections[^5]. When the host leads tens of thousands below in a hoarse voice chanting "_dong suan_," that overwhelming momentum often instantly pushes the atmosphere to its peak.

Beyond simple, powerful slogans, politicians' "election language" is equally a profound discipline[^1]. In campaign speeches, candidates often heavily employ grassroots language, metaphors, and contrast techniques to close the distance with voters. This linguistic strategy not only effectively ignites supporters' shared enmity but also creates easily transmissible memory hooks in the age of fragmented information[^1].

## The NT$30 Lunchbox Boundary: The Tug-of-War Between Law and Sentiment

To rectify election ethics, Taiwan's judicial organs have gradually developed a set of standards for judging vote-buying in practice. Most widely known is the so-called "NT$30 threshold"[^4]. This standard originates from a 2001 Ministry of Justice interpretation stating that if candidates distribute purely promotional items valued at NT$30 or below, they are generally considered insufficient to sway voters' voting intentions and therefore do not constitute vote-buying[^13].

However, in actual legal operation, "NT$30" is not an absolute, uncrossable red line. Courts judging whether vote-buying is constituted still comprehensively consider the candidate's subjective intent, the nature of the items, and whether they are sufficient to affect voters' willingness to vote[^4]. For example, providing simple fried rice noodles at a campaign rally, though the cost may exceed NT$30, is usually viewed as a display of the Taiwanese social custom of "treating guests" (_qing ke_ 請客), not vote-buying behavior[^4].

![Figure 4: Giant campaign flags fluttering in the wind at a campaign rally site. Vivid visual symbols and slogans intertwine in the sunlight, becoming the most representative democratic landscape during Taiwan's major elections, symbolizing the cohesion of voters' collective will.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/%E8%87%BA%E7%81%A3%E9%80%86%E8%BD%89%E5%8B%9D_Flag_of_Come-back_Win_for_TAIWAN.jpg)

Campaign flags are not only visual tools to mark camps; they also exert a cohesive centripetal force in collective activities. In these seas of flags, voters can temporarily shed the isolation of daily life and merge into a community sharing a common political imagination.

## Social Capital and Political Participation: The Deep Functions of Campaigning

From an academic perspective, campaign rallies possess the function of strengthening "social capital"[^16]. Through face-to-face collective participation, trust and norms among voters are reinforced and, transmitted through interpersonal networks, further influence voter turnout[^15]. Research shows that Taiwan voters' electoral participation is influenced not only by individual rational policy analysis but also by the emotional mobilization of their social networks[^15].

With the arrival of the digital age, the battlefield of campaigns has gradually shifted to online communities and meme propagation[^14], yet physical campaign rallies still possess an irreplaceable "theater effect" — they are the most vivid, most irreplaceable collective memory in Taiwan's democratic process.
