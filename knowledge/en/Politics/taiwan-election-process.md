> **30-Second Overview:**
> As a global semiconductor hub, Taiwan insists on using seemingly "low-tech" manual vote counting during its elections. This process—starting from the display of empty ballot boxes at 8:00 AM on election day to the ability for citizens to record and supervise the count—ensures that even in an era of rampant disinformation, the destination of every single ballot is clearly visible. This biennial collective ritual serves as Taiwan's most resilient line of defense for democracy. From the polling ban ten days before an election to the countless citizens returning to their hometowns to cast a crucial vote, Taiwan's electoral process is both a functioning institution and a microcosm of the nation's social pulse. In 2026, as disinformation and AI deepfakes challenge public trust, this "visible fairness" becomes even more precious.

On March 23, 1996, Taiwan held its first direct presidential election. Voter turnout that day reached 76.04% [^1], marking the official beginning of the era of universal suffrage on the island. Thirty years later, although digital technology has permeated every corner of life, Taiwan's electoral process retains a highly "ritualistic" quality. The voter turnout for the 2024 presidential election remained at a high level of 71.86% [^5], demonstrating the continued enthusiasm of Taiwanese society for democratic participation.

### The Overall Institutional Framework: Automated Democratic Rights

Acquiring voting rights in Taiwan is extremely simple and inclusive. Any citizen of the Republic of China who is at least 20 years old and has resided in an electoral district for at least four months (six months for presidential/vice-presidential elections) automatically possesses the right to vote [^32] [^34]. This "automatic registration system" based on the household registration system ensures that local household registration offices automatically compile the "Electoral Register" 20 days before an election [^34], allowing voters to participate in democracy without needing to actively register.

### Candidate Nomination and Campaign Rhythms

Candidates may run for office through party recommendation or via citizen signatures. For presidential candidates, the threshold for citizen signatures must reach 1.5% of the total number of voters from the most recent legislative election (approximately 290,000 people in 2024) [^7] [^9]. During the campaign period, the statutory time for campaigning is from 7:00 AM to 10:00 PM daily. The government also holds official policy presentation sessions to provide a neutral information platform.

### Ten Days Before the Election: Polling Bans and the 2026 "Democracy Holiday" Defense

Starting 10 days before election day, the law strictly prohibits the publication or citation of any polling data [^10] [^11]. This ban aims to reduce information interference prior to the vote. Entering 2025 and 2026, discussions regarding "absentee voting" and a "Democracy Holiday" have become political focal points.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Within the Legislative Yuan in 2026, there is a clear divergence in policy between the ruling and opposition parties. The opposition is pushing for a special law on "absentee voting" to facilitate voters living away from their hometowns [^22]; meanwhile, the ruling party has proposed a "Democracy Holiday" draft, advocating for a holiday on election day and the preceding day, along with transportation subsidies, in an attempt to resolve the burden of returning home to vote while maintaining the high-trust, "manual and local" system currently in place [^23]. The core of this debate lies in how to balance "convenience" with "preventing foreign interference/ensuring secret ballot security."

### Pre-election Preparation and the Ballot Security Chain

The number of ballots printed in Taiwan strictly follows the principle of "100% of the number of electors" [^26]. From the lockdown and handover at the printing plant to police escort, and finally to being sealed in safes in various townships and districts, every link is under dual supervision by the police and election officials. This system, which differs sharply from South Korea (where printing is based on estimated turnout), ensures a foundation of trust that "ballots will never be in short supply" [^26].

### 8:00 AM: The Task of the First Voter

At 8:00 AM on election day, the chief supervisor displays an "empty ballot box" to the first person present. Since 2014, Taiwan has used both paper and traditional acrylic ballot boxes; regardless of material, the "display of the empty box" before and after voting is a non-negotiable procedure [^39]. Voters must bring their ID card and notification slip, verify their names on the register, collect their ballots, and use a specially designed _bu_ (卜) character stamp in the private voting booth [^20].

### Vote Counting: The Rhythm and SOP of Democracy

When voting ends at 4:00 PM, the polling station immediately transforms into a counting station. Taiwan's manual counting Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) is extremely rigorous and consists of five main steps:

1.  **Verification**: Removing ballots from the box and confirming the total count.
2.  **Announcing**: Staff members hold each ballot up high, facing the audience, and loudly announce the candidate number, name, and "one vote."
3.  **Recording**: Another staff member marks a _zheng_ (正) character on a tally board and repeats the count for confirmation.
4.  **Sorting**: Ballots are categorized into piles by candidate, with every 100 ballots being verified once.
5.  **Displaying the Empty Box**: Once all ballots have been counted, the empty ballot box is displayed to the public again to confirm nothing was missed.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Taiwanese law strictly prohibits "sorting before announcing." Every single ballot must be removed from the box and immediately announced under the gaze of the public; this is to prevent any possibility of "swapping ballots" during the sorting process [^27]. This "low-tech, high-transparency" process has been praised by Reuters as a "high-trust model" for combating disinformation [^8].

### Post-count Data Transmission: An Extension of Transparency

After completing the count, staff members fill out a "Polling and Counting Report" on the spot. This report is made in multiple copies and must be jointly confirmed and signed by the chief supervisor and observers from various political parties. One copy is posted at the entrance of the counting station for public inspection, while others are delivered by designated personnel to district offices or election operation centers to be entered into the Central Election Commission's tallying system for national aggregation.

### Return and Sealing: The Final Physical Defense

Once counting is finished and reports are completed, the "physical evidence" of democracy enters its most critical sealing phase. All ballots (including valid votes, invalid votes, remaining ballots, and the electoral register) must be categorized into bags according to regulations, with the chief supervisor and observers applying official stamps or signatures at the seal [^36].

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** These bagged ballots are escorted by police throughout the entire process back to the township or district election operation center [^37]. According to regulations, these ballots must be sealed in the operation center for a period (usually 6 months to 1 year, or longer if there is litigation) until the statutory destruction deadline expires, at which point they are destroyed under the supervision of audit and election units [^38]. This complete chain "from ballot box to shredder" ensures that even after an election ends, the security of every single ballot remains protected at the state level.

### 2026's New Challenges: AI Deepfakes and Information Defense

In the context of 2026, the challenge of disinformation has evolved from text to AI deepfake imagery. The Central Election Commission (CEC) and civil fact-checking centers have established real-time clarification mechanisms, emphasizing that "physical counting" is the ultimate truth; any online tallying images that do not match the on-site reports can be immediately verified [^14] [^15].

### Grassroots "Counting Parties" and Alleyway Democracy

Beyond formal procedures, Taiwan's "counting party" culture demonstrates social resilience. In stir-fry restaurants or temple squares, neighbors gather to watch the tallying on television; this is both political participation and a form of social healing. This "alleyway democracy" transforms intense election battles into a shared social experience.

### Follow-up Procedures: Legal Remedies and Evolution

The results become clear on the night of the election, followed by official announcements and the issuance of certificates by the CEC. If there are disputes, Taiwan provides for lawsuits regarding "invalidity of election" or "invalidity of election results" [^35]. Despite ongoing discussions about digital transformation, Taiwanese people still hold their deepest trust in that piece of paper—the one with a red seal, held high for all to see.
