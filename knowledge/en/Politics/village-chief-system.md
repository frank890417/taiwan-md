# The Village and Neighborhood Chief System: 7,748 Elected Leaders, Taiwan's Most Grassroots Political Unit

> **30-Second Overview:** There are 7,748 village and neighborhood chiefs across Taiwan—more than the 6,600+ 7-Eleven stores. With a four-year term and a monthly administrative subsidy of NT$54,000, this is not a salary, but an office subsidy provided by the government to "local relationship proxies." The village/neighborhood chief position is the **only one in Taiwan's elections where independent candidates are dominant**, with over 60% running without party affiliation. Re-election rates are astonishingly high; the incumbency advantage is several times higher than for other public offices. The history of this position traces back to the Japanese-era _baojia_ (保甲) system, which was directly renamed and inherited after the war; they have been popularly elected since 1950—even earlier than mayors and county magistrates. While it may not feel like "politics," it is evidence of Taiwan's autonomy growing from the very bottom up.

---

## The Neighborhood Office at 5:30 AM

At 5:30 AM, the metal rolling door of an alley in Taipei's Wanhua District is slowly raised. The lights in the neighborhood office turn on before the breakfast shops. A chief serving their third consecutive term is counting the lunch boxes to be delivered that day—the list includes twelve elderly residents living alone; he notes in his head who wants soft food, who recently had dental work, and who just returned from the hospital yesterday, keeping details more precise than the official resident information system.

At 7:00 AM, he finishes a round on his scooter. On the way back to the office, he takes three calls: a resident reporting a flickering streetlight at the alley entrance; a mother asking about registration for next semester's community activities; and a wife saying her neighbor's dog barked all night, hoping the chief will "go talk to them"—not to call the police, but just to have a word.

At 9:00 AM, he logs into the Ministry of the Interior's village/neighborhood chief system to upload minutes from last night's resident assembly. At 10:00 AM, the District Office calls to ask if he can assist with a household registration promotion next week. At 11:00 AM, he sits in the office receiving an elderly veteran fresh from the hospital, helping him fill out a welfare application form—the forms are actually at the District Office, but this veteran cannot navigate the MRT.

In the afternoon, he patrols the neighborhood. In the evening, he returns to organize coordination documents for election polling stations—as the 2026 November 28 nine-in-one elections approach, he must conduct a site inspection for his neighborhood's polling station. At 7:00 PM, there is a resident assembly. After it adjourns at 9:00 PM, he buys a rice ball from a convenience store for dinner.

There are **7,748** such chiefs in Taiwan.

## 7,748 Elected Leaders

7,748—this number exceeds the store count of the leading convenience store chain. At the end of 2024, there were approximately 6,800 7-Elevens and over 1,100 PX Mart stores[^1] across Taiwan. The density of village/neighborhood chiefs is higher than that of the convenience stores people visit daily for coffee or rice balls.

Specific composition (based on the 2026 election announcement by the Central Election Commission and statistics from the Ministry of the Interior's Department of Civil Affairs)[^2]:

- **Neighborhood Chiefs in Special Municipalities**: approximately 4,800—the most grassroots level within the six major cities.
- **Village Chiefs in Non-Special Municipalities** (townships/towns): approximately 1,800—primarily in counties such as Hualien, Taitung, Yunlin, Chiayi, Nantou, and Pingtung.
- **Neighborhood Chiefs in Non-Special Municipalities** (county-administered cities): approximately 1,100—for example, neighborhoods within Keelung, Hsinchu, or Chiayi City.
- **Total: 7,748** (subject to the final 2026 CEC election announcement).

These 7,748 positions are elected simultaneously on November 28, 2026—they are grouped onto a single ballot alongside mayors of special municipalities, county magistrates, city mayors, councilors, township mayors, indigenous district chiefs, indigenous district representatives, and township/city representatives; this is the "ninth slot" in the term "nine-in-one elections"[^3].

Among these nine positions, the other eight feature party labels, policy debates, and large-scale rallies. The **sole exception** is the village/neighborhood chief—most candidates do not carry party affiliations, do not hold rallies, and do not print fancy flyers. Instead, they ring doorbells house by house, introduce themselves as "the neighbor from next door," and slip business cards into mailboxes.

## Legal Basis: Found in the Final Three Articles of the _Local Government Act_

The legal status of this position is written in Articles 59 through 61 of the _Local Government Act_—out of 88 articles in the entire law, village/neighborhood chiefs are placed near the end[^4].

Article 59 stipulates that each village or neighborhood shall have one "Village Chief" or "Neighborhood Chief," elected by the residents in accordance with law for a four-year term, and may serve consecutive terms upon re-election. The key phrase here is "**may serve consecutive terms**"—there is no term limit. Theoretically, a chief can serve five, eight, or twelve terms, as long as they win once every four years. In practice, many chiefs serve more than three terms, and serving more than five is not uncommon[^5].

Article 60 stipulates that the village/neighborhood chief is subject to the direction and supervision of the township (town, city, or district) mayor and shall handle village/neighborhood official business and assigned matters. The phrase "**assigned matters**" constitutes the true substance of their daily work—the law does not list them, but in practice, they cover everything under the sun.

Article 61 stipulates that the position is unsalaried (non-remunerative), but **the township (town, city, or district) office shall allocate an administrative subsidy for village/neighborhood chief affairs**, paid monthly. The amount is specified in Article 7 of the _Regulations on the Payment of Expenses for Local Elected Representatives and Administrative Subsidies for Village and Neighborhood Chiefs_: NT$50,000 per month for each village/neighborhood, with a 20% increase for indigenous areas. This article also ties this money to the adjustments made for military, civil service, and teaching personnel; any general adjustments are applied proportionally, with the adjusted amount and effective date announced by the Ministry of the Interior[^6]. This figure remained at NT$45,000 for many years until a 2022 amendment raised it to NT$50,000, effective from the inauguration of new chiefs on December 25 of that year[^27]. The Ministry of the Interior announced in July 2026 that it would rise further to NT$54,000 for general villages and NT$64,800 for indigenous areas, retroactive to July 1, 2026; on New Year's Day 2027, following a 4% increase for civil servants, these will rise to NT$56,160 and NT$67,392 respectively[^28]. The term "unsalaried" is crucial—it means the chief is **not a civil servant**; they have no labor or health insurance benefits, no pension, and are not subject to the civil service performance evaluation system. They receive an "administrative subsidy," not a salary.

The legal design of an "unsalaried" position supplemented by over NT$50,000 a month reflects a subtle stance: the chief is positioned as a "**dedicated local resident serving their community**," rather than a state employee. This positioning influences the entire political economy of the role.

## Statutory Powers vs. Actual Work

The law lists three statutory powers[^4]:

1. **Convene village/neighborhood assemblies**—at least once a year to discuss public affairs.
2. **Execute matters assigned by township, city, or district offices**—implementing higher-level government policies at the household level.
3. **Reflect public opinion**—reporting resident needs upward.

However, any incumbent chief will tell you these three are just the tip of the iceberg. The actual task list is much longer[^7][^8]:

**Mediation of Neighborhood Disputes**—leaking pipes between floors, parking disputes, pet noise, or trash sorting disagreements. A chief is not a police officer, judge, or arbitrator, but they are often the final buffer before a conflict reaches the police. An experienced chief can resolve 70% of neighborhood disputes in the office's kitchenette.

**Care for Elderly Residents Living Alone**—delivering lunch boxes, accompanying them to medical appointments, reminding them to pay utility bills, and reporting if no movement is detected at a home for several days. Taiwan's aging index has surpassed 100[^9]; chiefs are often the first people outside of Social Affairs bureaus to notice abnormalities in elderly residents.

**Election Polling Station Coordination**—In every election, the CEC establishes over 17,000 polling stations across Taiwan[^10]. Every station requires assistance from the chief regarding venue, traffic flow, and accessibility. Polling stations are usually set up in local elementary schools, community centers, or temples—the coordination of these venues is almost entirely in the hands of the chief.

**Disaster Reporting**—typhoons, earthquakes, flooding, fires. The chief is the terminal nerve ending of the local disaster prevention system. When power or water is cut, the first person residents call is often not 1999 (the government hotline) or 119 (emergency services), but their chief.

**Community Building**—Mid-Autumn barbecues, Dragon Boat Festival egg-balancing, senior citizen trips, "mommy classes," and children's talent workshops. These activities are not mandatory, but chiefs who organize them build a good reputation and can be re-elected for five terms or more.

**Household Registration Assistance**—helping elderly residents who cannot use computers fill out forms, explaining household registration transfer processes, and relaying new district office policies.

This list has no ceiling. A chief serving their third term might know the names of 70–80% of the households in their area—this is not salesmanship, but twelve years of accumulated social capital.

## From _Baojia_ to Village/Neighborhood: Eighty Years of Institutional Inheritance

The village/neighborhood unit of local governance was not invented by the Republic of China. Its predecessor was the **_baojia_ (保甲) system** during the Japanese colonial period[^11][^12].

In 1898, the Japanese Governor-General's Office promulgated the _Baojia Regulations_, reorganizing the existing _baojia_ structures from the late Qing dynasty into the terminal layer of colonial governance. Ten households formed a _jia_, and ten _jia_ formed a _bao_. Each _bao_ had a _baozheng_ (保正) and each _jia_ had a _jiazhang_ (甲長)—these were elected by local residents (though the list required approval from the Governor-General), with two-year terms. The functions of the _baojia_ system were broad: household registration, epidemic prevention, tax collection, maintaining public order, and labor mobilization.

The _baojia_ system operated in Taiwan for 47 years until it was abolished after the war in 1945. However, the "baojia" grassroots unit did not truly disappear—it was simply renamed.

In 1946, the Taiwan Provincial Administrative Executive Office abolished the _baojia_ and established "Villages" (for rural areas) and "Neighborhoods" (for urban areas)[^13]. The _baozheng_ became "Village Chiefs" or "Neighborhood Chiefs," but the boundaries, household counts, and organizational structures remained largely the same. Many _baozheng_ from the Japanese era transitioned directly into the first post-war chiefs during the 1946 reorganization.

Starting in 1950, as Taiwan Province implemented local autonomy, village/neighborhood chiefs changed to being **directly elected by residents**[^13]. This occurred even earlier than the direct election of mayors and county magistrates—the latter was not implemented until the first provincial-level elections in 1950[^14]. While village/neighborhood chiefs started at the same time as mayors, **their direct election has never been interrupted**, whereas mayoral elections experienced several adjustments due to central government legal revisions between the 1960s and 1990s.

With the enactment of the _Local Government Act_ in 1999, the village/neighborhood chief system was integrated into this law, officially becoming a unified grassroots autonomous position nationwide[^4].

From the establishment of _baojia_ in 1898, to the renaming in 1946, to direct elections in 1950, and legal integration in 1999—this **128-year institutional inheritance** is evidence of the continuity of Taiwan's grassroots governance. The administrative skeleton left by the Japanese colonial system was refilled with the content of democratic elections after the war and continues to operate today.

## Why This Position is Dominated by Independents

Among the nine positions, the village/neighborhood chief is the **only** one where independents hold a majority[^15].

This phenomenon is not accidental; it is determined by scale.

**The scale is too small**: An average neighborhood has a population of 2,000 to 5,000, and rural villages may have only 200–500 people[^16]. At this scale, whether a candidate wins depends not on party platform, but on **whether they know the residents and whether the residents know them**. A candidate who can call out neighbors by name is more useful than any party nomination.

**Factions are far stronger than parties**: Local clans (especially in southern/central Taiwan and offshore islands), hometown associations, temple management committees, farmers' associations, fisheries associations, and industry unions—the influence of these organizations at the village level far outweighs political parties[^17]. A candidate might belong to a certain temple committee or a farmers' association, but they won't run under those logos; they run under their own name.

**Incumbency advantage is extremely strong**: Re-election rates for chiefs have long remained above 70–80%[^15]. A chief serving their third term has already served twelve years; they know the streetlight number of every alley, the family situation of every household, and the contact person for every office window—this accumulated capital is difficult for a new candidate to catch up with in a four-month campaign.

**Party affiliation may actually be a liability**: Printing a party logo on village/neighborhood campaign materials might alienate half of the residents. A chief must serve "all residents"—regardless of political stance—so the cost of carrying a party label often outweighs the benefit. Many chiefs, even if they privately lean toward a certain party, register as independents publicly.

These four factors combine to create the unique ecology of village/neighborhood elections: **party affiliation is not the core variable; personal relationships and service records are**.

## Political Economy: NT$54,000 is Not a Salary

The monthly administrative subsidy of NT$54,000 is often misunderstood as "a village chief's monthly salary"—it is not[^6].

**The meaning of "Administrative Subsidy"**: This money is not personal income for the chief; it is an office expense paid by the government—covering electricity and internet for the office, stationery and photocopying, fuel for official business, tea and snacks for residents, and printing community newsletters or event flyers. **The chief must personally cover all expenses related to the position** by deducting them from this subsidy.

In reality, for a dedicated chief, NT$54,000 is often insufficient. Many chiefs pay out of pocket to bridge the gap—printing their own Spring Festival couplets for residents, giving red envelopes to elderly residents during Lunar New Year, or subsidizing equipment rentals for community events.

**Additional activity subsidies**: Besides the monthly administrative subsidy, various county and city governments allocate separate funds for village/neighborhood activities, business operations, and Lunar New Year greetings; the amounts fluctuate based on local finances[^18]. These are earmarked funds that require receipts for reimbursement.

**They cannot receive formal salaries like City Councilors**: As public officials, City Councilors receive monthly research fees, attendance fees, and health checkup subsidies totaling over NT$100,000[^19], along with labor/health insurance and council inspection allowances. Chiefs do not have these—they are not civil servants.

**Part-time work is common**: Because of the "unsalaried + NT$54,000 subsidy" design, many chiefs must hold other jobs to make a living—driving taxis, running small businesses, working in insurance, or serving as a chief after retirement while collecting both a pension and the subsidy. Very few people rely solely on being a chief for their livelihood, especially in small rural villages in central or southern Taiwan.

This economic structure determines who runs for these positions—usually middle-aged to retired individuals who **already have an economic foundation, want to serve their community, have time, and possess wide social networks**. For young people, there is a structural difficulty in running—not that they cannot win, but that they cannot sustain themselves economically.

## Structural Tensions

The village/neighborhood chief system has operated for eighty years, accumulating several unresolved tensions.

**Huge gap between urban and rural areas**: A neighborhood in Taipei's Xinyi District might have over 10,000 people, while a mountain village in Hualien might have only 200. Despite having the same administrative subsidy and salary structure, the workload can differ by more than twentyfold[^20]. Urban chiefs deal with high population density and complex administrative tasks; rural village chiefs serve fewer people but may cover a geographic area spanning several natural villages, requiring hours of travel for a single round.

Reform has been proposed to address this—such as adjusting subsidies based on population brackets—but every attempt at legal revision has struggled because "the 7,748 villages and neighborhoods affect too many people nationwide."

**Low proportion of women**: Traditionally, the position is male-dominated. While female political participation in Taiwan has steadily increased at the level of legislators, councilors, and mayors, the proportion of women at the village/neighborhood chief level rises more slowly[^21]. The reasons are complex: traditional neighborhood politics (temples, farmers' associations, clans) have higher barriers for women; the long and irregular working hours are a structural barrier for women with family caregiving responsibilities; and campaign resource networks are traditionally male-dominated.

In recent years, more female chiefs have appeared in urban areas—especially in younger neighborhoods in Taipei, New Taipei, Taichung, etc.—but the national average remains low.

**The "Ghost Neighborhood" phenomenon**: A minority of villages/neighborhoods are criticized as extensions of political networks rather than true autonomous units[^22]. So-called "ghost neighborhoods" refer to areas where elections have almost no competition, incumbents serve for twenty or thirty years, resident assembly attendance is extremely low, and the neighborhood office is effectively controlled by a specific network of connections. While not widespread, this phenomenon is frequently mentioned in academic and civic discussions regarding the quality of grassroots autonomy.

Corresponding reform discussions include: mandatory public disclosure of assets for chiefs (currently only a portion of the 7,748 positions are required to report), introducing service evaluation mechanisms, and strengthening the legal status of resident assemblies to ensure genuine participation. These discussions have yet to result in a unified national legislative version.

## International Comparison: Village/Neighborhood Chiefs are Very Taiwanese

Many countries have "the most basic unit of local governance," but their operational logics differ.

**Japan: _Jichikaichō_ (Neighborhood Association)**—Japanese _chonaikai_, _jichikaikai_, or _ku-kai_ are non-profit community organizations based on local ties; they are **not a formal level of government governance**[^23]. Association heads are elected by residents and have no legal enforcement power; joining the association itself is voluntary. Japan does not have an "elected local public official" equivalent to Taiwan's chiefs—the most basic elected officials are municipal assembly members.

**South Korea: _Tong_ and _Ban_ system**—Beneath a Korean _dong_, there are _tong_ and _ban_; the _tongjang_ and _banjang_ are either elected by residents or appointed by the _dong_ office[^24]. This system is flatter than Taiwan's, but the authority and resources of these leaders are far less than those of Taiwan's chiefs.

**France: _Maire_ (Mayor)**—A French village mayor (_maire_) has substantial administrative power, can issue administrative orders, sign birth/marriage certificates, and execute municipal budgets[^25]. A French _maire_ is closer to the level of a Taiwanese township mayor, not a village chief.

**United States: Precinct Captain**—In some US states, there is a "precinct captain" role, but this is an internal political party position, not a government public office[^26].

By contrast, **Taiwan's village/neighborhood chief is a rare hybrid**: they have the legal status of elected officials, specific statutory powers, budget subsidies, and 7,748 positions spread nationwide; yet they are positioned as "unsalaried," are not civil servants, are intensely localized, and have faint party colors. This hybrid is likely a unique result of inheriting the Japanese _baojia_ system and evolving through eighty years of democratization and autonomy.

## Points of Observation for 2026

Before the voting day on November 28, 2026, there are several indicators worth watching in the village/neighborhood chief elections:

**Will the proportion of independents remain high?**: In past elections, the independent rate has consistently been over 60%. Whether 2026 maintains this will test the phenomenon of "factions over parties" in Taiwan's grassroots politics.

**The "Young Chief" phenomenon in urban areas**: Recently, there have been cases in urban areas where 30–40-year-olds challenge incumbents in their 70s. These younger chiefs often use social media, focus on community building, and organize family-friendly activities—a different service model from traditional chiefs. We will see more of these challenges in 2026, especially in the metropolitan areas of Taipei, New Taipei, Taichung, and Taoyuan.

**Changes in the proportion of female chiefs**: Whether they can break through historical ceilings.

**Generational turnover in local factions**: Many current chiefs are second or third-generation successors (father to son, father to daughter, or husband and wife taking over). With many incumbents turning 70 or older by 2026, the speed and direction of this generational shift are worth observing.

**Vacancies in depopulating areas**: In rural areas with severe population outflow, such as Hualien, Taitung, Yunlin, Chiayi, Miaoli, and Nantou, some villages may see no candidates running at all. The CEC has tracked this phenomenon in previous elections—will it be more severe in 2026?

These are not mere news observations; they are structural observations. Village/neighborhood chief elections rarely make headlines, but their long-term trends speak to the pulse of Taiwan's grassroots governance.

---

## Conclusion: The Politics That Least Resembles "Politics"

The village/neighborhood chief is the **political position in Taiwan that least resembles "politics."**

There are no party debates, no policy white papers, no television advertisements, and no live-streamed policy presentations. It is one of 7,748 positions on a ballot—most people forget who they voted for as soon as they leave the polling station, yet every day, you will brush past your chief at the mouth of your alley.

Yet, this position is also **the most complete evidence of Taiwan's democratization**. From the colonial periphery of the Japanese _baojia_ system, to the post-war renaming and inheritance, to the direct elections of 1950, and finally the legal integration in 1999—at every historical node, an elected representative has existed at the most grassroots level. The eighty years of uninterrupted village/neighborhood elections are a fact of Taiwan's autonomy growing from the bottom up.

The ninth slot in the nine-in-one election is so ordinary it is almost invisible. But the simultaneous election of 7,748 elected leaders is a scale not found in other Asian democracies—**this is the density of Taiwan's grassroots autonomy**.

On the morning of November 28, 2026, when you walk into the polling station, you will receive nine differently colored ballots. The last one is usually for the village/neighborhood chief—the name on that ballot belongs to the person who has been helping you take out the trash at 5:30 AM, delivering lunch boxes to the elderly neighbor, or answering calls about a barking dog in the middle of the night for the past three terms.

That isn't politics. That is Taiwan's most fundamental, daily-operating, eighty-year-uninterrupted democratic infrastructure.
