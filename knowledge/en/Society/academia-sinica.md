In the winter of 1948, a shipment of wooden crates arrived in Yangmei, Taoyuan, and were stacked in a warehouse next to the railway station.

The crates belonged to the Institute of Historical Linguistics of Academia Sinica. The institute's founding director, Fu Ssu-nian, earned the nickname "Mr. Mover" because he had led the entire institute's archives through a long journey from Nanjing and Changsha to Kunming, Yunnan, and eventually Lizhuang, Sichuan, during the War of Resistance against Japanese Aggression[^1]. This trip in the winter of 1948 was yet another relocation of the same collection. Among the various crates from different agencies arriving in Taiwan at the same time, only those from the Institute of Historical Linguistics remained in Yangmei; the rest were transferred to a sugar factory warehouse in Taichung for storage[^2].

Few people crossed the sea with those crates. Academia Sinica's own institutional history website describes that year very briefly: "Only about 50 people from a few units, including the General Office, the Institute of Historical Linguistics, and the Institute of Mathematics, migrated to Taiwan with the government"[^3]. Fifty-odd people, a few units, and a borrowed warehouse—this was the first set of assets in Taiwan for an organization legally designated as the "highest academic research institution of the Republic of China"[^4].

> **30-Second Overview:** Academia Sinica was established in Shanghai in 1928; when it relocated to Taiwan with the government around 1949, only about fifty people remained. Today, it comprises 24 institutes, 9 research centers, and nearly 1,000 researchers. Its legal authority stems directly from the Organization Act of the Presidential Office, and it does not confer degrees or recruit students. The position provides "longevity": excavations at Yin Xu spanned ten years across fifteen separate digs; the Institute of Taiwan History took eleven years to transition from a preparatory office to a full institute. This same position also means the President selects the President of Academia Sinica from a list of three candidates, the budget is scrutinized item by item in the Legislative Yuan every year, and even whether to change an English name requires answering: "Is this a matter of national positioning?"

## The First Assets Brought to Taiwan, Temporarily Stored in a Railway Warehouse

Looking back twenty years, this institution's first meeting did not take place in some grand hall. In 1927, the proposal to establish Academia Sinica was passed at the Kuomintang Central Political Council Meeting. The Institute History website attributes the initiative to a group led by Cai Yuan-pei, while Wikipedia records that the proposal was submitted by Li Shih-tseng during the 74th meeting on April 17 of that year; these two versions have coexisted[^5]. The Organic Act was promulgated on November 9 of the same year. On April 23, 1928, the Nationalist Government specially appointed Cai Yuan-pei as President. On June 9, the first meeting of the Academy's affairs was held at the East Asia Restaurant in Shanghai, announcing its formal establishment[^5]. In a venue no larger than a restaurant private room, a national supreme academic institution was inaugurated. According to the Institute History website, during the early stages of the academy, "a total of ten research units were organized under extremely limited funding"[^3].

_The original appointment letter from the Nationalist Government specially appointing Cai Yuan-pei as President of Academia Sinica on April 23, 1928. Photographed at the former residence of Cai Yuan-pei in Shanghai. Photo: Unknown / Wikimedia Commons, Public domain_

Among the luggage brought by these fifty-odd people, there was one thing that was not packed in a box. Article 1 of the Organic Act of Academia Sinica states: "This Act is formulated in accordance with Article 17 of the Organic Act of the President of the Republic of China." Article 2 states: "Academia Sinica shall be the supreme academic research institution of the Republic of China, and its duties are as follows: 1. Humanities and scientific research; 2. Guidance, coordination, and encouragement of academic research; 3. Cultivation of high-level academic research talents."[^4]

While both are state-funded institutions, [the Institute of Mathematical Sciences and Engineering](/en/society/taiwan-design-research-institute/) falls under the Ministry of Economic Affairs, whereas the legal basis for Academia Sinica is the Organic Act of the President. It is not a university: it does not have the power to confer degrees, it does not recruit undergraduate students, and researchers are not required to teach. To offer doctoral programs, it relies on the Taiwan International Graduate Program (TIGP) established in 2002, which is co-organized with ten universities including NTU, NTHU, and NYCU; students receive a degree from the partner university along with a TIGP completion certificate[^6]. Today, its scale is listed on its English website as: "24 research institutes and 9 research centers, with almost 1,000 researchers"[^7].

In March 1948, Academia Sinica elected its first batch of Academicians, totaling 81 people: 28 in the Mathematics group, 25 in the Biology group, and 28 in the Humanities group. In September of the same year, the first meeting of Academicians was held in Nanjing[^8]. That list represented the top tier of the Chinese academic community at the time, but it dispersed just over a year later. Wikipedia summarizes that "most units of Academia Sinica and the majority of the first batch of Academicians remained in mainland China or chose to go abroad, with very few following to Taiwan"[^5]. Exactly how many accompanied the government to Taiwan remains a matter of debate, with different versions citing anywhere from 6 to 12 people. What is certain is that Wang Chung-hui arrived in Taiwan via Hong Kong in 1949 and passed away in Taipei in 1958; Wu Chih-hui passed away in Taipei in 1953; while Lin Ke-sheng, who was on the list, went to the United States in 1949 and never came to Taiwan[^9].

![Group photo of the first batch of Academicians of Academia Sinica in 1948, with dozens of scholars in robes and suits arranged in rows in front of a building](/article-images/society/academia-sinica-academicians-1948.webp)
_The first batch of Academicians posing for a group photo in Nanjing in September 1948. Of these 81 people, only a very small number followed to Taiwan just over a year later. Photo: Unknown / Wikimedia Commons, Public domain_

The "warehouse period" did not last long. In the summer of 1954, the first batch of buildings at the Nankang campus was completed, giving the institution its own address in Taiwan[^3]. The second meeting of Academicians was held in Taipei in 1957, and it wasn't until the third meeting in 1958 that the second batch of Academicians was elected[^8].

The presidency also continued. Hu Shih assumed office in April 1958. On February 24, 1962, while he was presiding over the fifth meeting of Academicians, the academy held a welcome reception that afternoon for new Academicians such as Jen Chih-kung. At 6:35 PM, he passed away suddenly from a heart attack during the reception[^10]. His burial site was later developed into Hu Shih Park following the natural terrain of the hill; today, elderly residents in Nankang still practice Tai Chi there.

## More Oracle Bones from a Single Pit Than the Total of Eleven Recorded Works

The most famous items in those crates came from Xiaotun Village, Anyang, Henan.

Between 1928 and 1937, the Institute of Historical Linguistics conducted fifteen large-scale archaeological excavations in the Yin Xu (Yinxu) area[^11]. Ten years, fifteen excavations, one institution. On June 12, 1936 (the 25th year of the Republic), the thirteenth excavation uncovered a pit in the western part of the Xiaotun palace district, designated YH127[^11]. This single pit yielded 17,096 inscribed oracle bones—whereas prior to this, the total number of oracle bones recorded in eleven works, including 《Tieyun Canggui》 (_Iron Clouds Hiding Turtles_), was only 9,913[^12]. A single pit produced more than the sum of eleven books.

It took eight years and twelve excavations to finally reach that one pit. Such a rhythm is almost impossible to fit into the calendar of an institution that must recruit students, offer courses, and deliver annual results. In most evaluation metrics, spending eight years without finding anything significant would be recorded as a failure; yet, for this pit, that very patience was the prerequisite for its existence.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> The common view is that Academia Sinica's success stems from talent and funding: finding the best people and providing the best conditions. This explanation isn't wrong, but it misses a third element provided by the system. An institution that does not need to recruit students, does not need to teach classes, and receives a budget from the state rather than tuition can treat "finding nothing for ten years" as a process rather than an accident. Funding can buy equipment, and talent can be headhunted, but only "the ability to wait" is granted by the system. This is why the controversies in every subsequent section revolve around the same question: why was this institution allowed to wait?

Within the same institution, another group is looking much further afield. In 2011, the U.S. National Science Foundation allocated a 12-meter ALMA North American prototype telescope to "a team led by the ASIAA and the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory (SAO)," which refers to the team co-led by Academia Sinica's Institute of Astronomy and Astrophysics and the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory[^13]. This telescope was modified and moved to Greenland, becoming the Greenland Telescope. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) officially positioned it as "the only submillimeter-wave astronomical observatory in the Arctic Circle"[^14]. Academia Sinica's Institute of Astronomy and Astrophysics is one of the 13 member institutions of the EHT collaboration; when the first image of a black hole was announced on April 10, 2019, Taipei was one of the six global locations for the simultaneous press conferences[^15].

There are also times of extreme speed. On March 9, 2020, Academia Sinica issued a press release: Yang An-suey's research team "produced the first batch of 46 strains of milligram-level monoclonal antibodies (IgG) targeting seven human coronavirus nucleocapsid protein antigens in just 19 days"[^16]. According to _Research Matters_, this compressed research that was originally estimated to take two months into just nineteen days[^17]. The results were subsequently delivered to nine manufacturers for use in diagnostic reagents—one of the most desperately needed items during the [first year of the pandemic](/en/society/taiwan-covid-pandemic-and-vaccines/), and the part of the [epidemic prevention system](/en/society/taiwan-public-health-epidemic-response/) that was closest to the laboratory at that time.

![The President inspecting the R&D results of the Academia Sinica Quantum Computing Special Topic Center, with researchers explaining near the instruments](/article-images/society/academia-sinica-quantum-computer-2024.webp)
_On January 29, 2024, the President visited Nangang to inspect the R&D results of the Academia Sinica Quantum Computing Special Topic Center. Oracle bones, black holes, and antibodies—all under the umbrella of the same institution. Photo: Presidential Office / Official Photo by Wang Yu Ching, CC BY 2.0_

One pit takes ten years to dig; a telescope takes years from allocation to being installed in the Arctic Circle; a batch of antibodies is made in nineteen days. While these three things might seem contradictory when placed in the same annual report, they are actually three different uses of the same kind of "margin": because they can afford to be slow, they have something ready when urgency strikes. What Yang An-suey's team produced in nineteen days was the realization of his "accumulated results and technology over decades"[^17].

## An Institution Born to Study China Begins to Study This Island

When it was founded in 1928, this institution's mission was to study China. It took a long time before it began studying the ground upon which it stands.

The turning point is noted on the history page of the Institute of Taiwan History: "In January of the 82nd year of the Republic (1993), Academician Chang Kwang-chih, convener of the Taiwan History Fieldwork Research Unit, returned to the country; seeing that the timing for establishing an institute was ripe, he convened relevant parties to discuss the establishment of the institute together" [^18]. On June 26 of the same year, the Presidential Office approved the establishment of the Preparatory Office for the Institute of Taiwan History; on July 10, Huang Fu-san, a professor in the Department of History at National Taiwan University, was appointed director of the preparatory office. The institute was officially inaugurated on July 1, 2004, with Chuang Ying-chang as its first director [^18]. It took eleven years to move from a preparatory office to a formal institute.

This is another way of using the same length of time: the former was spent on an eight-year exploratory phase without returns; this latter was spent pivoting the institutional consciousness, shifting from "we study that continent" to "we study this island beneath our feet." Pivoting is slower than digging a hole because it requires changing organizational structures, budgets, and personnel—it is much slower than simply moving a shovel.

_Academia Sinica's 2025 official image video is titled "Finding Who We Are Through Research." For an institution founded in Shanghai in 1928 to put "who we are" in its promotional title is, in itself, a footnote to this section._

This shift is also written in one person's resume. In Tsao Yung-ho's official academic biography, the education column contains only one line: "Graduated from Taipei State Taipei Second Middle School (1939)." The professional experience column reads "Adjunct Researcher, Institute of Sinology, Academia Sinica (1984–)," and the predecessor to that institute was the Sun Yat-sen Institute [^19]. In 1990, he published "Another Path for Taiwan History Research: The Concept of 'Taiwan Island History'" in the 15th issue of _Taiwan History Fieldwork Research Newsletter_, proposing a way to view this history using the island itself as the unit and the people living upon it as the subjects [^20]. In 1998, he was elected as an Academician of Academia Sinica for the 22nd term [^19]. A person whose highest level of education was middle school entered this list.

There is another line of research that takes treating this island as a subject even further—so far that it places it within the lineage of an entire language family. The widely accepted Robert Blust classification divides the Austronesian languages into ten branches, nine of which are distributed in Taiwan; the long-term comparative studies of Austronesian languages conducted by Lee Ren-kuei, an Academician at the Institute of Linguistics, Academia Sinica, are built upon this classification [^21]. In 2015, Chung Kuo-fang of the Academia Sinica Biodiversity Research Center served as the corresponding author for a study on the phylogeography of _Broussonetia papyrifera_ (paper mulberry) published in the _Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences_: most paper mulberry in the Pacific belongs to the haplotype cp-17, which evolved from the cp-9 and cp-16 haplotypes unique to Taiwan [^22]. These are pieces of evidence proposed by linguistics and genetics respectively; who proposed them and what the evidence is are all laid out in the paper. As for how this evidence should be told as a story about ancestors, that is a matter for the people being studied, and it is also the position that has been contested for decades in the [revitalization of indigenous languages](/en/society/taiwan-indigenous-education-and-language-revival/).

There is an even more recent turn. The Institute of Taiwan History published _White Memory: Interview Records of Political Victims and Their Families_, containing interviews with 29 respondents, totaling approximately 480,000 words [^23]. An institution that once only studied the faraway is today organizing the oral histories of [this island's own wounds](/en/history/taiwan-white-terror/).

## The List of Academicians Elected Every Two Years Moves Slower Than the World Outside

The system changes more slowly than social awareness, and this fact is written into law.

Article 5 of the Organic Act states: "Academicians of Academia Sinica shall be elected every two years by the Academicians' Meeting, with a maximum of forty members per election." Article 6 stipulates that candidates must first be nominated by universities, specialized societies, research institutions, or at least five academicians or councilors, and then be reviewed by the Council[^4]. Article 2 of the Regulations for the Election of Academicians divides the quota into four groups, with a maximum of ten members per group[^24]. With an election every two years and a maximum of forty names each time, this rhythm has continued from the original list of 81 people in 1948 to the present day.

Being an academician is not a job; once elected, one does not need to move to Nangang for work. Most continue to stay at their original universities or overseas research institutions. The authority granted to academicians by the Organic Act includes attending Academicians' Meetings, electing new academicians, and electing candidates for the President of Academia Sinica, but it does not come with a permanent desk within the organization[^4]. Taiwanese people use "Academician of Academia Sinica" as a status or rank, and this intuition is correct—it is just that the rank has nothing to do with whether one is employed by Academia Sinica.

> 💡 **Did you know?**
> The "Nth Academicians' Meeting" and the "Nth Generation of Academicians" are two different numbering systems that are often confused. The 1957 meeting in Taipei mentioned above is an example: the meeting was held, but no election took place—the official website states: "The 2nd Academicians' Meeting was held in Taipei in 1957, but no election of academicians was conducted at that time. The 2nd generation of academicians was elected during the 3rd Academicians' Meeting in 1958" ([Academia Sinica Academicians Section](https://www.sinica.edu.tw/tab/6)). A similar trap exists regarding the President: the official list of past presidents shows 10 individuals without numbering their terms, yet official press releases refer to Chen Chien-jen as the "13th President," because "term" counts the number of consecutive periods served; if the same person is re-elected, the count advances.

The 35th election in July 2026 elected 19 new academicians and 3 honorary academicians. Following the election, the total number of academicians increased to 302, consisting of 253 men, 49 women, and 18 honorary academicians[^25]. The gender structure of this term does not match the overall total: among the 19 new academicians, 8 are women, and all 3 honorary academicians are women, totaling 11—the highest number in years. All four new academicians in the Life Sciences Division are women[^26].

```tw-bars

*35th Generation New Academicians (19 total) | 42.1% | 8 Women
Source: Reports on the July 2026 35th Academicians' Election results from China Times, United Daily News, and Liberty Times
```

The longevity of the system has become an inertia here. The rhythm of one election every two years with a maximum of forty people per election means that the speed at which new blood dilutes the total is inherently slow: while nearly half of the most recently elected generation are women, women still make up only one-sixth of the entire list. No one is blocking anyone else. A list accumulated over seventy-eight years naturally requires a similar magnitude of change to be altered.

There is another point that is often read as a contradiction. The research institutes are divided into three major divisions: Physical Sciences, Life Sciences, and Humanities and Social Sciences. However, the election of academicians is divided into four groups—Physical Sciences, Engineering Sciences, Life Sciences, and Humanities and Social Sciences—according to Article 7 of the Organic Act[^4]. The additional Engineering Sciences group was added by an amendment to the Organic Act that went into effect on January 15, 2014. A press release regarding the draft passed by the Executive Yuan at the time stated it very directly: "Add the 'Engineering Sciences Division' for academicians, and revise the number of academician quotas to a maximum of forty"[^27]. There is no corresponding research institute for this group; academicians in the engineering field are attached to the existing research units within the three academic divisions. The two classification systems manage different things: one manages how physical units are organized, while the other manages how academician quotas are distributed.


The other two research centers (the Biomedical Translational Research Center and the Critical Issues Research Center) are interdisciplinary and do not belong to a single division, which accounts for the official website's statement of "24 institutes plus 9 centers"[^28]. The Organic Act also governs how the President is produced. Article 3 stipulates: "The President shall be elected from among three candidates chosen from the academicians by the Council, and then presented to the President [of the Republic of China] for selection and appointment... The term of the President is five years, and may serve one consecutive re-election." The Council consists of ex officio councilors and 30 to 50 appointed councilors; the appointed councilors are elected by the academicians and then presented to the President for appointment[^29]. The President's discretion is limited to those three names: he selects, rather than nominates. The current President, Chen Chien-jen, was appointed by President Lai Ching-te on January 2, 2026, with a term from June 21, 2026, to June 20, 2031, and assumed office on June 21[^30].

## Twenty-Six Consecutive Years, One Day Open Every October

An institution that does not recruit students, does not offer public courses, and usually only has a few buildings open during fixed hours, opens itself up entirely once a year.

The campus open house activities have been held annually in October since 1998; the official description states they have been "held for 26 consecutive years" [^31]. During the 2022 event, a reporter from Central News Agency described the scene as "crowded with visitors even in the rain, estimated at tens of thousands" [^58]. For the entire day, Academia Sinica Road Section 2 was packed with people; laboratories, specimen rooms, and server rooms all provided guided tours, with queues stretching from the main gates to the base of the research buildings.

![Academia Sinica Institute of Astronomy and Astrophysics Open House Day in 2012, citizens gathered around an outdoor booth, with a telescope set up nearby](/article-images/society/academia-sinica-open-house-2012.webp)
_The Institute of Astronomy and Astrophysics during the 2012 Campus Open House. One day a year; instruments that usually only appear in the acknowledgments section of research papers are brought out onto the grass. Photo: BreakdownDiode / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

In 2017, Academia Sinica also launched its own science communication media outlet. _Research Matters_ (研之有物) went online on April 6, 2017; its name is a homophone for the idiom "speaking with substance" (言之有物), and its mission is to turn the institution's research into illustrated content that the general public can read [^32]. One year and two months after its launch, it won an Excellence Award in Government Communication at the 11th Outstanding Public Relations Awards [^33]. The fact that a top academic institution needs to open a separate channel just to explain what it is doing speaks volumes about the distance between academia and the public. Occupying the same niche, [Taiwan's grassroots science communication community](/en/society/pansci/) preceded it by several years.

Some things are available every day. The _Taiwan Century Historical Map_ was developed by the GIS Special Topic Center of the Center for Human-Environment Studies; it includes multi-period layers ranging from fortress maps of the Japanese colonial period (1898–1904) to recent topographic maps produced by the Bureau of Censorship and Mapping. Users can select two or three layers to overlay, compare, and adjust transparency, and there is also a mobile app and WMTS services for integration with other systems [^34]. Anyone who opens it can see what their own plot of land looked like a hundred years ago. This is the accumulation of decades of digital archiving, presented as a single webpage.

There are also two free museums on campus: the Hu Shih Memorial Hall consists of three parts—Hu Shih's residence in Taiwan during his later years, an exhibition room, and Hu Shih Park—and is open from Tuesday to Saturday [^35]. The Institute of Historical Linguistics Exhibition Hall is open every Wednesday, Saturday, and Sunday, providing tours in both Chinese and English [^36]. For group visits, tours must be reserved in advance. Some of those items that were piled up in the Yangmei warehouse during the winter of 1948 are now displayed for free in the exhibition hall's showcases.

_The ecological pond at the Nangang Campus. For those living nearby, Academia Sinica is first a place, and only then an institution. Photo: Lysimachi / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

For residents of Nangang, all of this is even more part of daily life. The Sifen Creek flows down from the mountains of Nangang and winds around the perimeter of the campus; since 2011, the Taipei City Government has announced the closure of the stream to fishing to protect fish, making it the first stream in Taipei City to undergo such protection, maintained by river patrol teams composed of local residents [^37]. In Hu Shih Park, people practice Tai Chi; by the ecological pond, people push baby strollers. The things accumulated over time are brought out once a year. For the rest of the days, it remains in place as a patch of green, a stream, and a webpage.

## The Same Campus: Some Queue to Enter, While Others Say It Should Be Abolished

Being seen is not the same as being trusted. The same campus that people queue up to enter every October is also a place that has been repeatedly asked "on what basis" for the past decade. The very position that allows it to afford to wait has simultaneously given it two things. Because it does not teach or recruit students, it cannot produce ready-made performance metrics like student numbers or teaching hours; it can only answer "what these funds have bought" through its research itself. Its legal basis is written into the Organic Act of the Presidential Office, meaning its personnel and budget are not absorbed by any single ministry but are laid out before the President and the Legislative Yuan. Every year, the Legislative Yuan produces a budget evaluation report for it, providing itemized opinions: the 2025 report listed ten items, one of which scrutinized the newly added "Natural Gas Decarbonization Hydrogen-blended MW-class Hydrogen Power Generation System Construction and Testing Project" [^57]. Being able to afford to wait and having to explain oneself item by item every year are two sides of the same coin.

In March 2016, the unblinding of Hao Ding Biotech's breast cancer vaccine failed to meet expectations, leading to social questioning regarding the fact that the daughter of then-President Weng Chi-hui held shares in Hao Ding. On March 28, Weng resigned via an overseas telephone call and submitted a written resignation on the 29th. President Ma Ying-jeou required him to return to Taiwan first to provide an explanation; it was not until May 10 that the resignation was approved, with a letter issued on the same day stating: "Various sectors hold the view that 'resignation is appropriate' regarding the President's predicament caused by the investigation and search related to the Hao Ding case" [^39]. Official records of past presidents list his term ending in May 2016 [^40].

The judicial aspect must be split into two matters, as they are often conflated. The first is a corruption case. On January 9, 2017, the Shilin District Prosecutors Office indicted Weng Chi-hui and Hao Ding Chairman Chang Nien-tzu under the Anti-Corruption Act. On December 28, 2018, the Shilin District Court ruled both innocent in the first instance, reasoning that the prosecution failed to prove a meeting of minds regarding the specific contents of any bribe delivery. On January 21, 2019, the Shilin District Prosecutors Office announced it would not appeal, making the acquittal final [^41]. The second matter is an insider trading case involving five defendants, including Chang Nien-tzu, but Weng Chi-hui was not among them. That case saw a first-instance verdict on June 21, 2019, and the second instance upheld the innocence on January 26, 2021; the High Prosecutors Office decided not to appeal on February 24, 2021, finalizing the matter [^42]. The popular saying that "the Hao Ding case dragged on for five years" results from joining these two separate timelines into one.

Beyond criminal innocence, there is another line of inquiry. In July 2017, the Control Yuan passed an impeachment against Weng Chi-hui, proposed by Commissioners Chang Kuei-mei, Pao Tsung-ho, Liu Te-hsun, and Wang Mei-yu. The grounds for impeachment listed three points, one of which stated that as President, he had supervised the establishment of Academia Sinica's own conflict-of-interest avoidance principles, yet led by example in violating them [^43]. The Disciplinary Committee determined that he failed to report assets truthfully and failed to disclose his daughter's receipt of Hao Ding shares, issuing a reprimand. Following the reprimand, two paths diverged. Weng Chi-hui applied for a retrial three times, all of which were dismissed. On April 6, 2022, the Control Yuan—which had originally proposed the impeachment—reversed course and applied for a retrial on his behalf to the Disciplinary Court; as of today, no public result has been reached regarding this matter [^44].

Another case handled publicly within the institute involved academic ethics. An investigation determined that certain papers by Chen Ching-shih, a specially appointed researcher at the Institute of Biological and Biomedical Science, were falsified. Academia Sinica released the investigation report on March 30, 2018, and announced disciplinary actions on January 17, 2020: he is barred from applying for or collaborating on any academic activities or research projects within the institute for ten years, prohibited from using the institute's academic administrative resources, and required to return related prize money totaling NT$1.86 million [^45]. This case differed from the Hao Ding case in that it was an internal procedure determined and completed by the institution itself; there are no records in public data of him raising objections or applying for legal remedy.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> The resolution of public memory regarding this matter stops at the 2016–2017 slot. "The President of Academia Sinica was indicted" entered everyone's mind, but "acquitted on January 21, 2019" did not. This is not due to malice, but rather the physical nature of the news cycle: on the day of indictment, there were visuals, searches, and a resignation; on the day of acquittal, there was only a single piece of paper announcing the decision not to appeal. An institution uses ten years for one study, while the public forms an impression in two weeks, and that impression remains fixed. An institution that trades "length" for results is, in terms of reputation, defined by the shortest units of time.

The land-related timeline moves even slower. The National Biotechnology Research Park was built on the former site of the Nankang 202 Arsenal, occupying only a corner of that land. In May 2010, writer Chang Hsiao-feng knelt before President Ma Ying-jeou to plead for the preservation of the old trees and ponds there; the construction plan originally set for June 1 was halted by the Executive Yuan [^46]. On June 10, 2011, the Environmental Impact Assessment was conditionally passed, with requirements including green building certification and environmental education facility accreditation [^47]. The park opened on October 15, 2018; eight years elapsed from the kneeling to the ribbon-cutting. Today, the park's official website states it is "committed to maintaining a 14.19-hectare ecological protection zone and restoring a 4-hectare early Sanchongpu wetland restoration area" [^48]. A documentary by PTS _Our Island_ recorded that of the "25 hectares of land, the actual developed area is 9.6 hectares" [^49].

Looking into the courtyard, there is another sense of time. The term for a postdoctoral researcher is generally two years, after which they must reapply, totaling a maximum of four years. Applicants must apply within six years of obtaining their doctorate and must first obtain consent from an internal researcher to serve as an academic mentor; they can apply for research funds of up to NT$500,000 per year [^50]. An institution that can spend ten years excavating a single site provides its most junior researchers with a time scale of two-year terms, capped at four years.

Moving down another level, the sense of time changes again. In July 2022, a telephone interviewer (pseudonym "A-Xiang") from the Center for Humanities and Social Sciences Studies told the media that for many years, the Center had signed "contracting" agreements with some part-time interviewers, failing to provide labor insurance under the Labor Standards Act; employees had to bear the costs of labor insurance, the 6% labor pension contribution, and health insurance themselves. The background she provided was: in 2006, during Lee Yuan-tseh's tenure, a meeting decided to grant telephone interview specialists labor insurance benefits, but in 2014, an institute meeting resolved to switch to a contracting system. At the time, nearly 15 interviewers were preparing to seek compensation from the institute for overtime pay and unpaid labor pension contributions. The Center's response was: "This Center is actively handling the matter... we will act in good faith, consider their rights, and handle it appropriately in a legal and proper manner" [^51]. After that sentence, no public follow-up on this matter has been seen.

The name is another line that has not been completed in four years. On March 9, 2020, Legislator Fan Yun questioned the issue during a session in the Legislative Yuan and submitted a motion requiring Academia Sinica to draft a proposal to change its English name and report to the Legislative Yuan within three months, arguing that searching "Academia Sinica" on Google leads to the Chinese Academy of Sciences [^52]. The response from then-President Liao Chun-chih at the time was: "The name of Academia Sinica involves national positioning and policy; it is not something Academia Sinica can change unilaterally," and "Discussion can be opened, but a unilateral decision is not easy to make" [^52]. In March 2024, Academia Sinica Secretary-General Tseng Kuo-hsiang told the media: "This 'postponement' resolution represents that the majority of Academia Sinica colleagues do not believe there is a necessity for Academia Sinica to change its name" [^53]. The private organization Liberty Society suggested changing the English name to _National Academy of Taiwan_ [^54].

After the presidential appointment was finalized on January 2, 2026, voices also emerged within the Legislative Yuan. Kuomintang Legislator Wan Mei-ling explicitly questioned the generational transition: "The last three presidents of Academia Sinica... all took office at age fifty-eight; now, Chen Chien-jen, who is nearly seventy-five, will serve, which may run contrary to the practice of 'foresight and generational transition'"—Lee Yuan-tseh, Weng Chi-hui, and Liao Chun-chih were indeed all 58 when they took office. KMT Legislator Lee Yen-hsiu, however, believes Chen Chien-jen's academic standing and administrative experience are sufficient for the role, but "Chen Chien-jen's future challenge lies in 'politics'" [^55]. Being asked "on what basis" is also a process measured in years: from indictment to finality in the Hao Ding case took two years; from kneeling to opening at 202 took eight years; and from proposal to postponement regarding the name has taken four years.

> ⚠️ **Controversial Viewpoint**
> Chen Pei-che, a NTU professor and Academia Sinica Academician, argues that Academia Sinica should be "de-leveled," and even its very existence should be re-examined. His reasoning is: this institution was established in the early years of the Republic and is a system left over from the authoritarian era; it receives stable government budgets and abundant resources, yet still competes with various universities for National Science and Technology Council research funding, creating an uneven playing field. He advocates for preserving the honorary status of Academicians while returning research functions to national universities. One of his original remarks was "Academia Sinica must be abolished!" He also criticized Chen Chien-jen and the outgoing Liao Chun-chih for lacking "the integrity and character to face power" ([reprinted from a Feng Media interview with China Times](https://www.storm.mg/article/11143117)). In another interview, he placed the question of who the President is after the question of abolition, believing the latter is what Taiwanese society and academia should be discussing more [^56]. Public voices speaking to the level of "abolition" are currently concentrated on him alone. The sense of relative deprivation from [the university side](/en/society/taiwan-higher-education-expansion-and-decline/)—asking "on what basis can it afford to wait"—has always existed, but there is no named representative voice, which makes this fault line appear smaller on the surface than it actually is.

## A Place That Can Treat Decades as a Single Project Unit

In the winter of 1948, what was piled up in that warehouse in Yangmei consisted of several dozen crates and about fifty people. Seventy-eight years separate that moment from today's Nankang campus, home to 24 institutes, 9 research centers, and nearly a thousand researchers.

What has been gained during this period is a form of luxury: the ability to treat "decades" as a single project unit. It took ten years to excavate Yin Xu; it took eleven years to prepare the Institute of Taiwan History; and the evidence for Austronesian peoples spans from linguistics all the way to that 2015 paper on _Breynia retusa_. The way to afford this kind of longevity is to place oneself in a position where one does not need to teach, does not need to recruit students, and whose legal basis is written directly into the Organic Act of Academia Sinica. In this same position, its president is selected by the President from three candidates; its budget is scrutinized item by item every year in the Legislative Yuan; and even whether or not to change its English name requires first answering questions regarding the nation's positioning. Being able to afford the wait, and having to constantly answer "on what authority," are two sides of the same design.

On that Saturday in October, crowds still walk through the main gate on Section 2, Academia Sinica Road. For the remaining 300-plus days of the year, the building opens at fixed times, and group tours must be reserved in advance. Most of the work happening behind those doors will likely take several years before anyone can judge whether it was truly worth it—and whether it is worth it is ultimately decided by the very people who walk through the door.
