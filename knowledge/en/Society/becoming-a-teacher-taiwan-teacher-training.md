> **30-second overview:** On February 7, 1994, the Legislative Yuan passed the Teacher Education Act on its third reading, ending the half-century-old single-track normal school system and opening teacher education to general universities through education programs[^1]. The optimism at the time: allowing people from more diverse backgrounds to enter the education field. Thirty years later, the reality is cold: the original 9 universities of education, after a wave of mergers, were down to **3** by 2024 (National Taipei University of Education, National Tsing Hua University [which absorbed the former Hsinchu University of Education], and National Taichung University of Education), with universities of education accounting for approximately 95% of higher education merger cases[^2]. In the 2020 academic year, teacher education institutions nationwide fell short of enrollment targets by **1,551 students**, a historic high[^3]. In February 2018, the Teacher Education Act was amended again, changing the sequence from "internship first, then exam" to "exam first, then internship"[^4]. After the new system took effect, the 2023 teacher certification exam pass rate dropped to **51.64%** (9,072 examinees, 4,685 passed), a new low since the new question format[^5]; the 2024 pass rate was **52.2%** (10,377 registered, 5,022 passed), the second-lowest in four years[^6]. Among students who entered the teacher education system between 2011 and 2018, **42% quit before completing their internship or obtaining a teaching certificate**[^7]. A National Taiwan Normal University survey of teacher education students nationwide found **47% saying "uncertain about teaching in the future, but will take the courses first"**[^7]. The 1994 reform aimed to solve the "single background" problem, but the 2024 problem isn't single backgrounds—it's that **fewer people want to teach, and those who remain are trained further and further from the field**.

## 1994's Opening: From Single-Track to Multi-Track

Before 1994, Taiwan's elementary school teachers came almost entirely from normal junior colleges (later upgraded to normal colleges, then universities of education), while secondary school teachers came from normal universities. This "single-track normal school system" was a system carried over from 1949, with a clear design: **the state guaranteed graduates placement in schools, students received government funding, and after completing their service obligation they became tenured teachers**[^8].

This system allowed Taiwan to rapidly cultivate sufficient teaching manpower in the postwar decades. But it also had a structural problem: **teachers' backgrounds were too homogeneous**. Students entering through the normal school system mostly decided "I want to be a teacher" in high school, then spent the next six or seven years entirely within that system, exposed mainly to educational theory and homogeneous peers. This homogeneity was increasingly questioned in the 1980s: Should teachers come from more diverse backgrounds? Is someone who has only studied in the normal school system really better suited to teach children than someone who studied engineering, medicine, or literature before turning to teaching?

On February 7, 1994, the Legislative Yuan passed the Teacher Education Act on its third reading[^1], formally ending the single-track system. The new system allowed any general university to establish "education programs," where students could take education credits alongside their major, complete an internship, pass the teacher certification exam, and obtain a teaching certificate[^1]. Government-funded student quotas were drastically reduced.

On the surface, this was an opening reform. In substance, it turned one thing from "state guarantee" into "market competition": **formerly, normal school graduates had guaranteed placement; now, everyone who wants to teach must find their own school, pass the certification exam themselves, and compete for vacancies**. The effects of this change were not immediate. It took about twenty years to fully ferment.

## The Merged Universities of Education

After the Teacher Education Act passed in 1994, Taiwan's teacher education system underwent not just internal reform but a complete rewriting of its physical structure.

The clearest indicator is the fate of the universities of education. In the early 1990s, Taiwan had **9 universities of education** (or their predecessor normal colleges)[^2]:

National Taipei University of Education, Taipei Municipal University of Education, National Hsinchu University of Education, National Taichung University of Education, National Taiwan Normal University, National Tainan University of Education, National Chiayi University of Education, National Taitung University of Education, National Hualien University of Education.

By 2024, this number had fallen to **3**[^2]:

- **National Taipei University of Education** (remained independent)
- **National Taichung University of Education** (remained independent)
- **National Tsing Hua University** (the former National Hsinchu University of Education merged into NTHU in 2016)

The remaining universities of education were merged one after another: Taipei Municipal University of Education merged into University of Taipei in 2013; National Tainan University of Education became part of National University of Tainan; National Chiayi University of Education became National Chiayi University; National Taitung University of Education became National Taitung University; National Hualien University of Education merged into National Dong Hwa University[^2]. **Universities of education accounted for approximately 95% of Taiwan's higher education merger cases**[^2].

This merger was not an active policy choice for teacher education; it was the downstream effect of declining birthrates + teacher education opening. When teacher education shifted from "normal school monopoly" to "multiple universities can offer it," the normal school system's unique status disappeared. When declining birthrates reduced education demand, normal-school-background graduates couldn't find positions. When enrollment numbers fell below a threshold, the university system naturally moved toward merger or absorption. **An open reform dismantled the entire physical infrastructure of teacher education thirty years later**.

This is not alarmism. If a high school student in 2025 decides "I want to be an elementary school teacher," the number of "universities specializing in training elementary teachers" they can choose from is only 2 (National Taipei University of Education, National Taichung University of Education). This choice pool has shrunk by more than three-quarters compared to their parents' generation.

## Unfilled Teacher Education Classes

Taiwan's second warning signal for teacher education began in the 2010 academic year. That year, teacher education institutions nationwide saw significant enrollment shortfalls for the first time[^3].

Things did not improve afterward. According to the Ministry of Education's Teacher Education Statistical Yearbook, every year since the 2010 academic year has seen enrollment shortfalls, with the 2020 academic year reaching a historic high of **1,551 unfilled seats**[^3]. This means the total seats offered by all teacher education institutions in Taiwan exceeded actual applicants by 1,551.

This figure is easily misread as "people don't want to be teachers anymore." The reality is more complex. The number of people taking Taiwan's civil service exams (high and ordinary) hasn't noticeably declined, nor has the number entering medical school. **It's not that young people don't want stable jobs; it's that they're unwilling to equate "stable job" with "being a teacher."**

A twenty-year-old university student at a crossroads can choose "add 26 credits of education program + half-year internship + teacher certification exam + if no vacancy, become a substitute teacher," or "enter the labor market directly or continue to graduate school." The former is a clear but long road; the latter is a vague but flexible road. Over the past decade, more and more people choose the latter.

## 42% Midstream Departure

Those who enter teacher education don't necessarily stay to the end.

According to the Ministry of Education's Primary and Secondary School Teacher Database analysis, among students who entered the teacher education system between 2011 and 2018, **42% chose to quit before completing their internship or obtaining a teaching certificate**[^7].

This 42% is the heaviest number in Taiwan's teacher education system. It means: for every 100 university students who decide "I want to be a teacher" and start taking education credits, 42 stop halfway before they actually get their teaching certificate.

Why? The teacher education students surveyed by National Taiwan Normal University answered bluntly: **47% said "uncertain about teaching in the future, but will take the courses first."** This proportion at NTNU itself was **58%**[^7]. Nearly half of teacher education students treat this qualification as a fallback. Good to have, no loss if not.

This mindset itself is a side effect of the 1994 reform. Under the old single-track system, entering the normal school system meant deciding to be a teacher. After opening to diversity, entering a teacher education program just means "keeping an option open." If half the students in a nation's teacher education system only treat it as an option, then it's not cultivating teachers—it's providing insurance.

## 2018's "Exam First, Internship Later": Another Rewrite

On February 1, 2018, the Teacher Education Act was amended and took effect[^4]. The core of this amendment was **adjusting the sequence of the "Teacher Qualification Exam" and "Education Internship."**

**Old system process** (applicable to those who obtained teacher education student status before January 31, 2018):

1. Complete pre-service teacher education courses
2. Complete education internship
3. Obtain certificate of completion of pre-service teacher education
4. Teacher qualification exam
5. Obtain teaching certificate
6. Participate in teacher selection exams

**New system process** (applicable to those who obtained teacher education student status on or after February 1, 2018):

1. Complete pre-service teacher education courses
2. Obtain certificate of completion of pre-service teacher education
3. **Teacher qualification exam** (exam first)
4. **Complete education internship** (internship later)
5. Obtain teaching certificate
6. Participate in teacher selection exams

The new system's logic: **Let teacher education students pass the exam first to confirm basic competency, then go intern. Avoid the situation where they finish internship only to fail the certification exam, wasting half a year**[^4]. Sounds reasonable. But in practice this change had a hidden side effect: it **shifted the pressure of the certification exam from "post-internship verification" to "pre-internship threshold,"** turning what was originally part of the curriculum into a "reward for passing the exam."

Transitional provisions gave students already in teacher education a ten-year buffer, allowing them to follow the old rules until January 31, 2028[^4]. But for students entering teacher education after 2018, the new system is the only path.

## Certification Pass Rate Falls to 52%

Six years after the new system's implementation, problems surfaced.

**2023 Teacher Qualification Exam pass rate: 51.64%** (9,072 examinees, 4,685 passed)[^5]. Down over 10 percentage points from the previous year's 62.03%. United Daily News reported at the time: "New-format teacher certification pass rate hits new low; question design sparks controversy."[^5] University professors publicly questioned the stability of the exam questions, noting that students who fail cannot proceed to internship, may have to switch careers or spend another year preparing, potentially affecting teacher reserves[^5].

**2024 Teacher Qualification Exam pass rate: 52.2%** (10,377 registered, 5,022 passed)[^6]. Second-lowest in four years. Flip Education's report put it coldly: "5,022 obtain internship tickets"[^6]. That word "ticket" is precise—under the new system, the certification exam is not just qualification recognition; it's the ticket to internship. No ticket, no internship.

**A 52% pass rate means**: of every two university students who prepared for four years in teacher education, one is blocked at the final gate. This ratio might not be so glaring for the bar exam or medical licensing exam, but for a teacher education system "already facing enrollment shortfalls," it's a double squeeze—**fewer people want in, and half of those who do get blocked by the exam**.

The Ministry of Education's public response to the declining pass rate: the certification exam is a "moderate difficulty" criterion-referenced test, with historical pass rates around 50–60%[^5]. Technically correct, but it sidesteps a deeper question: **When the pass rate drops from 62% to 51% in the two years right after the new question format launched, is it really "moderate difficulty," or a side effect of the policy transition?**

## Government-Funded Student Provisions: The Final Link to the Remote Areas Act

In February 2024, the Ministry of Education amended the "Regulations on Government-Funded Scholarships and Placement Services for Teacher Education"[^9]. Government-funded students are those who, under the Teacher Education Act, receive government funding for teacher education and must serve in remote or special area schools after graduation[^9].

The amended regulations stipulate:

- Government-funded students receive **4 years of funding** (tuition, housing, living stipend)[^9]
- After graduation, they must serve in remote or special area schools, with **continuous service of no less than 6 years** at the assigned school[^9]
- During the funding period, they must complete at least **24 credits** of education professional or specialized courses[^9]
- The period during which funded students cannot pursue further degrees during their service obligation was **reduced from 4 years to 3 years**[^9]
- New provision: parental leave or military service with leave without pay counts as continuous service, but actual service must still be no less than 6 years[^9]

The significance of this regulation lies not in viewing it alone. It forms a **complete chain from teacher education to remote areas** with Article 5 of the [Remote Area Schools Education Development Act](/en/society/remote-area-schools-education-act/) "six-year appointment restriction":

1. A university student aspiring to be a remote-area teacher enters teacher education through the government-funded channel → receives 4 years of funding
2. After graduation, assigned to a remote school → serves continuously for 6 years per the funding regulations
3. If they entered through the remote area school selection → add the Act's Article 5 six-year appointment restriction

**Sum of three regulations**: From entering university to being able to leave a remote school, a government-funded student needs a minimum of about 10 years. This is the state's contract with teachers willing to go remote: **We give you 4 years of funding, but you must repay the remote areas with the next 6 years**.

But an awkward reality in 2024: **Among new students entering the teacher education system, how many are willing to take this path?** According to Global Views Monthly, the first university "Technical-Vocational Teacher Education Government-Funded Class" in 2024 drew a **candid admission from an NTNU professor that they were not optimistic**, because the technical-vocational teacher education track's appeal is even weaker[^10]. This story concretely illustrates one thing: even with the state offering complete funding + appointment guarantees, it still can't find enough people to enter. **The 2024 problem isn't "they enter remote areas and then leave," it's "no one enters in the first place."**

## 91% Satisfaction vs 23% Dissatisfaction

The most interesting contradiction lies in quality evaluation.

OECD's TALIS 2018 (Teaching and Learning International Survey) results: **91% of Taiwan's surveyed teachers believed their pre-service and in-service training had a positive effect on their teaching**, higher than the TALIS participating countries' average of 82%[^11]. This number sounds good. Taiwan's teacher education is more affirmed by teachers than the international average.

But open NTNU's "Teacher Education Longitudinal Tracking Database," and the picture flips: among 1,203 elementary school administrative supervisors surveyed nationwide in the 2019 academic year, **23.3% rated elementary teachers' performance in university teacher education courses as "unsatisfactory"**[^12]. In other words, nearly one-quarter of supervisors mentoring new teachers directly rated teacher education quality as unqualified.

**Why do two surveys of the same system yield opposite conclusions?** The difference lies in who is asked and what is asked. TALIS asks all in-service teachers, including many who received teacher education over a decade ago. Their satisfaction is based on the general impression of "is what I learned back then still useful today." NTNU asks current supervisors of new teachers, evaluating "are this batch of young teachers just entering schools actually prepared." The former is nostalgic satisfaction; the latter is real-time dissatisfaction.

This gap exposes a systemic problem in Taiwan's teacher education: **The system is evolving, but the direction of evolution is drifting further from the field's needs.**

## The Three Things New Teachers Lack Most

NTNU's 2017 academic year survey, synthesizing feedback from 1,000 school supervisors and 947 teacher peers, identified the **three competencies new teachers most need to strengthen**[^12]:

**Competency-oriented teaching ability** (the core competency of the 2019 Curriculum Guidelines, but many teacher education courses still linger in traditional knowledge transmission); **Differentiated and remedial teaching ability** (designing multi-level instruction for students of varying proficiency); **Counseling for special education students** (with inclusive education, every regular class now has special needs students).

These three competencies happen to be the directions where the education field has changed most over the past twenty years. The 2019 Curriculum Guidelines launched in 2019 requiring competency-oriented teaching; inclusive education has been promoted since the 1990s but intensified in recent years; differentiated teaching is the inevitable response to widening learning gaps.

But teacher education courses haven't fully caught up. The reason isn't that professors don't care—it's that **Article 7 of the Teacher Education Act stipulates that teacher education universities enjoy curriculum autonomy**[^1]. Each teacher education university can independently plan course content and credit allocation. This autonomy was originally meant to give different teacher education institutions flexibility, but the result is loss of horizontal consistency in curriculum design—the same elementary education program at NTNU, Dong Hwa, and Pingtung University may teach very different things.

[Teach for Taiwan (TFT)](/en/society/teach-for-taiwan/) mentions a detail: **TFT's six-week intensive training is sometimes criticized as "too short," but in practice it covers field competencies to an extent comparable to four years of university teacher education**[^13]. This comment seems exaggerated on the surface, but the problem it points to is real: if four years of teacher education lack intensive field connection, that time doesn't translate into corresponding competencies.

## Case Library Dominated by Urban Star Schools

There's another structural bias from "representativeness of field experience."

A key design in teacher education courses is "inviting field teachers to share practical experience." These talks, workshops, and case discussions form teacher education students' first-hand impression of the education field. But **the vast majority of invited teachers come from star schools in the Taipei–New Taipei metro area**. A teacher education student interviewed in TFT, Chi-wen, put it bluntly: "Teacher education courses invite field teachers to share or serve as co-preparation teachers, but these teachers are mostly from tough schools in the Taipei–New Taipei area or star teachers. These experiences mostly come from urban star schools, not all field voices."[^7]

The consequence of this bias is direct: if a teacher education graduate is assigned to a remote area, a high-needs school, or an Indigenous key school, their psychological preparation is near zero. Because their case library contains none of these fields.

[Indigenous Education and Language Revitalization in Taiwan](/en/society/taiwan-indigenous-education-and-language-revival/) describes full-immersion heritage language teaching; [Remote Area Schools Education Development Act Explained](/en/society/remote-area-schools-education-act/) discusses multi-grade teaching and 5:1 student-teacher ratios; [Taiwan Rural Education](/en/society/taiwan-rural-education/) covers community engagement methodologies—**these are all things remote-area teachers must know, but mainstream teacher education courses barely teach any of them**[^14].

The result? A teacher education student prepares for four years, gets their teaching certificate, is assigned to an Indigenous tribal elementary school in Laiyi Township, Pingtung, and discovers half of what they know is irrelevant to this field. They can only learn on the job, burn out after a year or two, leave, and get replaced by another new teacher equally unfamiliar with the field.

This is exactly the claim made at the end of that TFT thinkings article: Taiwan needs **a more "customized," more contextually grounded open innovative teacher education system for remote areas**[^15]. Not overhauling the entire four-year curriculum, but designing part of the teacher education track specifically for remote field contexts, so that some teachers willing to go remote start accumulating relevant competencies from university. This proposal has academic consensus but remains slow on the policy front.

## The More Complete the System, the Further from the Field

Back to the contradiction at the article's start: The 1994 reform originally aimed to make teacher education more open, more diverse, closer to the real world. Thirty years later, the result is:

Teacher education channels multiplied, but can't fill seats (1,551 shortfall in 2020); diverse backgrounds enter, but 42% quit midstream; courses have autonomy, but lost horizontal consistency; teachers overall satisfied with teacher education (91%), but field supervisors dissatisfied with new teachers (23.3%); case library enriched, but biased toward urban star schools; universities of education merged from 9 to 3, even physical training spaces disappearing; 2018's second major amendment changed "internship first, exam later" to "exam first, internship later," resulting in certification pass rates dropping to 52%, a recent low; 2024's amended government-funded regulations strengthened remote-area linkage, but even NTNU professors aren't optimistic about the technical-vocational government-funded class.

**The more complete the system, the further from the field.** This isn't reform failure—it's that the reform direction was misaligned with the problems needing solving. The 1994 reform wanted to solve the "single background" problem, but Taiwan's 2020s education problems aren't single backgrounds—they're that field diversity exceeds what any single training system can automatically handle. Competency-oriented curriculum, inclusive education, learning gaps, rural teacher shortages, Indigenous education, cross-cultural families—these challenges aren't solved by a "more open teacher education system" alone; they need **targeted training tracks designed for each field context**.

Taiwan is only starting this discussion a bit late. The Ministry of Education's "Remote Area Teacher Special Program," TFT's two-year program, and some teacher education universities' "local teacher education" experiments are early attempts in this direction. They're all still at the margins, not yet part of mainstream teacher education.

A 2024 joint public hearing by civil society groups made a more direct demand: **The gaps in the complete teacher education support system must be filled**[^16]. Demands include strengthening remote-area contextual teaching at the teacher education stage, expanding government-funded channels, reforming certification exam questions, evaluating the "exam first, internship later" system, etc. This is the starting point for the Act's teacher education supporting reforms, but realization is still far off.

The birth of a teacher, from the 1994 opening reform to the 2026 52% certification pass rate, this path has been walked into a circle—openness didn't solve the problem, because the problem wasn't "who can enter," but "can those who enter handle the real field." The harder question for the next decade.

## Further Reading

- [Teach for Taiwan (TFT)](/en/society/teach-for-taiwan/) — TFT's "Two-Year Teacher Program" is a parallel experiment to the current teacher education system: six weeks training + two years in the field + 500 hours of in-service training, directly moving the teacher education site to remote areas. Relationship with the Act: the Act authorizes NGO entry; TFT is the largest practitioner.
- [Remote Area Schools Education Development Act Explained](/en/society/remote-area-schools-education-act/) — The Act's Article 5 six-year appointment restriction, Article 7 special appointment teachers, Article 16 five-party cooperation are all downstream remedies for "teacher education isn't enough." Teacher education and the Act must be read together to be complete. The 2024 government-funded regulations' 6-year continuous service requirement directly connects to Article 5.
- [Indigenous Education and Language Revitalization in Taiwan](/en/society/taiwan-indigenous-education-and-language-revival/) — The 1.1% Indigenous teacher field dilemma, half stemming from the teacher education system lacking courses designed for tribal contexts. The Qamak model of filling gaps through personal effort is the exception, not the system.
- [Taiwan Rural Education](/en/society/taiwan-rural-education/) — The concentric circles framework of rural education explains teacher education as one of the most upstream links. If upstream loosens, downstream can't catch up no matter how hard it tries.
- Education Systems and Exam Culture — Exam competition culture affects students' volunteer application priorities; teacher education has been sliding down this value ranking in recent years.
- Alternative Schools — The control group for institutional teacher education: the Alternative Education Carnival handles "what kind of education" from outside the system; Su Yang-chih's "principal" identity exists in a parallel universe to the formal teacher education system.
