> **30-Second Overview:** On the streets of Taiwan, the neon lights of betel nut stalls and signs for _jiebingshui_ (iced water) form the first impression for many of this land. However, this seemingly ordinary green fruit is not only a sacred offering and a token of love in Indigenous cultures but was also the "miasma-cleansing pill" used by Han settlers during the early colonization of Taiwan. Today, betel nut has become a focal point of social controversy due to its health hazards and environmental impact, weaving a complex narrative of class, land, and culture.

### Betel Nut: From Sacred Fruit to Carcinogen—A Microcosm of Class and Land within a Single Seed

In Taiwan, many long-distance drivers, on hot afternoons, pull over at roadside betel nut stalls flickering with neon lights to buy a pack of betel nut and two bottles of _jiebingshui_ (iced water). This seemingly ordinary transaction represents a unique landscape of Taiwanese society and the complex journey of a fruit spanning centuries—from sacred offering to carcinogen. In 197le, the "Shuangdong Betel Nut Stall" in Caotun, Nantou, pioneered the era of the "Betel Nut Beauties," forever intertwining the story of this green fruit with Taiwan's working class, cultural shifts, and health and environmental issues.

#### A Cultural Capsule: Betel Nut and Taiwan's Indigenous Peoples

The history of chewing betel nut can be traced back five thousand years, and its connection to Taiwan spans over four hundred years. In the cultures of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples, betel nut is not merely a stimulant; it carries rich social and spiritual significance. Yang Cheng-hsien, an associate professor in the Department of Indigenous Studies and Social Work at National Dong Hwa University, points out that betel nut serves as a "cultural capsule" for Indigenous peoples [^1].

For the Amis people, betel nut is a token of love. During the "Lovers' Night" of the Harvest Festival, women present betel nut to men they admire; if the man accepts and chews it on the spot, it signifies mutual affection. This system of "matrilocality" makes betel nut an important medium for connecting emotions and families [^1]. The Siraya people view betel nut as a sacred object for communicating with ancestral spirits. Duan Hong-kun, head of the Jibei Shuwen Studio, states that betel nut (Siraya: _Abiki_) is an indispensable offering when priests pray to _Alid_ (ancestral spirits), even serving as a ritual implement during ceremonies; its sacred status is comparable to the "Peach Blossom Sword" of the Han people [^1]. Duan also mentions that in Siraya ceremonies, betel nut that has been used for ritual purposes cannot be discarded carelessly; it is placed on rooftops or in remote areas, demonstrating its sanctity [^1].

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The multiple roles of betel nut in Indigenous cultures challenge the modern society's singular "unhealthy" label. It is not merely a substance but a symbol that maintains community, transmits faith, and expresses emotion, with meanings far exceeding mere oral gratification.

#### The Han "Miasma-Cleansing Pill" and the Laborer's "Green Gold"

When Han immigrants arrived in Taiwan during the Qing Dynasty, facing a hot, humid environment prone to miasma, betel nut became an aid in adapting to the new land. Chewing betally nut induces body heat and sweating, which was viewed as a "miasma-cleansing pill" (_xizhangdan_) to expel dampness and prevent disease [^2]. This functional need allowed betel nut to take root in Han society, becoming an essential item for social interaction and wedding gifts. Even during conflict mediation, the party at fault might use betel nut as an expression of apology [^2].

Entering the latter half of the 20th century, Taiwan's economy took off, with a massive influx of blue-collar workers into infrastructure projects. Due to its stimulating effects, betel nut became a daily necessity for the working class, including truck drivers and construction workers. Consequently, betel nut stalls emerged and reached their peak in the 1990s; the cultivation area in Taiwan once reached as high as 65,000 hectares—second only to rice—with an annual output value reaching 8 billion TWD, earning it the title of "Green Gold" [^3]. Research by Professor Wu Chia-chun of the Department of Soil and Water Conservation at National Ping Tung University indicates that leaving one hectare of land completely bare for a year would result in the erosion of 294 tons of soil—about three centimeters of topsoil—and the soil retention capability of betel nut plantations is far inferior to that of forests [^3].

#### Betel Nut Beauties: A Unique Highway Landscape and Social Controversy

In 1976, the "Shuangdong Betel Nut Stall" in Caotun, Nantou, pioneered the "Betel Nut Beauty" sales model, using women dressed in light clothing to sell betel nut, which rapidly attracted customers' attention. In the 90s, the culture of Betel Nut Beauties reached its zenith, becoming a unique feature of Taiwan's highways. They were not just salespeople but also a source of solace for many blue-collar laborers during long working hours, representing an aesthetic of survival among the lower class and an expression of the free economy. However, Betel Nut Beauties were often accompanied by social moral controversies, workplace safety issues, and criticisms regarding the objectification of women [^4].

Alongside the Betel Nut Beauties, another "hidden bestseller" at betel nut stalls is _jiebingshui_ (iced water). For drivers and workers laboring under the scorching sun, iced water provides more than just thirst relief; it serves as a tool for physical cooling, tied to the body or placed in vehicles to provide brief respite. This seemingly simple product reflects the survival wisdom and needs of Taiwan's blue-collar laborers under harsh conditions [^5]. Some netizens have shared that in auto repair shops or construction sites made of corrugated iron where temperatures reach nearly 40°C, iced water is an "oasis in the desert" [^5].

#### The Double Cost to Health and the Environment

However, behind the prosperity of betel nut lies a heavy cost to health and the environment. Medical research has confirmed that chewing betel nut is a primary cause of oral cancer. In Taiwan, approximately 2,500 to 3,000 people die from oral cancer annually, and up to 90% of oral cancer patients have a habit of chewing betel nut. The betel nut nut itself has been classified by the World Health Organization as a Group 1 carcinogen; even without the addition of lime or _laoye_ (areca leaf), it remains carcinogenic [^6].

Regarding the environment, the shallow root system of the betel nut tree means its soil retention capability is far inferior to deep-rooted species when planted on hillsides. In 1994, Professor Chen Hsin-hsiung of the Department of Forestry at National Taiwan University proposed the "Betel Nut National Ruin Theory," pointing out that single-layer betel nut forests easily cause soil erosion and land degradation, exacerbating debris flows during typhoons and heavy rains [^3]. Although some studies suggest that betel nut is not the sole factor in soil erosion—for instance, some argue that "all economic crops have shallow roots, but compared to others, the betel nut tree is actually one of the best economic crops for soil and water conservation" [^7]—its negative environmental impact remains an indisputable fact. Although the government once promoted programs to convert betel nut plantations into forests, these efforts faced heavy resistance due to the livelihoods of farmers [^3].

#### Transformation and the Future: Swinging Between Tradition and Modernity

Facing the dual pressures of health and the environment, attitudes toward betel nut in Taiwanese society are gradually shifting. The proportion of the population chewing betel nut is declining annually, and the industry faces transformation. The draft "Betel Nut Health Hazard Prevention Act" announced by the Ministry of Health and Welfare will impose stricter regulations on the sale and advertising of betel nut, which also foreshadows the potential end of the era of the Betel Nut Beauties [^4].

However, for many Indigenous tribes, the cultural significance of betel nut remains deeply rooted in their hearts. How to achieve a balance between respecting traditional culture and maintaining public health and environmental sustainability is a challenge Taiwan must face. The story of betel nut is not just about the rise and fall of a single fruit, but a microcosm of Taiwan's continuous adaptation and reflection during the process of modernization; it reminds us that complex and subtle links exist between culture, economy, and the environment. This fruit is both a witness to history and a microcosm of the contradictions and tensions in contemporary Taiwanese society.
