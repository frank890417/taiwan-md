> **30-Second Overview:** On the streets of Taiwan, the neon lights of betel nut stalls and signs for "frozen water" (_jiebing shui_) form the first impression for many visitors to this land. However, this seemingly ordinary green fruit is not only a sacred offering and token of affection in Indigenous cultures but was also the "miasma-cleansing pill" used by Han settlers during the early stages of cultivation in Taiwan. Today, betel nut has become a focal point of social controversy due to its health hazards and environmental impact, weaving together complex narratives of class, land, and culture.

### Betel Nut: From Sacred Fruit to Carcinogen—A Microcosm of Class and Land within a Single Seed

In Taiwan, many long-distance drivers, on hot afternoons, will pull over at a neon-lit betel nut stall by the roadside to buy a pack of betel nuts and two bottles of "frozen water." This seemingly ordinary transaction is actually a unique feature of Taiwan's social landscape, representing a complex journey of a single fruit spanning centuries—from sacred offering to carcinogen. In 1976, the "Shuangdong Betel Nut Stall" in Caotun, Nantou, pioneered the "Betel Nut Beauty" (_binglang xishi_) era, forever intertwining the story of this green fruit with Taiwan's working class, cultural shifts, and health and environmental issues.

#### A Cultural Capsule: Betel Nut and Taiwan's Indigenous Peoples

The history of humans chewing betel nut dates back five thousand years, but its connection to Taiwan spans over four hundred years. In the cultures of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples, betel nut is not merely a stimulant; it carries rich social and spiritual significance. Yang Cheng-hsien, an associate professor in the Department of Indigenous Studies and Social Work at National Dong Hwa University, points out that betel nut serves as a "cultural capsule" for Indigenous peoples [^1].

For the Amis people, betel nut is a token of love. During the "Lovers' Night" of the Harvest Festival, women present betel nuts to the men they admire; if the man accepts and chews it on the spot, it signifies mutual affection. This matrilineal system makes betel nut an important medium for connecting emotions and families [^1]. The Siraya people view betel nut as a sacred object for communicating with ancestral spirits. Duan Hung-kun, head of the Jibei Shamanism Historical Studio, states that betel nut (Siraya: _Abiki_) is an indispensable offering when priests pray to _Alid_ (ancestral spirits), and it is even used as a ritual implement during ceremonies, holding a sacred status comparable to the "Peach Blossom Sword" of the Han people [^1]. Duan also noted that in Siraya ceremonies, betel nuts that have been used for ritual purposes cannot be discarded casually; they are placed on rooftops or in remote areas, demonstrating their sanctity [^1].

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The multifaceted role of betel nut in Indigenous culture challenges the singular "unhealthy" label imposed by modern society. It is not just a substance, but a symbol for maintaining community, passing down beliefs, and expressing emotion—its meaning extends far beyond mere oral gratification.

#### Han Settlers' "Miasma-Cleansing Pill" and Laborers' "Green Gold"

When Han immigrants arrived in Taiwan during the Qing Dynasty, they faced a hot, humid environment filled with miasma. Betel nut became an aid in their adaptation to the new land. Chewing betel nut causes the body to heat up and sweat, which was viewed as a "miasma-cleansing pill" (_xi zhang dan_) to expel dampness and prevent disease [^2]. This functional demand allowed betel nut to take root in Han society, becoming an essential item for social interaction and wedding gifts. Even during conflict mediation, the party at fault might offer betel nuts as a gesture of apology [^2].

In the latter half of the 20th century, as Taiwan's economy took off, a massive number of blue-collar workers entered the infrastructure sector. Due to its stimulating effects, betel nut became a daily necessity for the working class, including truck drivers and construction workers. Betel nut stalls emerged accordingly, reaching their peak in the 1990s. The total area of betel nut cultivation in Taiwan once reached as high as 65,000 hectares—second only to rice—and its annual output value once hit NT$8 billion, earning it the nickname "Green Gold" [^3]. Research by Wu Chia-chun, a professor in the Department of Soil and Water Conservation at National Pingtung University of Science and Technology, indicates that if one hectare of land is left completely bare for a year, 294 tons of soil (about three centimeters of topsoil) will be eroded; betel nut plantations have soil retention capabilities far inferior to forests [^3].

#### Betel Nut Beauties: A Unique Highway Landscape and Social Controversy

In 1976, the "Shuangdong Betel Nut Stall" in Caotun, Nantou, pioneered the sales model of "Betel Nut Beauties," using scantily clad women to sell betel nuts, which rapidly attracted customers' attention. In the 1990s, the culture of Betel Nut Beauties reached its zenith, becoming a unique sight along Taiwan's highways. They were not just salespeople; they provided solace to many blue-collar workers during long shifts, representing a grassroots survival aesthetic and an expression of the informal economy. However, Betel Nut Beauties were also frequently accompanied by social moral controversies, workplace safety issues, and criticisms regarding the objectification of women [^4].

Another "hidden bestseller" at betel nut stalls is "frozen water" (_jiebing shui_). For drivers and workers laboring under a scorching sun, frozen water is not just for quenching thirst; it serves as a tool for physical cooling, tied to the body or placed inside vehicles to provide brief relief. This seemingly simple commodity reflects the survival wisdom and needs of Taiwan's blue-collar workers in harsh environments [^5]. One netizen shared that in a tin-roofed auto repair shop or construction site reaching nearly 40°C (104°F), frozen water is an "oasis in the desert" [^5].

#### The Double Cost of Health and Environment

However, behind the prosperity of betel nut lies a heavy cost to health and the environment. Medical research has confirmed that chewing betel nut is a primary cause of oral cancer. In Taiwan, approximately 2,500 to 3,000 people die from oral cancer annually, and up to 90% of oral cancer patients have a habit of chewing betel nut. The betel nut itself has been classified by the World Health Organization as a Group 1 carcinogen; it remains carcinogenic even without the addition of lime or betel leaves [^6].

Regarding the environment, the shallow root system of the betel nut tree means that when planted on hillsides, its soil retention capability is far inferior to deep-rooted tree species. In 1994, Chen Hsin-hsiung, a professor in the Department of Forestry at National Taiwan University, proposed the "Betel Nut National Ruin Theory," pointing out that betel nut monocultures easily cause soil erosion and land degradation, exacerbating debris flows during typhoons and heavy rains [^3]. Although some studies suggest that betel nut is not the sole factor causing soil erosion—with some views arguing that "all cash crops have shallow roots, but compared to others, the betel nut tree is actually one of the best cash crops for soil and water conservation" [^7]—its negative environmental impact remains an indisputable fact. Although the government once promoted programs to convert betel nut plantations into forests, these efforts faced heavy resistance due to farmers' livelihood concerns [^3].

#### Transformation and the Future: Swinging Between Tradition and Modernity

Faced with the dual pressures of health and environmental protection, Taiwanese society's attitude toward betel nut is gradually shifting. The proportion of the population chewing betel nut is declining annually, and the industry is facing a transformation. The draft "Betel Nut Health Hazard Prevention Act" announced by the Ministry of Health and Welfare will impose stricter regulations on the sale and advertising of betel nuts, signaling that the era of the Betel Nut Beauty may be coming to an end [^4].

However, for many Indigenous communities, the cultural significance of betel nut remains deeply rooted. How to find a balance between respecting traditional culture and maintaining public health and environmental sustainability is a challenge Taiwan must face. The story of betel nut is not just the rise and fall of a single fruit, but a microcosm of Taiwan's continuous adaptation and reflection during the process of modernization; it reminds us that complex and subtle links exist between culture, economy, and the environment. This fruit is both a witness to history and a microcosm of the contradictions and tensions in contemporary Taiwanese society.
