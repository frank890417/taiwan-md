In 2020, the Ministry of Culture conducted a "National Survey on Languages Facing Inheritance Crises." The results were cited in a report by the Legislative Yuan's Budget Center as follows: the generational usage rate of Taiwanese has dropped by nearly 60%, Hakka by 70%, and Indigenous languages by 90%. [^1]

"Ninety percent" is the deepest wound among these three languages.

## Ninety Percent

This figure does not mean "90% of Indigenous people cannot speak their language," but rather refers to the gap in usage rates between generations. [^1]

In other words, the gap between the proportion of grandparents and grandchildren who use their native language is nearly tenfold. This is a classic linguistic signal of an "endangered" language: it is not that no one speaks it, but that speakers are concentrated among the elderly.

## The Survey Ten Years Later

On December 26, 2024, the Indigenous Languages Research and Development Foundation (ILRDF) released a survey on language proficiency and usage status. [^2]

The study lasted one year and interviewed over 24,000 Indigenous people. The report noted that compared to the Council of Indigenous Peoples' (CIP) survey from ten years ago, these results show a significant decline. [^2]

ILRDF Chairperson Mayaw Kumud pointed to mobility as the cause: once Indigenous people leave their ancestral homelands for urban areas, their language usage rates tend to weaken. [^2]

This is the most difficult aspect of Indigenous language policy to manage: language loss is not due to a lack of effort, but because the communities that carry the language are moving and thinning out.

## The Family as the Home Ground

In the same survey, the most policy-significant finding was the setting in which languages are used.

The settings where surveyed Indigenous people use their native languages are primarily within the family, far exceeding schools or churches. [^2]

Li Pei-jung, a co-investigator of the project, observed that, generally speaking, the listening and speaking abilities of Indigenous people are superior to their reading and writing skills. Therefore, the next step must find ways to improve literacy while simultaneously elevating oral proficiency. [^2]

Consequently, the ILRDF's recommendation is clear: strengthen methods for using and learning native languages within the family to make language use more integrated into daily life. [^2]

## The School Side

Schools have not been absent. The survey found that the use of native languages in schools increases during school-age years. [^2]

Researcher Li Shao-yang cited the Tsou and Kanakanavu peoples as examples, noting that their reading and writing scores are closer to their listening and speaking scores, indicating that many opportunities and measures have successfully revitalized the language through effective teaching. [^2]

Taur Mavaliw (Pan Yu-cheng), Section Chief of the Language Division at the CIP's Department of Education and Culture, stated that more people are beginning to speak endangered languages, and the age group using these languages is starting to trend younger. This suggests that measures such as Indigenous language nannies and immersion kindergartens are beginning to yield results.

These are subjective assessments from survey respondents and observations from researchers rather than rigorous impact evaluations; however, the direction aligns with the findings from the family side: the earlier and more routine the exposure, the greater the chance of survival.

## Systems and Budgets

The policy framework is the "National Integrated Language Development Plan." This plan was approved by the Executive Yuan in July 2022, covering the period from June 2022 to the end of December 2026. [^1]

Under the 2026 budget item "Indigenous Education Promotion—01 Indigenous Education Coordination and Development Funds," the CIP has allocated NT$1.075 billion for this plan. [^3]

Relative to the 90% generational gap, the scale of this budget illustrates two things: policy-makers have treated this as a long-term engineering project; and the effects of language revitalization are not something that can be "accepted" or measured within a single fiscal year.

## How to Read These Two Figures

The "90% loss" and "family-centric usage" may seem contradictory, but they are actually two sides of the same coin.

The "loss" describes the result: the daily scenarios for language use are shrinking. The "family-centric" aspect describes the leverage: the places where people are still willing to use the language are precisely the private domains that are hardest for policy to reach.

This also explains why recent policy has shifted from "teaching the language" to "using the language": immersion kindergartens, Indigenous language nannies, and family co-learning are all attempts to bring the language out of the classroom and back into life.

Urbanization remains the greatest variable. When Indigenous people migrate to cities, the linguistic networks of both families and communities thin out simultaneously; schools and media then become substitute carriers, but they provide learning rather than a way of life.

When the next survey is released, what is worth watching is not just a single percentage, but two things: whether the family usage rate has stopped falling, and whether the gap between literacy and oral proficiency is narrowing. These two directions explain the progress of revitalization far better than total volume alone. [^2]
