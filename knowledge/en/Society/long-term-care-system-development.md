# The Development of Taiwan's Long-Term Care System

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan possesses one of Asia's most comprehensive long-term care systems. Long-Term Care (LTC) 2.0 services 360,000 people, with service coverage prevalence rising from 20.30% in 2017 to 89.9% as of June 2025. On December 31, 2025, the Executive Yuan approved LTC 3.0, which will be implemented starting in 2026. [^1] Simultaneously, 214,000 migrant caregivers quietly sustain another, even larger care system. This is a story of institutional design, social values, and pragmatic compromise. [^2]

In 2017, the Tsai Ing-wen administration launched LTC 2.0, aiming to construct a "continuous and universal long-term care service system." That same year, the number of migrant domestic caregivers employed in Taiwan surpassed 200,000 for the first time.

This is no coincidence; it represents the greatest contradiction in Taiwan's care system: on one side is an official system backed by an annual government budget of NT$65 billion, and on the other is an invisible system of 214,000 families each employing their own migrant caregivers. The former serves 360,000 people, while the latter serves approximately 210,000—two parallel worlds of care that almost never intersect.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The most counter-intuitive aspect of Taiwan's LTC policy: we are building a world-class institutional care system while simultaneously cultivating a society that is among the most dependent in the world on migrant caregivers.

## The Birth of the System: Racing Ahead of Super-Aging

In 1993, the proportion of the population aged 65 and over reached 7%, officially entering an aging society. However, political response was surprisingly slow—it took until 2007, fourteen years after the onset of aging, for the "Ten-Year Long-Term Care Plan" to be officially launched.

This delayed plan was met with skepticism from the start. The budget of NT$81.7 billion over ten years seemed large, but it averaged only NT$8 billion per year—less than 2% of the National Health Insurance (NHI) budget at the time. Worse still, the service target was only about 20,000 people, representing a coverage rate of just 1.5%.

During the 2016 presidential election, long-term care became an issue that none of the three major candidates dared to avoid. Tsai Ing-wen promised to upgrade the system to "LTC 2.0," shifting the budget from annual allocations to dedicated funds from tobacco taxes, increasing the annual budget to over NT$30 billion.

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**
> Medical community criticism: Japan's LTC insurance funding is severely insufficient, with tax revenue and insurance premiums each accounting for half; Taiwan relies solely on tax revenue, raising concerns about fiscal sustainability.

But while the numbers look impressive, the reality is stark.

## The Era of Labor Shortages: Seven Home Care Workers for One Patient

"Not being able to find people" is the greatest pain point in the LTC system. Tu Hsin-ning, Chairperson of the Home Care Service Strategy Alliance, pointed out that the manpower scale of home care units is experiencing a structural collapse: in 2016, there were 186 home care units across Taiwan, with an average of 47.9 workers per unit; by 2024, the number of units increased to 2,235, but the average number of workers per unit dropped to 24.3. [^2]

As units increase and manpower becomes more dispersed, flexible scheduling becomes impossible. Chen Wan-yu, a case manager at Junwei Home Care Institution in Taipei, encountered an extreme situation: one client required seven different home care workers to rotate shifts, sometimes with different workers arriving at different times of the same day—one for bathing, one for walking, and one for repositioning.

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> Night-shift home care is almost impossible to find. Even though the government provides a NT$385 night-shift premium, the number of workers willing to work at night remains extremely low. For working families, caregivers find it difficult to "catch a breath" after returning home.

Even more serious is the massive loss of case managers in the midstream of the manpower chain. While the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) mandates a maximum load of 120 cases per case manager, they frequently handle over 150. Shih Sheng-mao, a senior case manager at the Taipei Red Cross Society, said: "A social worker for the elderly living alone can provide deep service for 30–60 cases; handling 150 cases makes holistic care impossible."

A piece of dark humor common in the LTC sector is: "In the future, our long-term care will rely on only two types of people—foreigners and robots!"

## The Invisible Half: A Parallel World Sustained by 240,000 Migrant Caregivers

While LTC 2.0 shines in the media, another much larger care system is quietly expanding.

When Taiwan opened its doors to migrant domestic caregivers in 1992, there were only 306. By 2024, that number reached 214,514—80% from Indonesia, 10.8% from the Philippines, and 8.9% from Vietnam. If they stood in a line, they could stretch from Taipei to Kaohsiung.

> **📊 Data Source**
> According to Ministry of Labor statistics, as of March 2024, there are 910,000 people aged 80 and over in Taiwan, 41.7% of whom have some level of disability.

These 214,000 migrant caregiver households form a care world that runs completely parallel to LTC 2.0. The monthly cost of employing a migrant caregiver is approximately NT$30,000 for 24-hour care; in contrast, the maximum monthly subsidy for those with severe disabilities under LTC 2.0 is only NT$32,340, and service hours are limited.

Crucially, the two systems almost never interact. Under current regulations, families employing migrant caregivers are, in principle, ineligible to apply for LTC 2.0 services (except for assistive devices and home accessibility improvements). This exclusion clause turns these 210,000 families into institutional islands.

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**
> Starting in 2026, LTC 3.0 will allow migrant caregiver families to use community care services, but the MOHW worries this could cause a surge in demand for migrant workers by 160,000, leading to an imbalance of supply and demand.

## 24 Hours vs. 8 Hours: A Clash of Two Care Philosophies

Why would a Taiwanese family prefer to spend NT$30,000 on a migrant caregiver rather than use LTC 2.0?

Lien Chin-ying in Hualien is caring for her 98-year-old mother with dementia while also looking after her 76-year-old husband. She says: "The LTC worker only comes for two hours a day. I have to rush home to cook for my husband during that time and be back before 1 PM. At night, you basically can't find anyone."

This is the fundamental difference between the two systems: LTC 2.0 provides "segmented professional care," while migrant caregivers provide "around-the-clock life companionship." The former meets international standards for professional care, while the latter meets the practical needs of East Asian families.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Taiwan's choice of care models is actually a struggle between two values: the Nordic model of "professionalization and community-based care" vs. the East Asian model of "familialization and holistic care."

After comparing care models in Taiwan and Singapore, Lynn Yu Ling Ng, a scholar at the National University of Singapore, found that migrant caregivers in Taiwan act more as "assistants to family caregivers" rather than "replacements for family caregivers." This stems from Taiwan's strict application thresholds—one must pass a medical assessment before being allowed to hire them—which forces Taiwanese employers to learn basic caregiving skills and continue participating in care even after hiring a migrant worker. [^4]

In contrast, professional LTC 2.0 services are often absent during the times they are needed most. Wei Li-hu, a home care worker who specializes in night shifts, says: "Most people still aren't willing to work at night, even with the NT$385 premium."

## Dignity on an Assembly Line: When Care Becomes Rushed Labor

The cost of labor shortages is not just difficult scheduling, but also a compromise in the quality of care.

Chang Kai-chieh (pseudonym), a social worker at a residential institution, observed that basic services like bathing have become assembly-line operations. "Bathing at 8 AM in the winter when it's 10 degrees Celsius means getting out from under warm blankets to wash. Residents line up outside the bathroom door, and three caregivers divide the labor: one undresses them, one supports their body, and one rinses them."

Li Shao-fen, an assistant professor at the Institute of Health Policy and Management at Academia Sinica, calls this phenomenon "rushed care"—care workers can only numb their senses day after day to complete labor tasks; the agency of care is restricted, and the risks are borne by the residents.

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> In many residential institutions, residents only bathe twice a week in winter because insufficient manpower prevents services from being provided at appropriate times.

Xiao-shi (pseudonym), a caregiver who once worked in a residential institution, said: "During bathing hours, we have to wash about ten beds every day, cycling through every two or three hours—constant cleaning, feeding, and bathing. It's very much like an assembly line; it's so rushed. By the time I'm done, I'm drenched in sweat."

This "efficiency-first" care model is one reason why Taiwanese families choose migrant caregivers: at home, they can at least maintain a basic level of dignity.

## International Perspectives: The Uniqueness of the Taiwan Model

Taiwan's dual-track care system is unique internationally.

Germany established long-term care insurance in 1994 using a "cash or service" model—one can choose to receive cash to arrange their own care or accept professional services. Japan's _kaigo_ (nursing care) insurance focuses on community-integrated service centers, emphasizing the integration of medical, nursing, and housing services. The Netherlands established long-term care insurance as early as 1968 and now provides standardized services through a "care package" system. [^3]

But no country is like Taiwan: running two large-scale, incompatible care systems simultaneously.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The contradiction in Taiwan's LTC system reflects the struggle of East Asian societies during modernization: do we want the professionalized Nordic model or the humanized family model? The answer is both, but we are failing at both.

Scholar Liang Li-fang points out: "Migrant caregivers in Taiwan are never intended to replace the family role, but to supplement the family's deficiencies in care. This aligns with Confucian cultural expectations regarding family responsibility."

However, the cost of this model is institutional fragmentation. The 210,000 families employing migrant caregivers cannot enjoy LTC 2.0 community resources, respite services, or assistive device support. They live outside the system's field of vision, left to fend for themselves.

## The Three Arrows of Rescue: How the Government Responds to Labor Shortages

Facing the LTC manpower crisis, the MOHW has launched three policy "arrows":

**The First Arrow: Pilot Program for Diversified Companionship and Care Services**
Allows citizens to purchase temporary, short-term care services at their own expense. Three months after its launch in 2025, it served a cumulative 485 person-times across 15 counties and cities, demonstrating real demand.

**The Second Arrow: Industry-Academia Cooperation International Talent Recruitment and Retention Program**
Recruits students from Vietnam and Indonesia to study in long-term care vocational programs, with a three-year service contract upon graduation. The first cohort of 22 students has already been fully reserved by the industry; the second cohort will expand to 40.

**The Third Arrow: Opening Opportunities for Mid-level Migrant Technical Talent**
Migrant caregivers who have worked for over six years and earn a monthly salary of at least NT$29,000 can transition to "mid-level technical manpower," removing the restriction on years of service. As of February 2024, 15,000 people have obtained this qualification.

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**
> The three arrows seem like a lot, but compared to the demand for hundreds of thousands of workers, the 22 Vietnamese students are criticized as "a drop in the bucket." The real solution may lie in raising the social status and salary levels of care services.

## The Bet on LTC 3.0: Integration or Further Division?

On December 31, 2025, the Executive Yuan approved the "Ten-Year Long-Term Care Plan 3.0," covering the years 2026 to 2035. The plan's vision includes "healthy aging, aging in place, and peaceful end-of-life care." Its eight major dimensions include integrated medical care, discharge preparation and rehabilitation, institutional capacity, family support, smart care, peaceful end-of-life, and manpower training, with three special chapters for dementia, indigenous peoples, and persons with disabilities. [^1] One aspect most relevant to migrant caregiver families is the MOHW's plan to incorporate migrant labor into the LTC system in three stages to supplement night care and respite manpower, though this still awaits amendments to the _Long-Term Care Services Act_. [^5] Will this policy resolve the contradiction of the dual-track system, or make the problem more complex?

MOHW Deputy Minister Lu Chien-te said: "Migrant workers are supplementary manpower; they do what local workers are unwilling to do." But the reality is that 214,000 migrant caregivers are no longer just "supplementary"—they are the backbone of Taiwan's LTC system.

If LTC 3.0 successfully integrates the two systems, Taiwan might create a unique "hybrid care model" in the world—combining the quality standards of professional services with the human warmth of family care.

If it fails, Taiwan may face even more severe institutional division: wealthy families who can afford migrant caregivers will enjoy dual services, while those without means must queue for government resources, magnifying class disparities at the very moment care needs are most urgent.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The future of Taiwan's LTC system is actually answering a deeper question: what kind of society do we want to be? An efficiency-first professional society, or a warmth-first humanistic society?

## Afterword: Two Ways of Loving

Tong Fu-chien, an 86-year-old from Tainan with kidney disease and dementia, has an Indonesian caregiver named Astuti who grinds silken tofu and vermicelli for him every day because "Grandpa can't bite hard food, but he likes noodles." Astuti says Grandpa is "_yi ji bang_" (excellent), and Grandpa's son says Astuti is like "another daughter."

On the other hand, 69-year-old Lien Chin-ying in Hualien cares for her 98-year-old mother with dementia. She uses LTC 2.0 home care workers to help with meal prep, but still has to sleep on a floor mattress in her mother's room at night. She says: "This is my mother! I cannot neglect her."

These two stories represent two understandings of "care" in Taiwanese society: one is the procurement of services with professional division and controllable quality; the other is an extension of the family through holistic companionship and emotional symbiosis.

The development of Taiwan's LTC system is a search for balance between these two ways of loving. We are still learning how to ensure professional quality while maintaining human warmth, and how to pursue efficiency without forgetting that the essence of care is the connection between people.

This learning process will continue for a long time. But at least, we have begun.
