> **30 Second Overview:** Taiwan's military service system is one of the deepest social imprints of this island over half a century. From the "three-year conscription" era during martial law to the current mandatory one-year term, this is not merely a numerical change in service length; it is an authentic reflection of Taiwan confronting geopolitical pressure. This article takes you from the gasp of surprise at the draft lottery—the "Golden Horse Award"—to the modern preparedness scene where even supermarkets are included in Hanwha exercises, deeply analyzing the practical transformation across exemption standards, new training schedules, various substitute services, and modernized reserve duty calls.

### Collective Breath-Holding at the Draft Lottery: Who Won the "Golden Horse Award"?

In Taiwan, every man reaches the age of 19 and faces a life-altering "draft lottery." In the hall of the local township office, the air is always thick with tension. What causes the most dread for conscripts and their parents is drawing the overseas island—Kinmen or Matsu—a fate mockingly dubbed the "Golden Horse Award."

The term "Golden Horse Award" originated in the 1980s when cross-Strait relations were highly confrontational; being assigned to an outlying island meant not only enduring solitude hundreds of kilometers from home but also facing the possibility of sudden conflict [^1]. Even today, amid "gray zone" conflicts between the two sides, the hardship of serving on these islands and the longing for home after only three months are the loudest sighs heard at the draft lottery.

📝 **Curator's Note:** In Taiwan, the draft is not a matter of probability; it is a national ritual concerning "luck" and "destiny."

### The Pendulum of Military Service: From Three Years to Four Months, Then to One Year

Taiwan's conscription system began in the late Japanese colonial period in 1945, initially designed to support the Pacific War [^2]. After the Nationalist government relocated to Taiwan, the Army service term was as long as two years; for the Navy, Air Force, and special forces (such as paratroopers), it was a full three years—commonly known as "Army One, Special Three."

As geopolitical circumstances evolved, this transitioned to a "four-month military training service" in 2013. However, facing escalating tensions across the Taiwan Strait, the government announced that starting from January 1, 2005, mandatory service would be reinstated to one year [^3]. A high of 73.2% public opinion recognized "extending military service as a necessary cost" [^4].

### Basic Military Training: An Eight-Week Baptism from Civilian to Soldier

Basic military training (boot camp) is the first stop for every soldier, usually conducted at new training centers like Cheng Kung or Guan Tian. This eight-week period aims to transform civilians into qualified combat personnel. Beyond physical conditioning, the core element is "live-fire shooting," as modern basic training has significantly increased the number of firing exercises, including zeroing, qualification shooting, and live-range practice [^5].

The "family visit" during boot camp is the most anticipated time for conscripts; parents bring instant noodles and home-cooked meals into the barracks. This brief period of freedom and familial connection often becomes one of the most unforgettable memories in a man's life.

### A Day as a Soldier: A Collective Schedule Precise to the Minute

"Brothers, wake up! The time is 0530. Unit wake-up!" In the barracks, time moves by the "minute." Below is a typical schedule for National Defense Force basic training [^6]:

| Time      | Activity                        | Details                                                                       |
| :-------- | :------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **05:30** | Wake up and personal duties     | Must fold the "tofu-block" blanket and mosquito net within 10 minutes.        |
| **05:50** | Roll call and morning exercises | Count personnel, perform jumping jacks or run.                                |
| **07:00** | Breakfast and gear issue        | Quick meal followed by receiving rifle and ballistic vest.                    |
| **08:00** | Morning training                | Individual combat drills, rifle handling, or shooting practice.               |
| **12:00** | Lunch and rest period           | A rare chance to "lie flat" after the barracks lunch.                         |
| **14:00** | Afternoon training              | Physical fitness tests, grenade throwing, or tactical march.                  |
| **18:00** | Dinner and washing up           | The collective memory of fighting for a shower at the water spigot.           |
| **19:00** | Evening activities              | Discussion after military education, equipment maintenance, or phone time.    |
| **21:00** | Pre-lights out preparation      | Organizing gear for the next day, final roll call.                            |
| **21:30** | Lights out                      | Conversation is strictly forbidden; the barracks falls into absolute silence. |

📝 **Curator's Note:** This schedule is more than physical training; it is a psychological reshaping. In communal life, individual will is reduced to the lowest level, replaced by absolute obedience to commands.

### Research and Substitute Services: Another Battlefield

Not all conscripts carry a rifle. Taiwan has developed diverse "substitute service" systems:

- **Research and Development Substitute Service:** Master's and doctoral students serve in companies like TSMC or Foxconn, channeling their research energy into national defense competitiveness [^7].
- **General Substitute Service:** Includes police service (traffic assistance), fire service (disaster relief support), and social service (long-term care assistance) [^8].
- **Reserve Personnel:** Those with specific family circumstances or physical conditions who only need 12 days of training before discharge.

However, as the one-year term is restored, the government has significantly compressed the quota for substitute services to prioritize regular military personnel needs [^9].

### A Complete Guide to Exemption: Who Can Receive a "Discharge Order"?

Taiwan employs a rigorous "physical status classification standard" to determine exemption [^10]:

1. **BMI:** Those with a BMI below 15 or above 35 are classified as exemptible in terms of physical condition [^11].
2. **Vision Standards:** Both eyes have a corrected vision less than 0.1, or severe myopia combined with retinal disease.
3. **Specific Illnesses:** Such as heart disease, mental disorders, thyroid issues, cancer, or serious injuries.
4. **Height Restriction:** Those shorter than 155 cm are also eligible for exemption.

### Modern Exercises and Reserve Calls: When Supermarkets Become a Battlefield

Hanwha exercises recently included supermarkets in the drills for the first time, reflecting the importance of wartime resource allocation [^12]. Furthermore, the "reserve call" for discharged soldiers has been fully upgraded to a 14-day "modern reserve call," which includes tactical marches exceeding 10 kilometers and increased live-fire quotas, ensuring that reserve forces can transition from peace to war at any time [^13].

### Conclusion: A Form of Protection Beyond the Health Insurance Card

Taiwan's military service system is a long-term dialogue about "responsibility" and "reality." The next time you see a young person in a camouflage uniform on a street in Taiwan, they are not just a serving soldier; they represent the quietest yet truest cost paid by this island to maintain its freedom.
