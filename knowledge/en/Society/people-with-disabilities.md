As of late June 2026, the total number of people in Taiwan holding a disability certificate is 1,258,972. [^1]

Of these, 638,430 are aged 65 or older, accounting for 50.7%; in other words, half of Taiwan's population with disabilities are elderly. [^1]

## The Composition of 1.26 Million People

Looking at the long-term trend, the population with disabilities increased from 1,203,756 in 2021 to 1,255,789 in 2025, and rose further to 1,258,972 in the second quarter of 2026. [^1]

This growth is not driven by a single factor. Part of it stems from degenerative diseases and dementia brought about by an aging population; another part is due to changes in disability assessment systems and increased awareness, leading more people to be willing to register for certificates. While Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) statistics do not directly state the exact proportion of each factor, the age structure provides a clue.

## Categories: Neurological and Mental/Mental Functions are Largest

Based on the new classification system as of late June 2026, the largest category is "Neurological structures and mental/mental functions," totaling 395,924 people (31.4%). This is followed by "Neurological, muscular, skeletal movement-related structures and their functions" at 329,993 people (26.2%), and "Eyes, ears, related structures, sensory functions, and pain" at 202,577 people (16.1%). [^1]

Additionally, 132,139 people belong to two or more categories simultaneously. Within single categories, the urinary and reproductive systems account for 81,340; circulatory, hematopoietic, immune, and respiratory systems account for 78,166; voice and speech structures account for 12,216; and digestive, metabolic, and endocrine systems account for 12,004. [^1]

By degree of disability, there are 128,752 people with "extremely severe" disabilities, consisting of 69,440 males and 59,312 females. [^1]

This distribution demonstrates one thing: Taiwan's population with disabilities is not a homogeneous group. The neurological and mental/mental function category covers dementia, autism, intellectual disabilities, and mental illnesses, which have service needs entirely different from those with sensory disabilities.

## Half are Aged 65 or Over

The age structure is the most noteworthy part of these statistics. Among the population with disabilities in late June 2026, there are 638,430 people aged 65+, 126,441 aged 60–64, 238,327 aged 45–59, 129,744 aged 30–44, 67,065 aged 18–29, and a total of 58,965 aged 0–17. [^1]

The shape of this chart differs from a standard population pyramid: instead of being wide at the bottom and narrow at the top, it is widest at the top. The reason is easy to understand: disabilities caused by degenerative and chronic diseases mostly occur after middle age.

Consequently, disability policy and long-term care (LTC) policy overlap significantly. Persons with disabilities aged 65 and over are also primary targets for LTC services, and the boundaries between these two systems often blur in practice.

## Distribution Across Major Cities

By county/city, New Taipei City has the highest number at 189,142, followed by Kaohsiung City (154,772), Taichung City (140,330), Taipei City (122,346), and Tainan City (102,353). [^1]

This ranking largely reflects population scale but is also influenced by the proportion of elderly residents. The six major cities combined account for the majority of Taiwan's disabled population; among non-major cities, Changhua County (73,166) and Pingtung County (54,129) have the highest numbers. [^1]

## Employment: 21.9% Labor Force Participation Rate

The "Survey on the Labor Status of Persons with Disabilities" conducted by the Ministry of Labor in 2024 (Year 113 of the ROC calendar) provides a full picture of the employment side. Among the 1.168 million civilian persons with disabilities aged 15 and older, the labor force is 256,000, consisting of 238,000 employed and 18,000 unemployed. The labor force participation rate is 21.9%, the unemployment rate is 7.1%, and the non-labor force stands at 912,000. [^2]

Compared to 2019 (Year 108), the labor force increased by 22,000 (+9.6%), the number of employed persons increased by 23,000 (+10.8%), the number of unemployed decreased by 1,000, the participation rate rose by 1.2 percentage points, and the unemployment rate fell by 1 percentage point. [^2]

Looking at the three-year trend makes it clearer: in 2014 (Year 103), the participation rate was 19.7% and the unemployment rate was 11.0%; in 2019, the participation rate was 20.7% and the unemployment rate was 8.1%; in 2024, the participation rate is 21.9% and the unemployment rate is 7.1%. [^2] Over a decade, employment conditions have indeed improved, but only marginally, with the participation rate rising by just 2.2 percentage points over ten years.

The gender gap is another dimension. In 2024, the labor force participation rate for males with disabilities was 26.3% and for females was 16.7%, a difference of 9.6 percentage points; the unemployment rate was 6.9% for males and 7.5% for females. [^2]

## What is Needed is Matching, Not More Vocational Training

The survey also asked, "What kind of employment services does the government need to provide?" A total of 32.5% responded that they need services: 37.6% of women and 29.5% of men. [^2]

When broken down by labor status, the gap is stark: 72.7% of unemployed persons indicated a need for government assistance; 68.1% of those in the non-labor force who have the ability and willingness to work also indicated a need; whereas only 18.9% of those already employed expressed a need. [^2]

The most requested item was "providing employment matching (including online)" at 68.7%, followed by "providing employment information" at 65.7%. [^2] These figures suggest that for most persons with disabilities who wish to work, the bottleneck is not skill, but finding employers willing to hire them.

## Bound by Demographic Structure

When these two sets of data are placed together, policy priorities are actually determined by demographic structure: half of the disabled population is aged 65 or older, and their needs lean toward medical care, long-term care, and assistive devices; the other half is distributed across employment and social participation, with a 21.9% labor force participation rate and a 7.1% unemployment rate. [^1][^2]

These two groups require entirely different tools for management. Taiwan's current system separates disability services and long-term care into two distinct systems, but in the overlapping zone of those aged 65 and over, users often have to face two separate application processes simultaneously.

This is also the most practical reminder from these statistics: 1.26 million is not a number to be pitied, but a population that requires categorized governance.
