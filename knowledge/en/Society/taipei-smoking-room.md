_Outside Ximen MRT Station Exit 6, Taiwan's first outdoor negative-pressure smoking room: a black-framed transparent glass box standing on the sidewalk. Photo: Huahua Daily / Reporter Zhang Xiang-ying (fair use editorial commentary)._

> **30-second overview:** Taipei has written "principle prohibition, exception allowance" into its streets: a comprehensive outdoor smoking ban in Ximending and the Xinzhongshan area, while simultaneously building Taiwan's first batch of outdoor negative-pressure smoking rooms that use exhaust to create an indoor-outdoor pressure difference, keeping smoke from escaping. Supporters say they can finally breathe freely; even anti-smoking groups oppose them, arguing that the more comfortable the rooms, the further they stray from the original goal of helping people quit. A visible glass box containing a 40-year tobacco control long march that has now pushed outdoors.

## A Transparent Box Parked at the Arcade Edge

On May 7, 2026, outside Ximen MRT Station Exit 6, between the signs for Chengdu Starfruit Ice and Taiwan Salt & Pepper Chicken Founding Flagship Store, a transparent box appeared on the sidewalk. About 16 square meters — someone on Dcard measured it as "almost exactly the size of a parking space." Inside were four layers of filters (oil filter, primary air filter, activated carbon, PM2.5); cigarette smoke goes in, gets washed layer by layer, then expelled through angled exhaust vents. Operating hours run from 8 a.m. to midnight, with a notice at the door: no entry for those under 20 or pregnant women. This is Taiwan's first outdoor negative-pressure smoking room[^1].

The term "negative pressure" sounds like laboratory jargon, but unpacked it's a counterintuitive physical phenomenon. As early as 2008, when the Sheraton Hotel installed a compliant indoor smoking room, PR manager Pai Ching-hui described it to a TV reporter this way: "The feeling of negative pressure is that when the door opens, the air inside doesn't rush out — instead, outside air rushes in."[^2] Exhaust fans pull the pressure inside the box lower than outside, so when the door opens, air flows in, trapping the smoke smell inside. This same principle is used in hospital negative-pressure isolation wards for infectious patients.

A city aiming to build a "smoke-free city" takes its first visible action by erecting a glass house for smoking at its busiest intersection. Contradictory? That breathing transparent box is actually the most concrete form yet of Taipei renegotiating the contract that places a legal — yet undeniably harmful to bystanders — cigarette back into public space. Following the smoke trail from office ashtrays to this glass house reveals a negotiation this city has spent forty years on without reaching a conclusion.

Only the box misbehaved from the start. Just three days after launch, the manual door on one unit wouldn't close properly and kept springing open; the Public Works Department said the door track had been adjusted[^3]. A box that supposedly relies on airtightness to maintain negative pressure, yet its door won't shut. That tiny gap perfectly foreshadowed all the disputes to come: between the claimed seal and the reality of leaks, there will always be an endless battle.

## Air Used to Be Free

To understand why a single street smoking room could become a major issue, we must first return to the era when cigarette smoke was simply part of the air. Before 2009, smoke drifted through Taiwan's offices, trains, KTVs, and restaurants. The Monopoly Bureau's Long Life cigarettes had been the market leader since their 1958 launch, still commanding 76% of Taiwan's total cigarette sales in 1987 — meaning three out of every four smokers smoked Long Life[^4]. The Songshan Tobacco Factory, begun in 1937 by the Governor-General's Monopoly Bureau, employed 2,000 workers at its peak with annual output value exceeding NT$21 billion, and operated until 1998[^5]. Tobacco was once a state enterprise, and smoking was the daily background noise of this island.

![Songshan Tobacco Factory cigarette plant north facade, a modern cigarette factory built in 1937 by the Taiwan Governor-General's Monopoly Bureau, which became the production base of the Tobacco and Alcohol Monopoly Bureau after the war](/article-images/society/songshan-tobacco-factory-north.webp)

_Songshan Tobacco Factory Cigarette Plant: Built in 1937 by the Governor-General's Monopoly Bureau, taken over by the Tobacco and Alcohol Monopoly Bureau after the war, operated until 1998, now the Songshan Cultural and Creative Park. Tobacco was once a state enterprise. Photo: Terahito Mencius / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0._

This background noise began to shift when one man had a lung lobe removed. Yen Tao learned to smoke at 12; at 52, he had his right upper lung lobe removed due to smoking-related illness, and after discharge quietly resolved to dedicate himself to tobacco control[^6]. On May 19, 1984, his close friend Tung Chih-ying, to thank him for resolving a legal dispute, offered US$2.5 million (approximately NT$1 billion at the exchange rate of the time); Yen Tao instead suggested using the money to establish a foundation focused on tobacco control[^7]. The Tung Foundation was thus born.

That same April, actor Sun Yueh was filming "Old Mo's Second Spring" in southern Taiwan. After a take, he habitually reached for a cigarette, but seeing two uniformed students approaching, a thought flashed through his mind: "Sun Yueh! Your smoking harms others — are you still going to smoke?" He quit his 38-year habit, volunteered at the Tung Foundation, and promoted a slogan many still remember: "Everyone has the right to refuse secondhand smoke."[^8] This phrase shifted the question from "Will you quit for your own health?" to "Will your smoking hurt the person next to you?" This reframing is the starting point for all outdoor smoking ban logic forty years later.

Thirteen years passed from that slogan to law. The Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act was promulgated in March 1997 and took effect in September. A 2007 amendment brought a total indoor smoking ban in public and workplace venues with three or more people, effective January 11, 2009. On March 22, 2023, the ban expanded further to cover preschools, universities, bars and nightclubs, banned e-cigarettes entirely, and raised the legal smoking age to 20[^9]. Each expansion shrank the space where smoking was permitted.

```tw-timeline

```

The tobacco business also exited stage by stage. In 2002, Taiwan joined the WTO and the Monopoly Bureau was restructured into the Taiwan Tobacco and Liquor Corporation, ending forty years of the monopoly system[^10]. In 2016, tobacco farmers and the Tobacco and Liquor Corporation reached an agreement; after curing the final batch of tobacco leaves in spring 2017, tobacco cultivation in Taiwan passed into history[^11]. Something that once dominated the air and fed the national treasury was escorted out of public space step by step.

## Indoors Won, But Outdoors Hasn't

This long campaign, fought into the 2020s, is a major victory on paper. The smoking rate among adults aged 18 and above dropped from 21.9% in 2008 to 12.8% in 2024, a decline of over 40%, the lowest since 1990[^12]. Secondhand smoke exposure in indoor workplaces also fell from 32.1% in 2008 to 22.1% in 2020[^13]. With several hundred thousand fewer smokers, ashtrays have all but vanished from offices.

But break down the secondhand smoke numbers and you'll see two lines moving in opposite directions. Secondhand smoke exposure in indoor public places plummeted from 27.8% in 2008 to 3.2% in 2024 — the indoor battle was all but a rout. Yet in outdoor public places without smoking bans, the figure climbed from 36.2% to a 2013 peak of 58.5% over the same period, only recently falling back to 48.9% in 2024 — still nearly half[^12]. The smoke didn't disappear; it was pushed out the door.

```tw-line
Two Lines of Secondhand Smoke: Indoors Nearly Cleared, Outdoors Still Nearly Half (Exposure Rate %)
Year | Indoor Public Places | Outdoor Non-Smoking-Ban Places
2008 | 27.8 | 36.2
2024 | 3.2 | 48.9
Source: National Health Administration Adult Smoking Behavior Survey
```

Ask where this smoke most often meets people, and the answer is specific. In the 2024 survey, the most common place to encounter secondhand smoke was "roads, streets, arcades, and other outdoor thoroughfares," at 27.9%, far ahead of parks (7.7%) and convenience store arcades (7.7%)[^14]. In other words, indoor smoking bans drove smokers out the door, and the arcades outside became new smoke corridors. Walking through Ximending, forced to become what others call "breath-holding masters" — the data doesn't exaggerate at all.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Worth pausing to examine the underlying mechanics of this policy. The old outdoor approach was "drawing no-smoking zones": first assume smoking is allowed everywhere, then circle a few patches where it isn't. The current city government's smoke-free city flips this default entirely: outdoors, smoking is prohibited in principle; only "listed" designated spots allow it. The same sidewalk, its default flipped from "can smoke" to "cannot smoke." Putting up a few more signs is just the surface; what's truly being rewritten is who the city's air belongs to.

## Prohibited in Principle, Exceptions Allowed

After the reversal, the remaining question was where the few who still smoke would go. The current city government's answer is "diversion management": a comprehensive outdoor smoking ban, but guiding smokers to designated smoking areas or smoking rooms[^15]. Starting June 1, Ximending and the Ximenzhongshan area implemented a comprehensive smoking ban, with violations punishable by fines of up to NT$10,000. At the same time, designated smoking areas citywide reached 183 locations, then increased to 201 by late May, among which were Taiwan's first outdoor negative-pressure smoking rooms[^16].

Regarding the principle of negative pressure, Public Works Department New Construction Division Chief Secretary Lin Hui-chung explained clearly on the opening day: "This facility adopts 'negative pressure airflow control technology,' using exhaust and pressure adjustment to make indoor air pressure lower than external atmospheric pressure, forming a physical barrier where air can only flow inward and cannot escape outward, effectively reducing smoke leakage."[^15] These boxes vary in size; the largest one on Zhonghua Road can accommodate 16 people[^1]; smaller ones like the one at Ximen Station Exit 4 measure 2 meters by 8 meters, and Research, Development and Evaluation Commission Chair Yin Wei said "it can fit 10-plus people at once."[^17]

This design ended up as "negative pressure" rather than the fully enclosed smoking rooms seen in Osaka, Japan, and behind this lies a technical detour. Taipei originally wanted to directly adopt Osaka's enclosed approach, but the central government blocked it on the grounds that "closing windows on all four sides makes it indoors, and the Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act prohibits indoor smoking"[^18]. Thus, the "negative-pressure" design relying on airflow control rather than complete physical enclosure emerged, to some extent, as a solution to circumvent the regulatory threshold of "whether it counts as indoors." The technology itself is not new. Japan's Health Promotion Act, enacted in 2020, already set a statutory standard of entrance/exit wind speed of 0.2 meters per second or more for enclosed smoking rooms, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government even established stricter supplementary regulations[^19]. Taipei's true novelty lies not in the negative-pressure filtration technology itself, but in turning it into a piece of street furniture that stands on the street and is free to use.

As for how much it cost, the city government did not disclose the final accounts for each unit. During council questioning, the city government used approximately NT$1.5 million per unit as a planning estimate basis, projecting total expenditure of around NT$100 million by the end of June[^20]. On the other side, National Federation of Parents' Associations Chairperson Tao Lian-sheng questioned from a cost-effectiveness perspective: outdoor smoking rooms are legally required to have independent compartments, independent air conditioning, negative-pressure design, and automatic doors; "when they cannot achieve the effect of 'not smelling smoke,' yet still cost NT$ hundreds of thousands to millions to install, they clearly do not meet cost-effectiveness"[^21]. One set is the city government's planning baseline, the other is a parent group's critical estimate; the two figures differ in nature, but both point to the same question: is this box worth it.

## Even Anti-Smoking Advocates Are Arguing

If only cost-conscious parents were opposed, that would be simple enough. What truly complicates matters is that even the anti-smoking camp itself has started fighting — and they're not arguing over whether to oppose smoking, but how to do it.

The Taiwan Anti-Smoking Alliance and the Tung Foundation have made their position clear: they oppose building enclosed negative-pressure smoking rooms outdoors. Lin Ching-li, director of the Tung Foundation's Tobacco Control Center, advocates "from the inside out — first enforce 100% indoor smoking bans, then allow designated outdoor smoking areas," and bluntly states that "a smoke-free city should make things inconvenient for smokers, not install smoking kiosks everywhere" [^22]. In their logic, the more comfortable the smoking room, the more it paves the way for smoking, drifting further from the original goal of "getting people to quit." This isn't a new stance — as early as 2011, the Tung Foundation criticized Taoyuan Airport's plan to restore indoor smoking rooms.

```tw-versus
Harm reduction: confine the smoke to a box | Reduction: make smoking progressively harder
Acknowledge some still smoke, give them a corner that doesn't bother others | Goal: keep driving the smoking rate down
Concentrate smoke smell and butts, cleaner cityscape | The more inconvenient, the more likely people smoke less or quit altogether
Current city government's diversion logic | Anti-smoking groups' and some medical professionals' position
Source: Taipei City Council interpellation and parent groups' cost-benefit estimates (CNA reports, etc.) + Tung Foundation and Taiwan Anti-Smoking Alliance positions[^20][^21][^22]
```

Dr. Kuo Fei-ran, attending physician at NTU Hospital's Department of Family Medicine and secretary-general of the Taiwan Medical Alliance for Tobacco Control, goes a layer deeper. In a contributed article, he cites the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, pointing out that no ventilation, air filtration equipment, or designated smoking room can prevent secondhand smoke exposure: "There is no safe level of exposure to secondhand smoke" [^23]. His sharpest remark targets the side effect of the box's very existence [^25].

```tw-quote
Strongly support a smoke-free city, but don't install outdoor enclosed smoking rooms; don't claim that negative pressure and filtration in smoking rooms mean no secondhand smoke; don't promote wrong concepts for a good cause.
Kuo Fei-ran | Attending Physician, Department of Family Medicine, NTU Hospital; Secretary-General, Taiwan Medical Alliance for Tobacco Control, 2026
```

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Kuo's statement conceals an easily overlooked mechanism. A glass box flashing with futuristic appeal, boasting "four layers of filters," gives the implication that "equipment equals safety": when you see smoke sucked into a high-tech box, you subconsciously feel the secondhand smoke problem has been handled. But scientifically, this sense of reassurance doesn't hold up. The 2006 U.S. Surgeon General's Report concluded that even separately enclosed, separately ventilated negative-pressure smoking rooms cannot prevent secondhand smoke from seeping into adjacent areas [^24]. The WHO and ASHRAE (American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers) share this position. The glass house is both a considerate compromise and a potential barrier that lets people lower their guard — both can be true at the same time.

## The Kyoto Team's Two Studies: One Measured an Enclosed Room, the Other an Outdoor Corner

The debate reached a point where both sides started citing evidence. The most frequently referenced are two successive investigations by a Kyoto Women's University team; they measured two different structures.

The Anti-Smoking Alliance cited the 2023 study at their press conference: an enclosed smoking room with high-spec HVAC equipment recorded PM2.5 concentrations exceeding the air-quality alert threshold, and even the readings five meters outside the entrance surpassed the Ministry of Environment's daily average for air pollution[^25b]. The paper, titled "Passive Smoking Situation Inside and Around a Certain Enclosed Smoking Facility," appeared in the _Journal of the Japan Society for Tobacco Control_, Vol. 18 (2023), and measured precisely the interior and surroundings of an enclosed smoking room[^26]. The research subject was an enclosed room, so this citation holds.

The same team published a separate study in early 2024, "Passive Smoking Situation Around the Kyoto Station Outdoor Smoking Area," this time targeting a semi-open smoking corner at Kyoto Station, measuring PM2.5 at 3 m and 10 m to the east and 5.7 m to the south. The maximum value at 3 m east far exceeded the Ministry of the Environment's 70 µg/m³ alert criterion, and the 10 m reading was also elevated[^26b]. Professor Hiroshi Yamato of the University of Occupational and Environmental Health, interviewed on the matter, called it an "open smoking corner" and argued that placing such a corner under a large roof is unreasonable; it should be at least 25 meters from non-smokers' traffic lines[^27].

Together, the two studies point to the same conclusion: if airflow design is inadequate, neither enclosed nor open configurations can contain the smoke. An enclosed room adds a physical boundary, but that boundary only holds when negative pressure and ventilation truly meet spec — a premise the CDC, WHO, and engineering community repeatedly emphasize.

## Whose Arcade

Turning the lens from data and papers back to the street reveals a more complex crowd. Those smokers invited into glass boxes—or chased out of arcades—are not a monolith.

![Ximending pedestrian zone street corner, Taiwan's first outdoor negative-pressure smoking room sits on this crowded sidewalk](/article-images/society/ximending-street-crossing.webp)

_Ximending Pedestrian Zone: Taiwan's first batch of outdoor negative-pressure smoking rooms were installed in this, the district's most crowded area. Who has the right to light up here is the core of the entire dispute. Photo: Bigmorr / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0._

Some welcome the change. In a _China Times_ street interview in Ximending, a person with smoking experience felt it was "good for non-smokers, they won't be affected by secondhand smoke"; a female smoker said she used to encounter children or parents with strollers when smoking on the roadside, and now that there are designated smoking areas, it actually reduces mutual inconvenience[^28]. But others feel pushed too far. One smoker complained on Dcard that the smoking room was smaller than imagined, barely the size of a parking space, questioning whether they have to queue to smoke and still be constrained by operating hours[^29]. The city government's response: Japan's negative-pressure smoking rooms also only stay open until 10 or 11 p.m., and the smoking rooms are all within a three-to-five-minute walk of each other[^30].

An interesting reply appears below. A netizen responded to that "small as a parking space" complaint with: "Do you know how non-smokers usually feel?"[^29] One sentence places two grievances side by side: smokers feel stuffed into a cramped, distant box; non-smokers feel they've been squeezed by tobacco smell for decades, and the space they could stand in was never that large either. The same glass house—one side sees spatial distress, the other sees long-overdue fairness.

And if we step back further, we see that smoking itself distributes along a class line. Among adult men, those with junior high education or below have a smoking rate of 35.8%, three times that of college graduates and above (11.9%); in the 30–49 age bracket, junior high or below climbs to 50%, still nearly double that of college graduates and above (27%)[^31]. The lower the education, the more likely one is to be the person smoking.

```tw-bars
Lower education, higher smoking rate: Men with junior high or below are 3× college graduates (%)
*Junior high or below | 35.8 | 3× college graduates
College graduates and above | 11.9
Source: Ministry of Health and Welfare (Adult male smoking rate, 6th anniversary of Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act implementation statistics)
```

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Beneath this threefold gap lies an uncomfortable cycle. A large portion of the health and welfare surcharge levied per thousand cigarettes goes back to subsidize the disadvantaged: the tobacco surcharge pays National Health Insurance premiums for 446,000 economically disadvantaged people and supports the operations of 13 public residential institutions nationwide[^32]. In other words, people of lower socioeconomic status smoke at the highest rates, and the extra tax they pay on every pack circles back to subsidize the vulnerable healthcare network that includes themselves. This is not a design that can be judged right or wrong in a single sentence; it deserves the reader's own reflection: when we usher smokers into a glass box, those ushered in are often the people who already stand at the margins of this city.

Local shop owners and village chiefs worry about more practical management issues. Liu Chia-hsin, chairman of the Ximending Pedestrian Zone Development Promotion Association, led the response to the smoke-free commercial zone, but he also said "daytime has fewer management problems; the bigger worry is the evening hours"; Ximen Village Chief Yeh Min-hui pointed out the large homeless population in Ximending, worrying that "future management will indeed be the biggest problem"[^33]. These were expectations about the future voiced during interviews, not events that have already occurred, yet they realistically reflect the daily reality a policy that concentrates people into fixed spaces will next face.

## Tokyo Built Them Too, So What?

Taipei is not the first city to grapple with this issue. Diverting smokers into designated spaces has long been a common language among many Asian cities, though each has its own approach.

Japan moved earliest and most fragmentarily. Tokyo's Chiyoda Ward imposed fines for street smoking by ordinance in October 2002 — ¥2,000 on the spot — one of Japan's earliest street smoking bans; Shibuya Ward followed in 2019 with a ban on smoking in all public areas[^34]. The fragmentation lies in the fact that Tokyo Metropolis has no unified citywide rules; its 23 wards each go their own way, so the same cigarette can be legal on one street but a ¥2,000 violation once you step into the neighboring ward. By contrast, Taipei currently operates under a single citywide standard, avoiding this cross-ward confusion.

![Outdoor designated smoking area at Fukuoka's Ohori Park, semi-open wooden lattice design with SMOKING AREA sign](/article-images/society/ohori-park-smoking-area-fukuoka.webp)

_The outdoor smoking area at Fukuoka's Ohori Park uses wooden lattices to enclose a semi-open corner — very different from Taipei's glass boxes, sealed on all four sides and maintained under negative pressure by exhaust fans. Photo: Hirho / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0._

Other cities each have their own algorithms, but with different currencies and mechanisms, direct magnitude comparisons are ill-advised.

```tw-stat
NT$2,000–10,000 | Smoking violations in Taipei's Ximending/Xinzhongshan commercial districts | Effective 2026-06-01
¥2,000 | Street smoking in Tokyo's Chiyoda/Shibuya wards | Fine, paid on the spot
HK$3,000 | Hong Kong statutory no-smoking areas | Raised from 1,500 effective 2026-01-01
€135 | Outdoor areas frequented by children in France | Effective 2025-07-01
Source: Announcements by respective city governments/health authorities
```

Singapore's approach is to paint "yellow boxes" on the ground outside hawker centres and coffee shops; smokers must stand entirely inside the box. The bustling Orchard Road area has been designated a no-smoking zone since New Year's Day 2019, with smoking allowed only at a few designated smoking points[^35]. Hong Kong, for its part, raised the fixed penalty for statutory no-smoking areas from HK$1,500 to HK$3,000 on New Year's Day 2026, and added a rule prohibiting smoking while queuing[^36]. France went further: from July 2025, smoking is completely banned in outdoor areas frequented by children — parks, beaches, school entrances[^37]. The boundaries of smoke separation are being pushed into ever more granular everyday scenarios across jurisdictions.

The Taipei City Government's own phrasing is "nationwide first," referring to the first outdoor negative-pressure smoking room within Taiwan; neither Chinese nor English media have claimed "world's first"[^38]. This relatively restrained claim holds up: within the scope of current verification, no other country's government has been found installing this type of outdoor mechanical negative-pressure booth on streets. As for the often-cited warning that "Tokyo has 80% non-compliance," tracing it back to the source reveals a Kobe University team study published in _BMC Public Health_ measuring the non-compliance rate of "indoor" restaurants and bars with smoking bans in Tokyo Metropolis, Osaka Prefecture, and Aomori Prefecture (78.4%) — not the violation rate of outdoor smoking areas[^39]. The two cannot be directly analogized, but both point to the same reminder: no matter how finely the rules are written, the real test is always enforcement.

## Zombie Pods, and a Reckoning That Never Ends

The battlefield of tobacco keeps shifting. Even as Taipei argues over glass boxes for paper cigarettes, the next fight has already changed opponents.

While paper cigarettes retreat, e-cigarettes have quietly grown among adolescents. Junior high school e-cigarette use rose from 1.9% in 2018 to 3.9% in 2021; for senior high and vocational students, from 3.4% to 8.8% — even as paper cigarette use among the same cohort declined[^40]. Taiwan banned e-cigarettes entirely in 2023; heated tobacco products took a different path, passing health risk assessment review and legally hitting convenience store shelves starting October 2025[^41]. The latest battle came in 2026: etomidate — a controlled drug commonly known as "zombie pods" — often uses e-cigarettes as a delivery vehicle. On June 25, the Executive Yuan passed a draft amendment to the Tobacco Hazards Prevention Act, raising fines for possession and use of e-cigarettes from NT$2,000–10,000 to NT$30,000–100,000, and making manufacturing and sale criminal offenses punishable by up to seven years in prison. The bill remains under legislative review and has not yet passed its third reading[^42].

![A modern disposable e-cigarette (pod-style); Taiwan has banned the manufacture, import, sale, and use of e-cigarettes entirely since 2023](/article-images/society/electronic-cigarette-pod.webp)

_E-cigarettes are the new opponent on the tobacco control battlefield: Taiwan banned them entirely in 2023, yet youth usage keeps climbing, and now the "zombie pod" drug has triggered another amendment. The cigarette has changed its shell; the fight isn't over. Photo: Jacek Halicki / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0._

Civil society keeps pushing forward. In September 2025, a citizen proposed "Ban Pedestrian Smoking" on the Public Policy Network Participation Platform, arguing that even walking while smoking should be prohibited; it quickly reached the signature threshold[^43]. Where smoking is allowed has shrunk from indoors to arcades, from arcades into that glass box; now some even argue that lighting up while walking down the street should be banned. The space where one can smoke continues to contract.

Back to that transparent box at Ximen MRT Exit 6. People inside complain it's cramped and small, forced to stub out when operating hours end; people outside finally don't have to hold their breath walking through the arcade. The current city government calls it considerate diversion; anti-smoking advocates call it a compromise that betrays the principle; family physicians say the danger is that it makes people think "filtered equals no secondhand smoke." Four kinds of people look at the same box and see four truths — and perhaps that's exactly why it's made of glass: who watches whom, and who is watched by whom, all laid bare on the glass.

Tobacco never truly disappeared. It retreated from office ashtrays to arcades, from arcades into this breathing glass box, and now dons the shell of e-cigarettes, drifting toward the next unfinished battle. Forty years ago, when Yen Tao lost a lung lobe, Taiwan's air was still free; forty years later, a legal but harmful cigarette has been placed back into this city's still-unfinished public space contract. That transparent box on Ximen's streets won't be a period — it's just the most visible shape this negotiation has taken so far.
