# Taiwan's COVID-19 Pandemic and Vaccines: The Eighteen Months That Were Bought, and the Shot That Came Too Late

On February 15, 2020, an unlicensed taxi driver died in hospital. He had never left Taiwan; he had been infected by a Taiwanese businessman he had once driven as a passenger. This was Taiwan's first COVID-19 death, and its first confirmed case of local transmission.[^11]

For that entire year, Taiwan reported 799 confirmed cases and 7 deaths nationwide.[^9]

Two years later, in 2022, the same virus took 14,667 lives in Taiwan. People 65 and older accounted for 86% of them, and the median age of death was 82 — a full ten years older than the year before.[^54]

A driver who had never left the country, and two years later, an entire cohort with a median age of 82. Between these two images lie two years — and eighteen months that one system bought for Taiwan.

Those eighteen months really were bought: through border controls, the name-based mask rationing system, and a National Health Insurance card, Taiwan kept the pandemic outside its doors for nearly a year and a half. This period was later written into a review paper in a European public health journal, in a sentence that read: "Taiwan's early containment measures might have delayed the epidemic curve, allowing time for better preparation and proactive responses."[^10]

The key is in the second half of that sentence. The time was bought, but the preparation didn't keep pace with it. On May 19, 2021, the day Taiwan raised its nationwide epidemic alert to Level 3, the government's self-procured vaccines had reached a grand total of only one batch of 117,000 doses.[^22] By the time Omicron arrived in 2022, the senior citizens who needed that shot most still had their third doses lagging behind their own first and second.[^56]

> **30-Second Overview:** At eight in the morning on December 31, 2019, Taiwan's Centers for Disease Control (CDC) emailed China and the World Health Organization to verify reports of an unexplained pneumonia in Wuhan, and that same day began boarding-gate quarantine checks on flights arriving direct from Wuhan. Through border controls, the name-based mask rationing system, and a National Health Insurance card, Taiwan kept the pandemic outside for nearly eighteen months, recording only 799 confirmed cases and 7 deaths in all of 2020. The first AstraZeneca vaccines didn't arrive until March 3, 2021, and only 117,000 doses at that. By mid-July that year, government-purchased doses from the two major brands combined had reached about 2.45 million, against a total contracted volume of 15.05 million. When Omicron arrived in 2022, senior citizens' third doses still lagged behind. That year, 14,667 people died of COVID-19, with a median age of death of 82. Who bore the costs beyond death is a separate ledger that has never been fully tallied.

## Eight A.M. Email, Same-Day Flight Checks

In the early morning of December 31, 2019, Lo Yi-chun, then deputy director-general and spokesperson of Taiwan's Centers for Disease Control (CDC), saw online reports of at least seven cases of atypical pneumonia in Wuhan. At eight in the morning, the CDC confirmed this with China's Center for Disease Control and Prevention and notified the World Health Organization's International Health Regulations (IHR) contact point by email. That same day, flights arriving in Taiwan direct from Wuhan were intercepted for boarding-gate quarantine inspection.[^5]

Ask first, then act without waiting for an answer. That sequence would repeat itself for the next eighteen months.

That email later became material for international debate, yet the CDC has never released its full text. Officials separately added an explanation: the email had mentioned that "patients have been isolated for treatment," from which public health professionals could infer the possibility of human-to-human transmission. That clarifying line was added in April 2020 — it was not part of the text sent on December 31.[^5] In many later retellings, the two have been merged into a single statement.

On January 20, 2020, the Central Epidemic Command Center (CECC) was established under Article 17, Paragraph 2 of the Communicable Disease Control Act, opening at Level 3, with CDC Director-General Chou Chih-hao as commander. The next day, Taiwan recorded its first confirmed case — a Taiwanese businessman who had returned from Wuhan. On January 23, the command level was raised to Level 2, with Minister of Health and Welfare Chen Shih-chung taking over as commander. On February 27, it was raised again to Level 1.[^6]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The "Level 3 alert" that Taiwanese people remember and the CECC's own "Level 1 activation" are two completely different systems. The former governs restrictions on people's daily lives; the latter governs the command center's own organizational tier. Once the CECC was raised to Level 1 on February 27, 2020, **it was never downgraded again until it disbanded on May 1, 2023** — through the takeout-only, empty-streets summer of 2021, the command center's own tier never changed at all. Reading "Level 3 alert" as "the CECC dropped back to Level 3" is exactly where the two systems are most easily conflated. The severity people felt and the bureaucracy's own tier are two curves that never moved in sync.

At nine in the morning on February 6, 2020, the name-based mask rationing system went into effect. People could buy masks at NHI-contracted pharmacies using their National Health Insurance card: odd-numbered national ID numbers could buy on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, even-numbered ones on Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday, and anyone at all on Sunday.[^7] The lines Taiwanese people stood in that spring, the numbers they memorized, the pharmacy doors they stared at — that was the most physical form this system ever took.

What eventually shortened that line wasn't in the government's hands. Wu Chan-wei (吳展瑋), founder of the Tainan-based "Howdy Studio," wrote his own map of convenience-store mask stock during the mask-buying chaos; a prototype had already appeared at a Tainan hackathon in late 2019, and the g0v community gradually joined in. Then-Digital Minister Audrey Tang noticed it and reached out, coordinating data openness between g0v and the government; the government later turned pharmacies' remaining mask counts into an open API.[^8] The step most often left out of this chain is the first one: the map's first version came from one engineer's own hands, and only afterward did the government step in to feed it data.

That same year, the "electronic fence" system launched in early February, using cell tower signals from a person's mobile phone number to determine whether someone under home quarantine had left their quarantine location.[^83] This system would later become a constitutional-level controversy — but that was still three years away.

By the end of 2020, Taiwan's report card read: 799 confirmed cases, 7 deaths.[^9]

Except the claim of "eighteen months with zero community transmission" doesn't hold up — two counterexamples fall within that very period. The unlicensed taxi driver who died on February 15 was a confirmed case of local community transmission.[^11] A hospital-acquired cluster at Taoyuan General Hospital in January 2021 produced 11 cases of community spread.[^12] The accurate version is this: breaches did occur, and each time one did, Taiwan poured enormous resources into containing it.

## That April 13 Meeting Left No Record

On April 13, 2021, the Command Center held an expert meeting to discuss relaxing quarantine regulations for foreign airline crew members. The following day, they announced a shift to "3+11"—three days of centralized quarantine followed by eleven days of self-health management—which was implemented starting April 15 based on flight arrival times. On May 31, the CDC stated that this adjustment was discussed across various groups within the Command Center but no meeting minutes were produced. On June 12, the regulations for crew members were rescinded, and those unvaccinated flying long-haul flights were switched to "7+7."[^13]

By late April, the outbreak in Wanhua was already spreading. Retrospective index cases (Case 1424) showed an onset date of April 23. On May 11, seven new local cases were reported, and the alert level was raised to Level 2. On May 15, the Taipei-New Taipei area rose to Level 3, and on May 19, the entire country rose to Level 3. [^14]

The regulations for Level 3 alert were highly detailed: all non-work-related private gatherings of more than five people indoors or ten people outdoors were prohibited, and masks had to be worn at all times when going out. Except for those necessary for life support, maintaining order, essential services, medical needs, and official government business, all other businesses and public venues were closed; nightclubs, bars, gyms, and entertainment venues were also on the list. Dining establishments that could not implement epidemic prevention measures switched to takeout, religious activities such as pilgrimages and temple processions were suspended, and primary and secondary schools nationwide were closed to the public.[^15] The phrase "non-work-related" was rarely remembered as a whole; most people still went to work as usual that summer.

At the 2:00 PM press conference on May 22, the Command Center announced two figures: 321 new local cases, plus an additional 400 cases for "correction and return" (_jiaozheng huigui_), which were backfilled into the daily counts from May 16 to May 21. The technical reason was a backlog in local reporting; over 10,000 test results were stuck at local hospitals. After the Command Center simplified the reporting fields from over twenty items down to eight, they processed more than 14,000 cases at once.[^16] The term "correction and return" was an improvised phrase used during that day's press conference rather than an existing epidemic prevention term; it later became a common slang term used by Taiwanese people to mock any situation involving backlogged or late submissions.

For the next year and a half, society spent its energy on a single question: who should be held responsible for that May. In August 2021, Chen Shih-chung told English-language media, "There was no three-plus-11 loophole," pointing out that after a three-month investigation, the Command Center found no link between crew member clusters and the May outbreak.[^17]

The Control Yuan investigated for nearly a year and a half, and in August 2022, a report was released. Its conclusions were far more conservative than the simplified versions circulating in the public. The Control Yuan held that before deciding to implement the strengthened model, the Command Center had comprehensively considered factors such as the local outbreak at the time, airline management responsibilities, crew PCR test results, and the physical and mental stress involved; thus, it was not an arbitrary decision made without evaluation. Criticism focused on the failure to fulfill the responsibility of providing detailed public explanations and the lack of proactive communication when questioned by the media and legislators. No illegalities were found, and no corrective measures were issued. The Control Yuan simultaneously recommended legislative amendments to specify whether meeting minutes should be produced and made public for major epidemic prevention decisions.[^18]

That recommendation for legislative amendment revealed one thing: current regulations do not mandate meeting minutes. Therefore, the fact that "no record was left on April 13" is both a fact and does not constitute an illegal act. It was a gap in the system, not the fault of any single individual. If you trace this line to its end, that is the answer.

During that same May, Wanhua was hunted by the media and internet users for a long time. There were actually two types of establishments in Wanhua carrying "tea room" signs, which were often conflated: _cha zhuo zi_ (tea tables) only sold tea and simple snacks, which differed in nature from establishments with female attendants. Those who visited were not necessarily the socially vulnerable; the commonality was loneliness. Consumption there cost NT$200 per pot of tea and NT$10 per song.[^19]

The truly scarce resource was not part of this debate: on May 19, the day the entire country entered Level 3 alert, the total amount of vaccine purchased by the government had only reached the batch of 117,000 doses delivered on March 3[^22], plus the 199,200 doses sent via COVAX on April 4[^21]. All of Taiwan had approximately 316,200 doses on hand for a population of 23 million people.

## 15.05 Million Doses Contracted, 2.45 Million Arrived by Mid-July

At the end of 2020, the government announced it would procure approximately 20 million doses of vaccines from abroad, plus a pre-purchase of 10 million domestic doses (5 million each from Medigen and United Biomedical).[^20] The composition of those 20 million foreign doses consisted of 10 million AZ doses, approximately 5.05 million Moderna doses, and 4.76 million doses ordered through the global vaccine supply platform COVAX; Taiwan had paid the security deposit for these in October 2020.[^21]

The first batch of 117,000 AZ doses arrived at Taoyuan International Airport at 10:21 AM on March 3, 2021, via Korean Air flight KE691.[^22]

As of July 15, 2021, the self-purchased arrival figures announced by the Command Center were: a total of 1.303 million AZ doses across three batches, and a total of 1.15 million Moderna doses across four batches.[^23] The combined total from these two major brands was approximately 2.45 million doses, less than 20% of the total contract volume of 15.05 million doses.

Contracted doses, arrived doses, and doses that can actually be injected into an arm are three different numbers. The shape of the "wait" is defined by the gap between these three figures.

> **⚠️ Controversial Perspectives**
> The most common rebuttal to the "vaccines arrived late" argument is: in the first half of 2021, the whole world was scrambling for stock; slow arrival was a supply chain issue, not a choice made by Taiwan. This claim is half-right—during those six months, global production capacity was indeed swallowed up by several large nations that signed contracts first. The other half can be checked against the timeline: by July 15, 2021, Japan had donated 3.34 million doses across three batches and the US had donated 2.5 million doses on June 20; just these donations from two countries totaled 5.84 million doses, more than double the government's self-purchased arrival volume (2.45 million doses).[^23][^24] Those donations were carved out of the same global supply. Shortages were real, but shortages do not explain everything.

Japan eventually donated a total of 4.204 million doses across six batches, making it the country that provided the most vaccine aid to Taiwan globally. The US's 2.5 million Moderna doses arrived in Taiwan on June 20; Poland's 400,000 doses arrived on September 5; Czechia sent 30,000 and Slovakia sent 10,000, while Lithuania's 20,000 doses can only be found via secondhand reports.[^24]

The most heated line of argument that summer concerned BNT. There are three versions of how the deal fell through, each with its own source, and they do not agree on the attribution of "why it couldn't be bought."

In November 2020, the agency agreement for Taiwan's Tongyang Pharmaceutical fell through; the official version was that both sides could not reach an agreement on quantity and price, as the government's initial procurement scale was far lower than the manufacturer's expectations.[^26]

In December 2020, the government entered the final stages of contract signing directly with BNT. The CDC's own account is that BNT requested to remove the words "our country" (我國) from the Chinese version of a press release; the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) sent out a revised version using "Taiwan" (台灣) the next day, after which negotiations ended without success.[^27]

In February 2021, Chen Shih-ming revealed that five million doses had already been purchased from BNT, but plans to announce it in December 2020 were derailed due to "external interference," though he did not name names. BNT's official statement during the same period was: "BioNTech is committed to helping end the pandemic around the world, and our intention to provide vaccines to Taiwan is part of this global commitment. Relevant discussions are ongoing, and BioNTech will provide updates as they become available."[^28]

Shanghai Fosun Pharmaceutical asserted at its shareholders' meeting that the Taiwan agency rights for BNT vaccines were held by Fosun, meaning all official or private procurement had to go through them; the MOHW's position was to ask manufacturers to formally submit import applications.[^29] Later that same year, Taiwanese officials publicly accused China of obstructing the deal with BNT for the first time; China denied this throughout and stated it was willing to provide vaccines to the people of Taiwan.[^30] The only commonality between the three versions is: the direct negotiations at the end of 2020 did indeed fall through.

The knot was untied by the private sector: TSMC submitted an application on June 10; the Yonyou Foundation, founded by Hon Hai founder Terry Gou, announced its contract completion on June 12; Tzu Chi Foundation CEO Yen Po-wen submitted an emergency import application to the FDA on June 23 and confirmed a procurement of five million doses on July 2; and Pegatron Chairman Tong Tzu-hsien donated NT$500 million in his own name to assist with Tzu Chi's procurement funds.[^31]

With five million doses from each of the three parties, totaling a 15 million dose contract, they followed a "manufacturer produced, manufacturer packaged, direct to Taiwan" route, completely bypassing Fosun, with the Command Center handling unified distribution once they arrived. The first batch of 932,400 doses arrived on September 2, 2021, and the final batch arrived on January 27, 2022; a total of 15,207,600 doses actually arrived, which was 207,600 more than promised.[^32]

The contract terms themselves were also debated for a long time, with two key terms being conflated. "Sealed for thirty years" refers to the retention period for official documents; the MOHW clarified that this is a regulation under the Archives Act stating that "documents shall not be destroyed if they are less than thirty years old," not a declassification date.[^33] Commercial confidentiality periods are a different matter: then-MOHW Minister Hsueh Jui-yuan stated clearly at the Legislative Yuan, "For AZ and Medigen, it is 5 years; BNT requires 10 years of confidentiality; as for Moderna, they do not agree to declassification—they will never declassify."[^34]

Additionally, a widely circulated claim that the "Novavax contract has seven-year confidentiality" was a misstatement. CDC Director Chuang Jen-hsiang's comment regarding "Novavax contract confidentiality lasting up to 7 years" actually referred to an H1N1 influenza vaccine contract signed with Novartis in 2009; it involved two different companies, two different vaccines, and two different eras compared to Novavax in 2021.[^35] The Medigen contract was eventually disclosed early on January 16, 2024, more than a year ahead of the originally agreed five-year term.[^36]

Five years later, it was that donation that truly ended up in court. On August 6, 2026, the Taichung District Prosecutors Office in Taiwan indicted seventeen people. According to the prosecution's indictment, a lawyer and a vaccine broker claiming to be a major shareholder of Shanghai Fosun used rhetoric such as "both Hon Hai and TSMC assisted in vaccine procurement through Mr. Li" to induce the Tzu Chi Foundation to pay NT$1,061,981,295; investigators also seized large amounts of gold and cash, and the prosecution has requested the court to confiscate criminal proceeds exceeding NT$1.1 billion.[^1] Defendants are presumed innocent until a verdict is finalized.

Tzu Chi issued a statement that day, stating that the 2021 procurement process adhered to principles of rigorous use of donations, complied with open market standards, and was decided based on publicly available information, all of which were certified by accounting firms and audited by the MOHW. A legal procurement application process and a broker's commission fraud are two different matters; the indictment targets the latter.

During that half-year when the goods were still on their way, the debate within the island was about something else entirely.

## The Same Yardstick: Medigen Failed Both Tests While Medigen Passed

The legal tool Taiwan used to approve Medigen is often referred to as an EUA, but it is actually "special approval for manufacturing or importation" under Article 48-2 of the _Pharmaceutical Affairs Act_. This is a different mechanism from the internationally recognized EUA. The statute clearly states that the central health authority may grant special approval "to prevent, treat, or diagnose diseases that endanger life or cause serious disability and for which no appropriate drugs or suitable alternative therapies are available in the country," or "to meet the needs of an emergency or significant public health situation." The second paragraph of the same article also specifies conditions for revocation, one of which is when "the emergency or significant public health situation has ended," and may require the applicant to dispose of or recall unused drugs within a specified period. [^37]

This exists at a different level than formal drug registration. The subsequent batch of expired vaccines that were destroyed was legally grounded in this very article.

On May 28, 2021, the FDA expert meeting established the efficacy evaluation criteria for domestic vaccines[^41]. On the same day, the CDC signed contracts for five million doses each with Medigen and United Laboratories (Unilabo). [^36] On June 10, the standards were officially announced: clinical trial reports must include at least 3,000 subjects, with follow-up for at least one month after the administration of the final dose[^38]. Efficacy was to be determined by two immunobridging indicators: the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the geometric mean titer (GMT) ratio must reach at least 0.67 times that of the AZ control group, and the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the seroreactivity rate must be above 50%. [^40]

On July 18, Medigen's expert review meeting was held with twenty-one attendees; the chairperson did not vote. The result was 3 passed, 15 conditionally passed, 1 requested additional information for re-discussion, and 1 failed. It was approved the following day, July 19. [^39] Medigen's actual values were a GMT ratio lower bound of 3.4 times and a seroreactivity rate lower bound of 95.5%, both far exceeding the threshold. [^40]

At 10:00 AM on Sunday, August 15, the same set of standards was applied to Unilabo. The venue was Meeting Room F327 on the third floor of Building F in the FDA's National Biotechnology Research Park. There were twenty-two attendees; the chairperson did not vote. The result was 4 requested additional information for re-discussion and 17 failed. The meeting resolution stated: "As the CDC has determined that there is indeed an urgent public health need regarding the epidemic and vaccines in our country, an assessment of the overall benefits and risks of the Unilabo vaccine shows that its safety is acceptable and it shows a trend toward cellular immune response; however, compared to the external control group of citizens vaccinated with AZ, the neutralizing antibody data failed to meet the COVID-19 vaccine efficacy evaluation criteria established by the expert meeting on May 28, 2022." [^41]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The common claim is that "those standards were tailor-made for Medigen." For this accusation to hold, one must first explain one thing: using the exact same criteria established on May 28, Medigen passed, while Unilabo failed both tests. If the standards were truly adjusted for a specific company, the second company would not have been blocked by the same yardstick. The real technical disputes have nothing to do with the Blue or Green political camps; they concern whether neutralizing antibodies as a surrogate endpoint can represent clinical protection, the comparability of external control groups versus head-to-head trials, and whether numbers measured against the ancestral strain can be extrapolated to the Delta variant. These questions were being debated simultaneously in international regulatory science circles in 2021, running on a parallel track to Taiwan's political warfare.

The most powerful part of those meeting minutes is how each of the twenty-one expert speakers expressed themselves. Some wrote only a single sentence; others filled an entire page with details on animal testing. One expert was very specific regarding data for subjects aged 65 and older: "The seroconversion rate for UB-612 in elderly (65+) subjects was only 89%, meaning one in every nine people receiving the Unilabo vaccine fails to achieve a basic immune response. Combined with the fact that neutralizing antibody GMTs in elderly subjects were lower than the overall subject group, the protection offered by the Unilabo vaccine for the 65+ population is likely insufficient." [^41]

Another expert wrote in the same document: "The use of immunogenicity (neutralizing antibodies) as a surrogate efficacy indicator is gradually being discussed internationally." [^41] This sentence refutes the simplified narrative that "review committee members rejected the immunobridging method." The members voted against it because Unilabo's data failed to meet the threshold.

Immunobridging is not an invention of Taiwan: on June 24, 2021, the International Coalition of Medicines Regulatory Authorities (ICMRA) held a workshop co-hosted by Japan's PMDA and the UK's MHRA, with representatives from the FDA, EMA, and COVAX advisory groups in attendance. One of the conclusions was: "Immunogenicity bridging studies may be needed if an assessment of effectiveness of 2nd generation COVID-19 vaccines in clinical endpoint efficacy studies are no longer feasible." [^42]

On September 15 of the same year, the Access Consortium—comprising Australia's TGA, Canada, Singapore's HSA, Switzerland's Swissmedic, and the UK's MHRA—issued a statement: "Access Consortium members agree that well-justified and appropriately designed immunobridging studies are an acceptable approach for authorizing COVID-19 vaccines." [^43] Taiwan set its standards (June 10) two weeks before that ICMRA workshop.

The opposing voices were equally specific: Chen Pei-che, an academician at Academia Sinica, resigned from the FDA's vaccine efficacy evaluation expert group at the end of May. His reason was: "The biggest difficulty in the review is President Tsai; she has already said that domestic vaccines must be administered by the end of July. In this situation, can the FDA withstand the pressure from the Presidential Office? It is a very simple logic." He added, "The review committee faces many difficulties and can no longer maintain its independence and professionalism." [^44] His concerns focused on the political context of review independence, not the scientific basis of immunobridging.

Physician Lin Chin-chia's criticism targeted the method itself: "Medigen's 'immunobridging' method compares the neutralizing antibody titers of the Medigen group against the AZ group for the 'ancestral strain' live virus; this is merely 'building a house behind closed doors' in a laboratory setting." He cited the case of Germany's CureVac, where efficacy was only 47% in a 40,000-person Phase III trial, to illustrate that good immunogenicity does not equate to high protection. [^45] Supporters were also blunt; former Deputy Director of the Department of Health Chang Hung-jen said, "The golden age for Phase III clinical trials of COVID-19 vaccines has passed. Using traditional clinical trials to test the effectiveness of next-generation vaccines will become increasingly unfeasible; immunobridging will inevitably become the 'new Phase III clinical trial' for vaccines." [^46]

Medigen eventually conducted a Phase III trial in Paraguay in collaboration with the University of Asunción School of Medicine. A 2022 interim analysis showed neutralizing antibody titers reached 3.7 times that of the AZ control group, and Paraguay's regulatory agency, DINAVISA, approved its EUA on February 14, 2022. The sample size was approximately 1,000 people, far smaller than the tens of thousands typical of a standard COVID vaccine Phase III trial, and the design was also immunobridging. [^47] While they did conduct it, the scale and design remained points of criticism.

Of the five million doses of Medigen purchased by the CDC, approximately 3.4 million were actually used; about 1.6 million were destroyed due to expiration. [^48] In March 2026, this company obtained marketing authorization in Vietnam for its Enterovirus 71 vaccine, with the first batch of products already arriving in Vietnam, while simultaneously advancing the development of a second-generation multivalent enterovirus vaccine. [^49] The name that caused protests in the streets five years ago now appears in headlines regarding the export of pediatric vaccines.

This argument burned from June to August, consuming the entire summer. Large shipments only began arriving in autumn—later than the end of the dispute, later than the virus entering the community, and later than the time the people who needed it most could afford to wait.

## The Goods Arrived, but the Third Dose for Seniors is Still Lagging

The memory of 2022 is defined by two lines on a rapid test. Entire classes took turns being infected; office seats emptied out week by week; several old neighbors in the village passed away. Being confirmed positive shifted from a major life event to something that was simply "your turn." Starting May 12, for those under home quarantine or medical observation, a positive rapid test confirmed by medical personnel counted as a diagnosis; on May 26, this was expanded to everyone. [^50] The single-day peak of confirmed cases reached 94,808, announced by the CDC on May 27. [^51] (Many retellings remember the peak in June, but that was a prediction from late April; the actual peak arrived earlier.)

In April, Premier Su Tseng-chang proposed the "New Taiwan Model," setting official goals of zero deaths, mitigation toward mild cases, and maintaining normal economic operations. [^52] On October 13, entry requirements changed to 0+7, eliminating the need for home quarantine. [^53] The policy shifted entirely toward coexistence.

Supplies were no longer scarce: by the end of August 2022, the government's cumulative procurement had reached 68.41 million doses, with an expenditure of NT$43.375 billion. This included 41.05 million doses of Moderna and 7.6 million doses of BNT procured directly by the government. [^25]

![2022年健保提供65歲以上民眾免費領取的家用快篩試劑](/article-images/society/covid-free-rapid-test-kits-2022.webp)
_In 2022, National Health Insurance provided free home rapid test kits for citizens aged 65 and above. That year, two lines became a routine verdict. Photo: ThomasYehYeh / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

What was missing was the final stretch: when the wave of deaths occurred, the third-dose vaccination rate for seniors aged 75 and above still lagged behind their own first and second doses. [^56] Two years ago, when supplies were hard to come by, the gap was at the docks. Two years later, with goods piling up in warehouses, the gap had moved to those last few kilometers: the doorsteps of seniors living alone, the vaccination consent forms in long-term care facilities, and the unanswered appointment calls that no one was there to help make.

The final tally for that year looked like this: statistics on causes of death from the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) showed 14,667 COVID-19 deaths in 2022. The ranking of COVID-19 as a cause of death rose from 19th in 2021 to 3rd; the age distribution was primarily among those aged 65 and above, accounting for 86%, with a median age of death at 82—ten years higher than the previous year. [^54] _The Reporter_ used a different metric, reporting 14,699 local deaths, noting that deaths were "almost all concentrated in the population aged 70 and above, accounting for approximately 77%." [^55] Two numbers, two cut-off points, each internally consistent.

Chien Chang-chuan, a professor at National Taiwan University College of Public Health, calculated the pace of that period: from May to December 2022, an average of at least 25 people were confirmed positive every minute, and an average of five people died from COVID-19 every two hours. [^55]

In 2022, total national deaths reached 207,230, an increase of 23,498 over the 183,732 deaths in 2021. This was the first time the figure had exceeded 200,000 since the Ministry of the Interior began its statistics 46 years ago. [^4] There is another way to calculate "how many more people died that year," called excess mortality; public health experts use different baselines to calculate figures that are four to five times higher. [^57][^58][^59]

```tw-note
Explanation
"How many more people died that year" and "how many people died of COVID-19 that year" are two different questions. The latter is a direct count; the former is excess mortality, which requires first estimating a baseline of "how many people would have died if the pandemic had not occurred," and then subtracting that from the actual death count. The answer varies significantly depending on how the baseline is drawn.
A paper published by Kung/Liang/Lu in the *Taiwan Journal of Public Health* (Vol. 43, No. 2, 2024) used monthly mortality data from 2010 to 2019 across various counties and cities to perform quasi-Poisson regression, controlling for monthly effects and population aging. They calculated that after May 2022, national excess deaths totaled 20,702, with indirect (non-COVID-19) deaths accounting for 29.9%. > **📝 Curator's Note**
```
> The common narrative is "Taiwan has the lowest mortality rate in the world, so the cost was small." This sentence conflates two different statistics. The denominator for cumulative mortality is either the total population or the number of confirmed cases; it does not require a counterfactual baseline and its average is pulled down by the extremely low baseline period between 2020 and 2021. Excess mortality looks at the gap between a single year and the pre-pandemic baseline, which requires a model. It is like someone who wasn't sick for the first two years but caught a severe cold in the third: "The average number of sick days over three years is low" and "I feel very unwell this year" can both be true at the same time. The cumulative mortality rate announced by the Command Center in February 2023 was 742.2 per million people, which ranked sixth lowest in a comparison of 38 OECD nations plus Taiwan and Singapore [^3]. In October 2022, Commander Wang Pi-sheng stated that Taiwan had the third lowest excess mortality rate globally [^60]. Both were official announcements, but they compared different sets of countries.

The time bought over eighteen months was not entirely spent on those who needed it most. There is no conspiracy in this statement, nor does it require anyone to have failed in their duty for it to be true; it is written plainly in the median age of death.

## Nearly 90% of Applicants Received a Letter Stating "No Relation"

The cost did not fall only on those who missed their shot. For those who experienced medical issues after vaccination, the path was different.

"I received no consultation throughout the entire review process, and my medical records are very straightforward. How can they decide, based solely on my health checkup data, that my vaccination is unrelated to my myocardial infarction?" said Chen Shih-chin, 51, who suffered a myocardial infarction after receiving the AZ vaccine. [^63]

The system on paper was not strict. The written report provided by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) to the Legislative Yuan stated clearly: According to Article 6 of the _Regulations Governing the Collection and Review of the Vaccine Injury Relief Fund_, applicants only need to submit an application form and do not bear any additional burden of proof; the burden of proof lies with the administrative authorities. The determination of causality is primarily based on the World Health Organization's 2018 updated guidelines for assessing causality in adverse events following immunization. [^64]

A paper comparing the systems in South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan placed Taiwan and Japan on equal footing, describing the recognition standards in both regions as "relatively lenient." In Taiwan, payments are made even when the determination of causality is "related" or "uncertain." [^61]

The "payment even if uncertain" design is unique to Taiwan. As of the snapshot on February 17, 2022, there were a cumulative 4,485 applications, with 351 reviews completed. Of these, 47 resulted in payments (18 judged "related," 29 judged "uncertain"), while 304 were judged "unrelated." This means nearly 87% were ruled unrelated, and the completion rate of reviews was less than 8% of total applications. [^65] Even though Taiwan pays out even for "uncertain" cases, nearly 90% of completed cases are still ruled to have no relation to the vaccine.

By June 8, 2023, 2,689 reviews had been completed, of which 303 resulted in payments (11.26%) and 2,386 were denied relief. [^62]

The reviewers also had their say: Chiu Nan-chang, a physician from the Department of Pediatric Infectious Diseases at Mackay Memorial Hospital and convener of the review panel, explained the logic behind the determinations: "Data from various countries show that mRNA vaccines cause myocarditis, primarily occurring in young people after the second dose... Cancer and coronary artery blockage are formed over many years; they are not sudden accidents. Therefore, it is illogical to rule them as caused by the vaccine." [^66]

The source of funding is also squeezing this system: Taiwan's relief fund comes from a NT$1.5 levy per vaccine dose, whereas Japan and South Korea use a national treasury system. [^61] Lin Hsin-jou, an associate professor at Chang Gung University and a member of the review panel, used a metaphor: "Suppose the original relief fund was meant to handle measles vaccines, but now COVID-19 vaccines have been added... that would make the original pool smaller!" [^63]

Cumulative relief payments and subsidies for COVID-19 vaccines reached NT$153,025,000 by September 2023, exceeding the total relief amount for all other types of vaccines combined between 1989 and 2020, which was NT$129,625,000. In 2023, an average of 235 cases were reviewed per month, nearly eighteen times the 13 cases per month in 2020. [^77] The highest single payment was NT$6 million for a 47-year-old woman who suffered cerebellar hemorrhage complicated by cerebral venous sinus thrombosis and severe thrombocytopenia after receiving the AZ vaccine. [^65]

The second group bearing the cost is in the hospitals. The nursing vacancy rate rose from 4.52% in 2019 to 4.7% in 2021, and to 6.53% in 2022; the turnover rate went from 11.12% to 10.13%, and then to 11.73%. [^69] In 2023, following the pandemic, the turnover rate reached 12.61% and the vacancy rate hit 9.05%, both of which are near ten-year highs. [^70]

On May 25, 2022, the Command Center held a symposium titled "Listening to the Voices of Nursing." Anonymous comments at the scene included: "If you think it's so easy, you try doing it," and "Would an extra NT$2,000 provide an extra pair of hands?" One nursing supervisor from a medical center demanded that the Command Center specify how much general medical care would be reduced for every "30% increase in specialized staff," reasoning that "otherwise, if the hospital director says we must open [new units], we are just lowly nursing directors; how would we dare tell the director that nurses can no longer sustain such a workload?" [^71]

Kao Ching-chiu, former chairperson of the National Federation of Nurses and Nursing Assistants, offered a more long-term judgment: "With the declining birth rate combined with an environment where industry conditions have not improved, the manpower will not return. Manpower shortages may become the norm." [^70]

A three-shift nurse-to-patient ratio was first implemented via an incentive system on March 1, 2024; it was formally codified through an amendment to the _Medical Act_ passed by the Legislative Yuan on May 8, 2026, and will be implemented in stages starting from May 20, 2027. [^72] It took four years from that symposium to its codification into law.

The third group is not even given the chance to participate; their roles are assigned. On June 2, 2021, a cluster outbreak occurred at the Jing Yuan Electronics plant in Miaoli, where the vast majority of the five hundred-plus confirmed cases were migrant workers. On June 7, the Miaoli County Government announced a ban on all foreign migrant workers in the county from going out, except for commuting to and from work; the ban was lifted on June 29. [^73] Taiwanese employees at the same plant were not subject to the same intensity of restrictions.

The legal opinion of the Command Center at the time was very clear: the ban "raised concerns regarding potential differential treatment violating the principle of equality" and, in accordance with the Constitution, international human rights conventions, and the intent of the _Communicable Disease Control Act_, "should have no legal binding force." [^74] The Taiwan Association for Human Rights issued a post on June 18 demanding an explanation from central government agencies. The ban remained in effect until June 29.

A year later, the Control Yuan censured the Miaoli County Government and the Ministry of Labor. The censure stated that "the Miaoli County Government issued a curfew restricting all migrant workers from going out without clear legal authorization, including those with no history of contact with infectious diseases," and noted that the Command Center's failure to explicitly demand an immediate withdrawal "resulted in discriminatory treatment against migrant workers, constituting a significant dereliction of duty." This incident was included in the annual human rights reports by Amnesty International and the U.S. Department of State. [^75]

> **✦** "I feel like I am a criminal."

These were the words of Emma, an Indonesian migrant worker, during an interview with The Reporter on June 22, 2021. She also added: "We are also afraid of being infected." [^76]

The losses in insurance and catering are of another magnitude: combined pandemic-related insurance claims from 2022 to the end of March 2023 totaled NT$264.983 billion, wiping out approximately twenty years of profits for the property and casualty insurance industry. [^67] In May 2021, the month when the nationwide Level 3 alert and the ban on dine-in services were implemented, catering turnover dropped to NT$50.2 billion—a monthly decrease of 23.7% and a year-on-year decrease of 19.1%. Casual dining restaurants saw a year-on-year decline of 30.8%, the largest drop among all types; Western fast-food outlets actually grew by 5.4% due to their customer base favoring takeout. [^68]

There are several other groups that have never been separately counted from beginning to end. No single agency has independently published the annual death toll for residents of long-term care facilities and elderly people living alone. There is no corresponding official survey on the income changes of delivery drivers and daily wage workers during the Level 3 alert period. There are no figures for the vaccine coverage rate of migrant workers or the wage compensation following those three weeks of curfew. There are also no public statistics on the paths for appeals and administrative litigation available after being judged "unrelated." The circumstances of these groups have never been placed into any table.

## No Illegal Acts, Lack of Due Diligence, or Established Causality Found

After the events passed, three different agencies conducted investigations, resulting in three seemingly contradictory conclusions.

From June 2021 to February 2023, the Taipei District Prosecutors Office handled over sixty vaccine-related complaints. The allegations included insufficient procurement, refusal of private donations, the policies and pricing of Medigen, and special approval procedures; in April 2023, the office issued a press release stating that all cases were closed as "no illegal acts found."[^78]

On March 1, 2023, the Control Yuan released an investigation report on vaccine procurement, requesting that the Executive Yuan urge the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) to review and improve areas where vaccine preparation, procurement decisions, and operational procedures were "not sufficiently prudent," relevant laws were incomplete, and information disclosure was insufficient. One such point was the lack of international recognition for domestic vaccines: the Control Yuan stated that at the time, only ten countries recognized Medigen, primarily because there were no traditional Phase III clinical trial results to prove efficacy.[^79]

On the judicial side, after a two-year-old boy passed away from an infection, his father questioned the delay in emergency medical services and sought NT$1 in state compensation from the New Taipei City Government. The court ruled that it was "difficult to establish a significant causal relationship with the actions of public officials," and the appeal was dismissed with finality in November 2023.[^80]

The three agencies were asking three different questions: the District Prosecutors Office asked if anyone was involved in criminal illegality; the Control Yuan asked whether administrative procedures were prudent and whether information disclosure was sufficient; and the court asked whether there was a significant causal relationship between a specific death and the actions of specific public officials. "Illegal acts," "lack of prudence," and "no significant causal relationship" are three different legal standards. Each track answered its own question, and each left behind parts that could not be answered. To read them as if they were contradicting one another is to treat three different rulers as if they were one.

What all three rulers failed to measure was the same thing: who decided the rules during a state of emergency, and when.

One rule concerns whether decisions should leave a paper trail: the meeting on April 13, 2021, had no minutes. After its investigation, the Control Yuan acknowledged that current regulations do not mandate them, and thus recommended amending the law to specify whether meeting minutes for major epidemic prevention decisions should be produced and made public.[^18] This rule was only proposed after the events had already occurred.

The second rule concerns whether authorization is written clearly enough: Article 7 of the _Special Act for Prevention of Epidemic and Relief for Severe Special Infectious Pneumonia_, which passed its third reading on February 25, 2020, consisted of only one sentence: "The Commander of the Central Epidemic Command Center may implement necessary emergency response measures or actions as required for the prevention and control of the epidemic." This Act expired and was repealed upon its term ending on June 30, 2023.[^81]

Lin Ming-hsin, a professor at National Taiwan University College of Law, wrote a monograph in a legal journal examining the constitutionality of this article. He approached it from three angles—the constitutional mandate of clarity, the specificity of authorization, and the separation of powers—emphasizing that even if this article has not been abused currently, the question of its constitutionality warrants discussion.[^82] The Control Yuan used a different set of language, noting that the current legal system "relies heavily on general clauses within laws" and that the "emergency-fication of normal laws" poses a risk of administrative power expansion.[^83] While the scholars' terminology differs from that of the Control Yuan, they are referring to the same issue.

The third rule concerns how data is collected and deleted; technological epidemic prevention is not without legal basis: the electronic fence system cited Article 48, Paragraph 1 of the _Communicable Disease Control Act_, which states "the competent authority may require persons who have been in contact with a patient with a communicable disease or are suspected of being infected to undergo testing; when necessary, they may be ordered to be moved to a designated place for examination, vaccination, medication, or other necessary measures such as control or quarantine in specific areas," supplemented by Article 58, Paragraph 1, Item 4 and Article 20 of the _Personal Data Protection Act_.[^84] These provisions never mentioned mobile phone numbers or base station positioning. The controversy lies in whether the authorization is clear, not in whether a legal basis exists.

The Control Yuan's investigation into the electronic fence found deficiencies in four areas: procedure, administrative transparency, personal data protection, and the legal system. The system was activated in early February 2020, but official operational procedures were not announced until March 8, and the service platform contract did not take effect until March 26.

High-risk groups in Wanhua District were included as subjects for National Health Insurance card notations starting May 15, 2021, yet this was only announced at a press conference on May 28, just as the notation period was about to end. The "maximum of 28 days" for personal data retention was only explicitly written down four months after the system went online, whereas telecommunications providers have a separate one-year retention regulation.[^83]

The rules for the SMS-based contact tracing system were similarly decided as they went: from its launch on May 19, 2021, to its shutdown on April 27, 2022, the National Communications Commission announced that nearly 4.8 billion messages were sent, and the data was completely deleted on May 26, 2022.[^85]

Judge Chang Yuan-sen of the Taichung District Court noted during the operation of this system that the police using SMS contact tracing data as a basis for requesting search warrants raised suspicions. Citizens might have been forced to participate merely to purchase daily necessities, and investigating criminal cases falls outside the authorized scope of "use solely for epidemic investigation." The Command Center responded that data must not be used for criminal investigation; the Criminal Investigation Bureau clarified that they did not directly access MOHW 1922 SMS data but obtained all message content following the procedures of the _Communication Security and Surveillance Act_; meanwhile, the Judicial Yuan stated that judges should make case-by-case judgments or that more precise legislation should clearly define the rules.[^86]

> **💡 Did you know?**
> Taiwan's contact tracing followed the path of base station positioning and SMS reporting. During the same period, many other countries chose decentralized contact notification via mobile Bluetooth—where two phones exchange random codes when close to each other, with the codes staying on the phone rather than uploading location data. The privacy costs between these two paths differ greatly. Why Taiwan chose the former was not part of any public discussion regarding architectural selection at the time; what remains in the literature are the subsequent legal controversies, not the choice of technical route.

In 2023, the Command Center gradually dismantled itself. On February 20, indoor mask mandates were lifted; on March 20, the requirement to report mild cases was changed to self-health management; on May 1, COVID-19 was downgraded from a Category 5 to a Category 4 statutory infectious disease, and the Command Center was disbanded on the same day.[^87] The 960th and final press conference was held on April 27. Over three years, they held 960 press conferences, issued 2,199 press releases, produced nearly 7,000 hand-held signs, and were attended by over 20,000 journalists; at the peak, 220,000 people were in quarantine simultaneously.[^88]

Over those three years, more than ten million people in Taiwan accumulated infections. The cumulative death toll does not align across different official figures: on the eve of its disbandment, CNA cited over 19,000 deaths[^88], while a snapshot from an open data platform in September of the same year recorded 10,239,510 cumulative confirmed cases and 17,668 cumulative deaths.[^91]

On the international stage, Taiwan's situation is a different matter: between 2009 and 2016, it was invited to serve as an observer at the World Health Assembly under the name "Chinese Taipei," but has not been invited since 2017.

In 2023, then-MOHW Minister Hsueh Jui-yuan said in Geneva: "Without WHO membership, Taiwan is also unable to provide various surveillance data to the global influenza surveillance and the response system, which could alert the world to the next pandemic. Taiwan is willing and it should also be included in the pandemic accord that is under negotiation."[^89]

On May 27, 2025, the 78th World Health Assembly passed the _Pandemic Accord_ and the Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing System; eleven diplomatic allies proposed inviting Taiwan to participate as an observer, but Taiwan has still not been invited.[^90]

The world has written Taiwan's epidemic prevention into academic reviews. The article in the _European Journal of Public Health_ noted that while Taiwan avoided nationwide lockdowns by using targeted restrictions, it also pointed out that the vulnerability of the elderly population led to higher mortality and infection rates, as well as a lack of clear transition plans.[^10] During that same period, Taiwan's monitoring data could not enter that system.

On August 4, 2026, the CDC announced the previous week's epidemic situation: 18,990 outpatient visits for COVID-19, a 74% increase from the previous week, along with 62 new local severe cases and 3 local deaths. Director-General Lo Yi-chun estimated the peak would fall around August 16, with weekly outpatient visits potentially reaching 51,000.[^2] The person who saw reports of "unknown pneumonia in Wuhan" via internet messages on that early morning seven years ago held the title of Deputy Director and Spokesperson for the CDC; today, he is the Director-General. The same virus, the same person, spanning seven years.

During those eighteen months, Taiwan did indeed buy time. Whether that time was ultimately used for those who needed it most is another question, and the answer is written in the median age of death.

The remaining threads are also very clear. The Tzu Chi fraud case was indicted on August 6, 2026, and has yet to go to trial. Real-world effectiveness studies following the immunobridging approval cannot be found in published literature. There are no public statistics on appeals or administrative litigation paths after being judged "unrelated." No agency has left behind figures regarding the situation of long-term care residents, elderly people living alone, or migrant workers during those three years. In 2026, government-funded vaccinations are still ongoing, and outpatient visits continue to rise. These statuses are tracked up until August 8, 2026.
