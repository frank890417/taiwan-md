> **30-Second Overview:** In 1994, Taiwan had 58 colleges and universities; fewer than 20% of eighteen-year-olds could attend a four-year university. The April 10 March called for the "broad establishment of high schools and universities," and the government opened the doors using the easiest method: allowing existing junior colleges to upgrade their status. Twenty years later, the number of institutions surged to 148, with net enrollment rates exceeding 70%, allowing women, disadvantaged students, and first-generation college students to enter. Then came the wall of a declining birthrate: student numbers peaked in 2012, but withdrawal regulations were not enacted until 2022. This experiment was designed only for opening; the rules for closing arrived ten years late. Today, as Gaofeng, Yongda, and Toko shut down, the bill falls on students and teachers with the fewest choices.

---

In the summer of 2009, a massive recruitment billboard stood in front of the Pingtung Railway Station, featuring the Yongda Institute of Technology. It was a common scene during Taiwan's era of mass expansion: private schools placed billboards in high-traffic areas, waiting to turn every young person walking out of the ticket gate into a new student. Five years later, Yongda ceased operations. The billboard was taken down, but the school gate remained, and the campus in Linlu Township fell silent.

One billboard opened the door with fanfare; one school closed it in silence. These thirty years tell the story of how Taiwan built an entire system of higher education on a foundation of goodwill, only to begin dismantling it when faced with the wall of a declining birthrate.

## What the March Demanded vs. What the Report Provided

On April 10, 1994, over two hundred civil organizations took to the streets of Taipei. This march, later known as "April 10," demanded four goals: implementing small-class/small-school models, expanding the establishment of high schools and universities, promoting education modernization, and establishing a Basic Education Act [^1]. The "expansion of high schools and universities" was particularly striking because, in that era, university admission was an extremely narrow gate. Mathematician Huang Wu-hsiung, a key advocate, later recalled that in 1994, fewer than 18% of eighteen-year-olds in Taiwan could enter a four-year university [^2]. Most people were funneled into society after taking the joint entrance exams; a university degree was a privilege for the few.

The march demanded "expansion," but no one pinned down the specifics. When Huang Rong-chun, who took over the Ministry of Education later, was interviewed, he stated that the original intent of April 10 was actually for the government to establish more "public" universities [^3]. However, two years later, the _Report on the General Consultation of Education Reform_ submitted by the Executive Yuan's Education Reform Committee explicitly stated a different direction: "The growth of public schools should be slowed, while private schools can be expanded significantly." [^4]

In one sentence, the direction of the door changed. The streets called for public doors; the report opened private ones. This was not a conspiracy, but a shift in policy discourse over two years. The "Nuclear-Style Reform Reporting System" (核定制改報備制) that circulated in rumors does not exist in official records; what remained in the documents was the phrase: "private schools can be expanded significantly." [^4]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The expansion of universities is often told as a story of "idealism vs. greed": idealists wanted to give children opportunities, while private school owners wanted to make money. However, looking at the original texts reveals that the true fork in the road wasn't between good and evil, but an unspoken choice: whether the door should be opened by the public or private sector. The streets chose public; the report chose private—the latter being the path that required the least government expenditure. Thirty years later, almost all of the costs are being settled at this junction that was never seriously debated back then.

When the direction changed, the first unsolvable problem emerged: who decided this direction? Li Yuan-che was the chairman of the Education Reform Committee at the time; in 2005, he apologized to the Legislative Yuan for the reforms, but his scope was narrow, stating that the committee could only submit reports and had no further influence. By 2024, he changed his stance, claiming the expansion was Huang Wu-hsiung's suggestion, saying it "ended up being blamed on me." Former President of Dongwu University, Liu Yuan-jun, rejected this claim, pointing out that all thirty-one members of the Education Reform Committee were selected by Li Yuan-che, and "not a single education scholar" was among them [^5]. Huang Wu-hsiung has defended "expansion" for years, insisting the concept itself wasn't wrong; rather, it was the subsequent unchecked upgrades and lack of supporting infrastructure that failed [^2]. Responsibility became like a ball passed between different people for twenty years, never landing. And whether the ball landed or not did not affect the truth of the next event.

The report described how higher education should expand, encouraging private initiatives and opening doors for private institutions. Yet, in the entire report, not a single page stated who would take over if they had to be closed one day. Years later, education scholar Li Feng-jü, looking back from the perspective of critical pedagogy, argued that this reform was dominated by market logic from the start, pushing higher education toward becoming a commodity [^6]. This is one interpretation. But to truly understand what happened to that door later, one must first see how it was opened. The answer was convenient: "Upgrading."

## Universities Weren't Built; They Were Upgraded

To multiply the number of universities within a decade, the fastest method was not building new schools. Building a university requires land acquisition, construction, and hiring sufficient faculty—a time-consuming and costly process. Taiwan chose another path: allowing existing junior colleges and technical institutes to "upgrade" [^1]: junior colleges became technical institutes, which then became technology universities; the signs were changed once, and the status was raised by one level.

From 1994 to 2023, while many institutions appeared in Taiwan, only 36 were actually newly established; as many as 108 came from upgrades and restructuring, a ratio of roughly three-to-one [^1]. Some schools underwent three consecutive jumps: Xingwu Vocational School first became Xingwu Technical Institute, then Xingwu Technology University [^1]. A single diploma changed its name three times on the same campus.

The flip side of the upgrade wave was the disappearance of junior colleges. In 1994, there were still 72 junior colleges in Taiwan; by 2023, only 12 remained [^1]. This disappearance began long before the withdrawal wave—it was a byproduct of expansion. Every junior college that upgraded to a technology university meant one less place in Taiwan for training skilled laborers and technicians. The vocational education system wasn't knocked down by the declining birthrate; it was diluted away in the tide of "university-fication."

Yan Chang-shou, Chairman of the Public Welfare Platform Culture Foundation, has strong views on this. He cited catering schools as an example: to upgrade to a university, a school must have at least three colleges. Schools forced themselves to add departments just to qualify for the upgrade, diverting resources that should have been invested in their core business: "Schools thought adding departments would help recruitment, but they ended up becoming their own worst enemies." [^7] Students graduating from catering majors often switched careers, while the industry cried out for labor. This old question regarding education culture and technical value actually began earlier than the expansion of universities—it is tied to [education system and admission culture].

Upgrading was the cheapest way to open the door. It made opening fast, cost-effective, and politically easy; no one would oppose a local junior college "upgrading" to a university. However, after thirty years on this path, it was only when they had to turn back that they realized no one had built a road for the return trip. Vocational education paid the first bill, and when it was taken away, almost no one noticed.

## Women, Disadvanted Students, and First-Generation College Students Truly Walked In

If the story ended here by saying "the door was opened incorrectly," it would miss something more important: the door did open, and people truly walked through it.

The group that benefited first from this door might differ from intuition. The fact that Taiwan's female higher education enrollment rate exceeded that of males is not a recent phenomenon. The Ministry of Education’s gender statistics analysis states clearly that "the trend of females exceeding males has continued since the 83rd academic year." [^8] The 83rd academic year was 1995. It has been common for thirty years in Taiwan for women to enter universities and, more often than not, perform better than men. Many people mistakenly mark this intersection as 2015—a full twenty years too late.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Why is a fact from 1995 collectively remembered as 2015? Perhaps because "women surpassing men" sounds like news, and news always belongs to the "recent." But by placing it back in 1995, the significance changes: it happened even before the expansion of universities. The momentum for Taiwanese women to squeeze into the narrow gate of higher education existed before the door was pushed wide open. Expansion did not create this trend; it merely provided more space for it.

The net enrollment rate is another line. In 1991, only about twenty percent of the eligible population in Taiwan were attending university—a hallmark of elite education. In 2004, this figure crossed 50%, officially entering the "massification" stage defined internationally; by 2016, 46% of the population aged 25 to 64 held a degree higher than high school [^9]. The thirty years during which enrollment rose from under twenty percent to over seventy percent also meant that an entire generation became the first in their families to enter university.

The "Star Program" (Fen-xing) is a specific outlet of this door. In 2016, Hsuan Jun-yao from Miaoli's Zhuolan High School used a score of 48 on the General Scholastic Ability Test to gain admission to National Taiwan University’s Department of Forest Environment and Resources, setting a record for the lowest entry score for NTU via the Star Program. He said his thought at the time was "just to prove it to them—I can actually handle the studies." [^10] Only after entering NTU did he realize, "At first, I really thought that because I came in with a label, everyone would look at me differently, but after some time, I realized no one cared." [^10] When the door opens, the labels fade.

This is the most honest defense of this experiment. Because opening the door truly changed lives, "keeping the door open" looked like the rational choice every year. No Education Minister or parent would have been willing to take away the ladder for their own generation.

But honesty has another side. The door being open does not mean that what lies behind it was distributed equally to everyone. Lin Ming-ren, a professor of economics at NTU, found by correlating tax data and student records that children from households in the top 70th percentile had about a 1% chance of entering NTU; for those above the 90th percentile, the probability jumped to 5% or 6% [^11]. The door was open for everyone, but when standing before the best doors, family wealth still decided. The market value of the degree itself also changed: Ministry of Labor statistics show that in 2023, the starting salary for those with a university degree was about 33,000 TWD, only 5,000 more than those with a high school or vocational diploma [^12]. The dividends were distributed, but they became thinner.

Even if it was thin and unevenly distributed, the door still truly changed lives—this is precisely why it became so difficult later. Because the benefits were so real and widespread, when the wall of population finally appeared, no one was willing to shut the door first.

## After Students Began to Decrease, the Withdrawal Rules Waited Ten Years

The door remained open, but the number of people in the classrooms began to dwindle.

The number of students in higher education followed a bell-shaped curve. In 1994, there were 720,000; it climbed steadily until peaking at approximately 1.355 million in 2012, and then began to fall, leaving only 1.095 million in 2023. The Ministry of Education estimates that by 2039 (the 128th academic year), there will be only 905,000 [^13]. From the peak to now, this is equivalent to losing the entire student body of one NTU plus over a dozen medium-sized universities.


The problem was not that no one saw this wall. It was seen very early. As early as 2003, the government followed a resolution from the National Education Development Conference to "temporarily suspend applications for new private universities" [^1]. This was the first brake, though it was pressed lightly and quietly. While late-stage private schools were failing to recruit students, the government simultaneously concentrated large amounts of competitive funding into top-tier universities; this competition for resources pushed weaker institutions further toward the margins [^14]. It wasn't until 2009 that the Ministry of Education officially acknowledged to the Legislative Yuan that "excessive number of schools and over-high admission rates" were major problems; in 2015, they produced the "Higher Education Innovation and Transformation Plan" [^15]. The _Private Higher Secondary School Withdrawal Ordinance_, which finally had legal status, was not passed until 2022. By that time, student numbers had been falling from their peak for a full decade.

```tw-timeline
2003 | First quiet brake | National Education Development Conference resolution to suspend new private university applications
2009 | Official admission of too many schools | Ministry of Education reports "excessive number of schools and over-high admission rates" to Legislative Yuan

2015 | Transformation tools introduced | Ministry of Education releases "Higher Education Innovation and Transformation Plan."

2024 | Four schools close same year | Datong, Mingdao, Global, and Oriental; 898 students were ultimately relocated.

Source: Ministry of Education, Legislative Yuan reports, Higher Education Evaluation Center, Central News Agency
```

The moment the threshold hit rock bottom was visible in the headlines. In 2007, during university placement based on the entrance exam, the Information Technology department at Toko Institute of Technology and Management had a minimum weighted score of 18.47; converted to the original total score, it was about 11.2 points—meaning one could be admitted with an average of 2.8 points per subject. They expected to recruit 66 people but only recruited 46 [^16]. The following year in 2008, the Resource and Environment department at Lide University had a minimum score of 7.69; in the same report, Toko's Environmental and Occupational Health department "failed to attract any" (抱鴨蛋) students. That year, the university admission rate soared to 97.1%, hitting a historic high [^16]. "Getting into college with just a few points" became a joke for everyone.

![Toko Institute of Technology and Management Campus (2008, Puzi, Chiayi)](/article-images/society/toko-university-campus-2008.webp)
_Dijiang Institute of Technology and Management campus (2008, Puzi, Chiayi). Due to its low-score admissions in 2007, it made headlines; it closed and transitioned into a long-term care institution in 2021._

Facing this joke, Huang Wu-hsiung's reaction differed from most. He wrote: "The phrase 'you can get into university with eighteen points on the exam' once became a popular joke. But what is there to laugh about? As an educator, what I see is: getting into university with 18 points means these children did not receive a good education in elementary and middle schools." [^2] The same set of numbers can be read as "devaluation of degrees" or as "children let down by primary and secondary education." This debate has never stopped.

It is worth noting that the schools under the joke had different fates. Lide University did not simply "collapse." In 2011, it changed its name to Kaning University; in 2015, it merged with Kaning Nursing College; and in 2020, the Tainan campus stopped admitting students. The Taipei campus currently operating under the "Kaning University" sign is actually the lineage of the original Kaning Nursing College [^17]. Where the legend of getting into university with low scores went after that is far more complex than the phrase "the 7-point school closed."

Looking back at that series of policies, every brake was one beat slower than the population shift. No policy could stop the wall of the declining birthrate [Taiwan's low birth rate crisis]; the wall was always visible, but the problem was that this vehicle was designed without a brake pedal from the start—it only had an accelerator. The doors were not closed in time, and they eventually had to be closed in the most painful way: letting the schools fall while people were crushed underneath.

## 498 Were Relocated, but Only 339 Graduated

To understand "withdrawal," one must first understand where private school funding comes from. The vast majority of revenue for Taiwan's private universities comes from tuition fees. More students mean more tuition; schools can then pay teachers' salaries, repay bank loans, and maintain the campus. However, as the declining birthrate caused student numbers to drop year by year, this cash flow began to dry up: failing to recruit students meant being unable to pay salaries; once the cash flow was cut, the school could no longer sustain itself. Withdrawal is, ultimately, a situation where a school's cash flow reaches its end.

Pan Kun-zheng stood at that very end. In 2014, he was a senior student at Gaofeng Digital Content College, only one semester away from graduating. In February of that year, Gaofeng became the first higher education institution in Taiwan to close due to the declining birthrate. "I was just one semester away from graduating, and then the school ended. I was quite stunned when I found out," he later recalled [^18].

In the policy dictionary, what happens next is called "relocation": students are transferred to other schools to continue their studies. But in a person's life, the word "relocation" looks different. Pan Kun-zheng was moved to Shude Technology University; he described those days of transferring: "Riding a motorcycle on mountain roads, and the bike wasn't very reliable at the time. The speed on the mountain road could only reach 30 or 40 kilometers per hour; it was very slow." [^18] Two words lightly stated in a policy document became for one person a struggling motorcycle, a mountain road, and a semester that had to be retaken.

> **✦** There is no standard operating procedure (SOP) for closing. Doors do not close gently on their own; therefore, the cost is borne by humans as a buffer.

Furthermore, "relocation" does not equal "graduation." When Yongda Institute of Technology withdrew, there were 625 students across the school; 498 were relocated, but according to a 2017 Ministry of Education survey, only 339 actually received their diplomas. Nearly one-third of those who were relocated did not reach the finish line [^19]. This was not an issue unique to Yongda: for six private schools that closed between academic years 107 and 111, the dropout rate among relocated students was as high as 16.42%, compared to only 7.30% in general higher education institutions [^20].

```tw-stat
498 → 339 | Yongda Relocated Students → Final Graduates | Nearly one-third did not reach the end
16.42% | Dropout Rate of Relocated Students from Withdrawn Schools | General Higher Education was 7.30%
14% | Success Rate of Staff from Closed Private Schools finding new jobs | Total of 1,064 people across 17 schools
Source: PTS, Ministry of Education (via media reports)
```

Yang Dong-shou, a student at Global College of Technology, attributed his alma mater's closure to "failing to curb spending despite clearly being hit by the declining birthrate," and noted that promised housing subsidies and credit transfers were never fulfilled [^21]. Furthermore, the nature of withdrawal is not just about "not being able to hold on." During an investigation by the Control Yuan into Dahan Technical Institute, it was found that before its closure, the school in Hualien still had approximately 500 students, 26 teachers, and over 80 million TWD in campus funds. It did not run out of resources but still applied for closure—if withdrawal regulations cannot control these "running away while there is still room" situations, it becomes a moral hazard [^22].

Beyond the students are the teachers. Of the 1,700 staff members from 17 recently closed private schools, only 14% successfully transitioned to new positions through the Ministry of Education's matching platform [^20]. Chen Po-hien, a researcher at the Higher Education Workers Union, put it more bluntly: "The number who can actually move to other schools as full-time faculty is likely in the single digits." [^20] The rest of the 14% had to find their own paths. Zhang Yi-chung, a professor who left Taiwan Capital University, simply started his own business, arguing that departing professors should not be over-sympathized with [^23]; however, the figures from the matching platform show that only a few are able to walk away like that.

A deeper layer of the bill lies outside the walls of the withdrawing schools. In order to ensure there were enough classes for more than a hundred universities, Taiwan worked hard to cultivate PhDs during its expansion period; by the contraction period, this capacity became an excess in the entire system: among 3,000 to 4,000 new doctoral graduates each year, many could not find full-time faculty positions, and the proportion of part-time lecturers once approached ten percent, becoming "temporary workers" in the academic world [^24]. The people raised during expansion had nowhere to go during withdrawal. There has never been an SOP for closing; the human bill comes first, followed by the financial bill.

## The 200 Billion That Still Haven't Been Calculated

When a university withdraws, many things are left behind: empty classrooms, land, buildings, and equipment. To whom these assets ultimately belong is one of the least calculated parts of this experiment.

The figures themselves are suspicious. In 2015, the Taiwan Higher Education Industry Union targeted sixty private institutions with the fewest teachers and, based on audits by certified public accountants, estimated that if these schools were all withdrawn, the total value of campus assets would reach as much as 200 billion TWD [^25]. However, when four schools actually withdrew in 2024, the actual inventory of asset values was approximately 7.7 billion [^26]. Between 200 billion and 7.7 billion lies a gap that no one has looked back to recalculate: "200 billion" was an estimate from ten years ago, not a current audit, yet it continues to circulate as if it were a present-tense fact.

Where did the assets go? Each case is different. The 2.368 billion TWD in assets of Zhongzhou Institute of Technology were all transferred to the Ministry of the Interior; the 2.483 billion TWD worth of assets from Oriental Design University were handed over to Kaohsiung Institute of Technology for a joint industrial-academic zone [^26]. The properties of withdrawing schools are either turned into public property or repurposed; there is no unified answer. In contrast, when Yongda withdrew, the withdrawal fund only covered 51.58 million TWD for the overdue civil service pensions and retirement benefits of 141 staff members [^33]. Assets in the hundreds of millions versus labor rights in the tens of millions are two different scales of accounts within the same withdrawal.

The operators of private schools on the front lines of withdrawal have their own arguments. In 2018, Tang Yen-po, Chairman of the Association of Private Technology Universities, called for Taiwan to "follow Korea's example," allowing a portion of the assets of withdrawn schools to return to their original foundations to be reinvested in other private schools or social welfare institutions [^27]. This claim is worth serious consideration, but it must be placed in its proper tense: Tang himself said that Korea was also "discussing" how to formulate withdrawal regulations. He cited a proposal under discussion, not an existing system; according to Korean media reports, it wasn't until 2023 that Korea moved toward amending laws to relax dissolution conditions and allow some remaining assets to return to original entities [^28]. Removing the "Korea" packaging, the core issue of this proposal remains unanswered today: would allowing a portion of assets to return to the original foundation provide a dignified incentive for withdrawal, or open a side door for the privatization of campus assets? The Higher Education Workers Union and the Association each hold one end, while Taiwan's legislators have yet to accept his version.

Are there specific cases of "stripping" foundations? This is a matter of fact that can be verified, not a question of stance where both sides are just arguing for their own interests.

> **⚠️ Controversial View**
> In 2015, CTBC Financial Holding used its Taiwan Lottery subsidiary to donate and take over the endangered Xingguo Management Institute, restructuring it into CTBC Financial Management Institute, allowing the school to survive. CTBC stated they "invested over 20 million in education public interest and never sought profit from it." However, a later ruling by the Tainan District Court determined that after CTBC took over, all 28 full-time teachers of the original Xingguo resigned; the school used an opaque "relocation plan" and misled teachers into signing early resignation contracts. The court ruled that the Higher Education Workers Union's criticism that "campus assets became a windfall" was a reasonable comment. The investment was a fact, and the lack of information identified by the court was also a fact. The most honest way to state this case is to separate the "facts found by the court" from the "qualitative judgments still being debated." [^29]

Returning to that 200-billion-dollar bill, the truth is only half of the story: no one recalculated the total, and the judiciary only confirmed the lack of transparency in specific cases; whether there was a systematic stripping of assets remains a matter of debate. This is the deepest layer of "not designing the closing": even after the doors closed, they were still arguing over who owned what while they were closing them.

> **💡 Did You Know?**
> Taiwan is not the only place dismantling universities. Korean media uses the term "Cherry Blossoms Fading in Order" (벚꽃엔딩) to describe how local universities in lower-latitude areas, where cherry blossoms bloom earlier, were the first to close due to the declining birthrate. According to Korean media statistics, between 2000 and 2024, Korea closed 22 universities [^28]. Across the Pacific, the United States is similar: between 2008 and 2023, nearly 300 higher education institutions closed, of which sixty percent were for-profit schools [^30]. The declining birthrate does not discriminate by country; it's just that Taiwan opened its doors much faster and more widely, so the collision with the wall was particularly loud.

When opening the doors, no one asked: "If these schools have to close one day, who do these lands and assets belong to?" That silence of the past is now being answered by every withdrawing school. So, after thirty years, what has this society learned?

## The Transcripts Are Archived, But Teachers and Students Are Still Looking for a Place

Let's look at the report card again: has this society learned how to close the door? The answer is an honest fifty-fifty.

Half of it, the system did catch. Article 20 of the Withdrawal Ordinance explicitly stipulates that when a school ceases operations, students' academic records must be permanently preserved and transferred to a designated school. Since 2018, the Ministry of Education has commissioned Yunlin University of Science and Technology to establish the "Database for Custody of Withdrawn Schools' Student Records"; graduates of withdrawn schools can apply for transcripts online — NT$20 per copy, NT$100 for a degree certificate[^31]. The alma mater is gone, but the paper proving you studied there remains. This is a real, human-catching design; withdrawal does not equal an archival vacuum.

Chunghua Elementary School is another way of catching. After Gaofeng withdrew, its campus was taken over by the I-Kuan Tao Tien Huang Foundation and became Chunghua Elementary School in 2016[^18] — Taiwan's first "university turned elementary school." The declining birth rate killed universities with one hand and created a need for elementary schools with the other; the same campus swapped in a batch of smaller children. Toko University then transformed into a long-term care foundation, and the vacated campus welcomed older people.

But the other half, people are still catching themselves. That 16.42% dropout rate, that 14% teacher career-change rate — they're talking about the same thing: the documents have a custodian, but the people are still looking for a place. And the wall hasn't finished being hit. The Ministry of Education projects that by 2040 (academic year 129), first-year university students will number only 146,000[^32]; starting in 2028, the screening ratio for university admission applications will be adjusted from 3x to 4x, so more examinees can squeeze into the second stage[^32]. As for further ahead, 135,000 births in 2024, only 108,000 in 2025 — this cohort eighteen years later (around 2042, 2043) will be the ceiling for university freshmen, though this already exceeds the Ministry's official projection range and can only be roughly inferred from birth numbers[^13].

If you're a parent wanting to check whether the school your child will apply to is safe, you'll discover something: the Ministry of Education does not proactively publish the complete list of warned and specially counseled schools[^20]. You want to check, but you can't. That silence itself is an answer to the question "for whom do universities exist?"

The story returns to Pan Kun-cheng. Ten years later, he's 33, working as a real estate agent. When asked about his education, he used to mention Gaofeng, but "many people don't know Gaofeng, takes too long to explain, so later he stopped bringing it up and just says he graduated from Shu-Te University"[^18].

The final trace Gaofeng left in the world has two locations. One is in the database, in the Yunlin Tech-managed system, where a transcript costs NT$20 and can be retrieved anytime. The other is in Pan Kun-cheng's self-introduction — a name he no longer speaks aloud.

Opening the door took Taiwan ten years. Learning how to close it took thirty — and they're still learning. That recruitment billboard in front of Pingtung Railway Station came down long ago; Yongda's gate still stands by the road in Linluo Township. Between the door opening and the door closing, it has always been people standing there.
