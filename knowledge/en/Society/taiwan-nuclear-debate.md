On April 28, 2026, the Nuclear Safety Commission (NSC) held its first briefing session in Hengchun Township Office for the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant license renewal. During the Q&A session, Chiang Kuo-liang, honorary chairman of the Chingma Garden Urban-Rural Cultural Development Association, asked about a ballot: last year's referendum on restarting the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant failed to meet the threshold, so why restart it? "And restarting doesn't even require an environmental impact assessment?" In response to the referendum failure, Taipower Deputy General Manager Hsu Yung-hui replied: "The extended operation plan is just a plan." He compared the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant to a retired athlete, now merely confirming whether they have the qualifications to return to the field.[^1]

On the same day, Hengchun Township Mayor Yu Shih-ching said he was "optimistic" about the restart, believing that the "yes" votes outnumbered "no" votes in last year's referendum, showing that restarting is the consensus of the local majority, provided the reactor is safe.[^2] In that vote, Hengchun Township cast about 60% "yes" votes, with a turnout of 27.33%.[^3] Outside the township office, Ta-kuang Village cultural worker Lung Chang-wei said at a civil groups' press conference: "I am a Hengchun resident, I oppose nuclear power."[^4] Also in Ta-kuang Village, Village Chief Chiang Chin-chiao said on last year's ballot counting day that more "yes" than "no" votes represents the local voice; residents have coexisted peacefully with the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant for 40 years with no safety concerns.[^3]

The government says nuclear power restart must meet three conditions: safety assured, nuclear waste resolved, and social consensus achieved.[^5] The competing interpretations in Hengchun center on the third condition. The first condition is written in law: the Nuclear Reactor Facility Regulation Act stipulates that for a reactor whose license has expired to resume operation, the competent authority must confirm "no safety concerns." The NSC reviewed this for half a year following this procedure, issuing 589 review opinions and approving Taipower's extended operation plan.[^6][^7] Neither this law nor its review regulations specify what constitutes achieving social consensus. When asked at the Legislative Yuan in November 2025, Economic Affairs Minister Kung Ming-hsin said, according to United Daily News, that social consensus mainly means nuclear safety.[^8] On November 28, Case No. 22 "Abolish Nuclear-Free Homeland" referendum will be voted on the same day as the nine-in-one elections; how to read the resulting numbers, all sides have already spoken.

> **30-Second Overview:** Whether the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant can resume power generation, 2026 proceeds on two tracks. One at the NSC: Taipower submitted the extended operation plan in March, approved on September 24, followed by implementation results report, autonomous safety inspection report, and license renewal review. The other at the ballot box: Case No. 22 asks "Do you support the government using 'nuclear power' to revitalize the economy, to abolish the 'nuclear-free homeland' energy policy?", voting on November 28 alongside the nine-in-one elections. Of the three restart conditions, only safety has regulations specifying how to review it. The voting results are scheduled to be announced by the Central Election Commission (CEC) on December 4.

## The Legislature's Final Case: Main Text Changed Before the Vote

On 21 November 2025, the Legislative Yuan passed a third-reading amendment to Article 23 of the Referendum Act, binding referendums once again to the same day as general elections: a referendum must be held within three to six months after it is announced as established, "and if a national election falls within that period, it shall be held on the same day as that election." [^9][^10] On 26 June the following year, KMT Chairperson Cheng Li-wen said merely restarting the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant was not enough; she would propose a referendum on restarting nuclear power, arguing that restarting takes at least three years and the existing nuclear plants cannot all be brought online. [^11] On 14 July, the KMT caucus's referendum proposal was referred directly to second reading in the plenary session. [^12]

On 14 August, the plenary session processed the opposition caucuses' referendum proposals in sequence; the "Abolish Nuclear-Free Homeland" referendum was the last item. [^13] The original main text read: "Do you agree that the government should abolish the 'Nuclear-Free Homeland' energy policy to ensure public health, stable power supply, affordable electricity prices, enhanced national defense resilience, and support for artificial intelligence (AI) industry development?" [^14] Before the vote, the KMT caucus submitted an amendment changing the main text to the version now printed on the announcement: "Do you support the government using 'nuclear power' to revitalize the economy, thereby abolishing the 'Nuclear-Free Homeland' energy policy?" The DPP objected; the amendment was put to a vote and passed 59 to 51. [^13][^14][^15]

During the vote, DPP legislators held placards reading "Restarting nuclear power requires nuclear waste, nuclear safety, and social consensus — a hollow referendum cannot solve this." After passage, KMT legislators raised signs reading "Restart nuclear energy to mind the economy." Each side's placard gave one reason; the DPP's included the four characters "social consensus."

_CEC Announcement No. 1153150284 issued 28 August 2026; the first page lists the voting day, voting hours, case number, and main text. Source: Central Election Commission announcement._

The statement of reasons announced alongside the main text comprises four sections. The first discusses international trends, citing the EU Taxonomy for Sustainable Finance, remarks by Jensen Huang and Elon Musk, and the Trump administration's nuclear energy targets. The second addresses public opinion, claiming that returning to nuclear power has become an overwhelming cross-party consensus in Taiwan, citing a Taiwan Inspiration Association poll released in May that asked whether the Fourth Nuclear Power Plant should be activated provided safety standards are met — 79% supported: "This shows that regardless of blue or green, regardless of stance, nearly 80% of Taiwan's people deeply realize that Taiwan cannot afford power shortages or soaring electricity prices." [^15][^16]

The third section proposes building new-generation large reactors or small modular reactors at the Fourth Nuclear Power Plant site, using nuclear energy for seawater desalination and hydrogen production. The fourth section concludes: the Nuclear-Free Homeland "under the test of science, democracy, and international trends, no longer conforms to Taiwan's national security and economic interests today." [^15] Fellow party legislator Ko Ju-chun put it more succinctly: "Without electricity, there is no computing power; without computing power, there is no economy, no national security, no sovereignty." [^17]

On 17 August, multiple environmental and human rights groups demanded the CEC block this referendum case. Green Citizens' Action Alliance Secretary-General Tsui Su-hsin said: "Taiwan's energy future should not be designed by political parties with referendum topics full of misleading content, nor should it become a tool for political party calculation." In the same breath she also criticized the Lai administration for "still talking about restarting nuclear power." KMT legislator Lai Shih-bao responded the same day: "We urge the CEC to view our referendum case from a neutral and independent stance. What computing power needs most is electricity; electricity is insufficient, everyone knows this, so restoring nuclear power is absolutely necessary, so the Nuclear-Free Homeland should be removed — let the people decide." [^18]

On 28 August, the CEC's 626th committee meeting reviewed the three referendum cases referred by the Legislature. On the Abolish Nuclear-Free Homeland case, "apart from Committee Member Chen Yueh-tuan who abstained, the remaining six members all agreed it complied with regulations," determining it belonged to the creation or rejection of a major policy; the caning and traffic fine cases in the same batch were rejected. [^19] Case No. 22 became the only one of the three to be established; the CEC estimates administering a single referendum case costs about NT$810 million. [^20]

## First Year of Nuclear-Free: Nearly Half of Power from Gas

On May 17, 2025, the operating license for Unit 2 of the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant expired, and from that day Taiwan had no nuclear power plants generating electricity.[^21][^22] That year, natural gas accounted for 47.8% of national power generation, coal about 35%, renewables about 13%, and nuclear just 1.1%; thermal power's share rose from 83.0% the previous year to 84.7%.[^22][^23] The lost nuclear generation was made up by thermal and renewables, with both coal and renewable generation reaching record highs.[^23]

The United States and Israel began attacking Iran on February 28, 2026, after which Iran nearly closed the Strait of Hormuz.[^24] Acting Director-General of the Energy Administration Wu Chih-wei said on March 5 that 34% of Taiwan's natural gas imports passed through the Strait of Hormuz in 2025; the statutory safety stockpile is 11 days, to be raised to 14 days by 2027.[^25] Minister of Economic Affairs Kung Ming-hsin said the same day that if the shortfall widened in April, activating standby coal-fired units would not be ruled out, calling it a "last resort."[^26] In late March he said natural gas stocks had remained above 12 days since the war began.[^27] On April 6, Taipower confirmed the Ministry of Economic Affairs had asked it to dispatch Units 1 and 3 of the Formosa Plastics Mailiao Power Plant in the short term, and said annual coal consumption would not exceed the previous year's.[^28]

The timeline for restarting the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant had been set by the Ministry of Economic Affairs before the war: on November 27, 2025, it approved Taipower's assessment of nuclear plant status, finding the No. 1 Nuclear Power Plant not feasible for continued operation, while the No. 2 and No. 3 Nuclear Power Plants were; Taipower planned to submit restart plans for both plants in March the following year.[^29] On March 22, 2026, Lai Ching-te explained why nuclear power was being reviewed: "Taiwan's power supply is stable, but Taiwan has entered a new situation; in the AI era, the electricity needed to build computing centers exceeds our original estimates." TPP Chair Huang Kuo-chang responded: "We hope the DPP government will formally abandon this anti-nuclear sacred cow and face the erroneous energy policies of Taiwan under DPP governance."[^30]

Electricity price pressure was also accumulating: on March 27 and September 18, 2026, the Electricity Price Review Committee twice decided against adjustments, keeping the national average rate at NT$3.7823 per kWh, the first applying April through September, the second from October onward.[^31][^32] According to Taipower's rate review filing, a 12.83% increase was originally slated for October; the Ministry of Economic Affairs instead prioritized securing a NT$71.1 billion government subsidy for Taipower, pushing the rate issue to the third committee meeting in December.[^32] On October 28, Kung Ming-hsin told the Legislative Yuan that if the subsidy bill failed, "if we reach a dead end, the only option is to reflect it in prices."[^33]

On June 2 at a computer expo, Lai Ching-te said, "Right now, Taipower's reserve margin is around 21%," and "This stable power supply will have no issues until 2032."[^34] In early September the Atomic Energy Council said the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant review had entered its final stage; KMT Culture and Communications Commission Chair Chen Yi-hsin said on September 3, "If you knew this would happen, why did you do it in the first place?" calling it a "major wipeout" of the DPP's decades-long nuclear-free policy.[^35] In October Taipower said reserve margins for July through October were all green lights.[^36]

Whether there is a power shortage can be gauged by the reserve margin figure. Whether society agrees to restart nuclear power, each side holds different numbers.

## 589 Review Comments, Approval Granted to a Plan

On May 23, 2025, the amended Article 6 of the Nuclear Reactor Facility Regulation Act was promulgated, allowing Taipower to apply for renewal of the operating license for the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant (Kuosheng), whose license had expired, pursuant to Article 6, Paragraph 3.[^21] On October 8, the Nuclear Safety Commission (NSC) revised the Regulations for Review of Applications for Nuclear Reactor Facility Operating Licenses, requiring applicants for license renewal to submit a restart plan covering six items: site and unit status and schedule; organization, personnel, and training; work items and maintenance inspection; operating specifications and document restoration; quality verification and audit; and "other matters designated by the competent authority."[^21][^37]

On March 21, 2026, Lai Ching-te attended the Pan-Shih Club chairmanship handover ceremony in Taichung. The Presidential Office press release stated that Taipower was expected to submit its restart plan to the NSC for review by the end of the month, and that "three key factors will be weighed, including post-extension safety of the units, nuclear waste disposal, and social communication and public consensus."[^38] On March 27, Taipower submitted the license renewal application and restart plan for the No. 3 plant to the NSC, which entered substantive review on April 8.[^39]

Progress on the No. 2 Nuclear Power Plant (Kuosheng) lags behind No. 3. In November 2025, the Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA) stated that the spent fuel in the No. 2 reactor must await the commissioning of the outdoor dry storage facility before it can be removed; only after the core is emptied can relevant reactor safety inspections proceed.[^29] In May 2026, the NSC said it had not yet received the No. 2 plant's license renewal application and restart plan, which were expected to be submitted in the second half of the year.[^40] Regarding the outdoor dry storage progress, the MOEA said in January that completion and commissioning were expected by year-end.[^41] By September, the Economic Daily News reported that Taipower said the dry storage site had been completed and obtained the NSC's hot-test permit, and planned to initiate the reactor's autonomous safety inspection after partially removing fuel from the core; Minister of Economic Affairs Kung Ming-hsin said that as long as personnel safety can be ensured, it may proceed.[^42]

At the Hengchun briefing on April 28, NSC Nuclear Safety Control Division Director Kao Pin stated: "In addition to conducting substantive review, the NSC will also perform on-site verification to confirm whether operations are proceeding according to proper planning."[^2] On August 1, the NSC held a second briefing in Hengchun.[^43] On September 24, the NSC approved the restart plan: after three rounds of review, the review team raised 589 comments, all of which Taipower had responded to and explained. The NSC clarified that the completion that day was the review of the restart plan; subsequent procedures and safety control operations still need to be carried out in accordance with regulations.[^7][^44]

After approval, Taipower must conduct personnel training, equipment inspection and testing, and document restoration per the plan, and submit an execution results report; the first phase is expected to be submitted in Q2 2027.[^21] Concurrently, the autonomous safety inspection proceeds, including aging assessment and management, radiation-related issue verification and assessment, and seismic safety assessment.[^7] Taipower has already contracted with Westinghouse (USA) to perform equipment aging assessment; Taipower estimates the entire autonomous safety inspection will take 18 to 24 months, and the report must likewise be submitted to the NSC for review.[^45][^46] Under the review regulations, only after the execution results report is approved by the NSC and the operating license is reissued can Taipower reload nuclear fuel into the core.[^37]

As for when the No. 3 plant might resume power generation at the earliest, Minister of Economic Affairs Kung Ming-hsin said in December 2025 that, following NSC review, the earliest restart would be 2028. NSC Chairperson Chen Ming-chen said the same month at the Legislature that a definite timeline was difficult to give; if Taipower performs thoroughly and on-site inspections proceed smoothly, a 2028 restart "is certainly possible."[^47] Chen Ming-chen also said in April 2026 that the review must proceed step by step and cannot be accelerated.[^39] Taipower Chairman Tseng Wen-sheng, questioned by legislators in March on whether the autonomous safety inspection could be completed in under 18 months, countered: "Would faster be better? If safety isn't properly addressed, would faster be better?"[^48]

At the Hengchun session on April 28, the NSC also said it would consider how to translate complex nuclear information into forms the public can easily understand, to promote public participation.[^1] These rounds of review all concern the units and documents. Whether society accepts restart is not addressed in the first five items of the restart plan; the sixth item is left for the competent authority to designate separately.

## Social Consensus: The One Condition Among Three That the Law Doesn't Say How to Measure

Nuclear Safety Commission (NSC) Chairperson Chen Ming-zhen stated in May 2025 that restart requires three major conditions: nuclear safety, nuclear waste disposal solutions, and social consensus. She also said that the decision to restart lies with the policy competent authority; the NSC is merely a regulatory body that reviews and decides on approval according to international standards.[^49] On October 22, 2026, Presidential Office Spokesperson Guo Ya-hui, when asked about the stance on Case No. 22, said Taiwan has already achieved the nuclear-free homeland goal and power supply is assured; Lai Ching-te does not rule out any energy options, and nuclear restart must meet three necessary conditions: safety assurance, nuclear waste resolution, and social consensus.[^5]

| Condition                | Where It's Written & Who Handles It                                                                                                                           | Progress as of Early November 2026                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Safety Assurance         | Nuclear Reactor Facility Regulation Act Article 6 and Regulations for Review of Applications for Nuclear Reactor Facility Operating Licenses; reviewed by NSC | Restart plan approved in September; implementation results report, self-initiated safety inspection report, and license renewal pending review |
| Nuclear Waste Resolution | MOEA drafting High-Level Radioactive Waste Siting Act; Taipower planning on-site dry storage                                                                  | Siting Act had no concrete version as of January; dry storage facility at Third Nuclear Power Plant still in planning                          |
| Social Consensus         | Neither Nuclear Reactor Facility Regulation Act nor Review Regulations have corresponding provisions                                                          | NSC solicits local opinions during review period, incorporates them as reference for review                                                    |

As for how to measure social consensus, at least three approaches have emerged this year. The KMT's explanatory statement cites nationwide figures, referencing polls saying nearly 80% support Lungmen (Fourth Nuclear Power Plant) activation, aiming to use this referendum to "transform this public opinion into substantive policy."[^15] The second approach lies within the review itself: Kung Ming-hsin's November 2025 statement was that social consensus mainly means nuclear safety; if that can be assured, he believes opinions will converge.[^8] The NSC says it solicits opinions from local governments during the review period, and also collects opinions from local communities and civic groups through local briefing sessions and visits to local figures, incorporating them as reference for the review.[^50]

The third approach looks at the locality where the power plant sits: In April, groups including the Citizens of the Earth Foundation held a press conference in front of Hengchun Township Office, urging the ruling party to lay out clear procedures and thresholds for how the three principles would be concretely implemented, so the public can scrutinize them.[^1] At the second briefing session in August, a joint statement from multiple groups said the concrete standards of the three principles are difficult to clarify, urging the NSC, Taipower, and the Ministry of Environment to respond to civil demands as soon as possible.[^51]

The plant localities themselves don't speak with one voice either: In Wanli, New Taipei, where the Second Nuclear Power Plant is located, Dapeng Village Chief Li Chien-tsai said on March 21, the day Lai Ching-te spoke on nuclear restart: "Are you treating Wanli as second-class residents?" What he wants is: "Not everything should be decided by the center; the locality must participate in oversight mechanisms; if safety conditions cannot convince the locality, we oppose restart to the end."[^52] At the second Hengchun briefing session on August 1, local resident Hsiao Kai said: "The Third Nuclear Power Plant provides job opportunities, and it also gives back to Hengchun Township in moderation — some local repair funds." Speaking for the Pingtung County Government, Deputy Secretary-General Chiang Kuo-feng said: "Safety, safety, and more safety — this has always been the county government's very clear and primary stance on the Third Nuclear Power Plant's restart."[^43]

These three approaches measure different objects: one is the nation's eligible voters; one is the safety of the reactor units themselves; one is the people living around the power plants who bear the risk.

## Thirty-Two Years Ago, Gongliao Voted Once Before

On the evening of May 21, 1994, an anti-nuclear briefing was held at Renhe Temple in Gongliao. This historical material comes from the fan page "Their Nuclear Stories," which compiled and posted these materials before the 2021 Lungmen referendum while urging the public to vote "disagree"; the _Liberty Times_ reported on the content: that day the media released a poll saying over half the nation supported building Lungmen, and the moderator asked, "Has anyone here been asked by this survey?" The crowd shouted back: "No!"[^53]

The next day, Gongliao Township held its own self-organized referendum on Lungmen; among the residents who voted, roughly 96% disagreed.[^53][^54] The Referendum Act did not take effect until 2003, so this vote had no legal force. A nationwide poll and a local vote produced opposite numbers; three decades later, people are still arguing over which number counts.

In 2014, Lin Yi-hsiung went on a hunger strike to halt construction of Lungmen, and anti-nuclear groups and civil society occupied Zhongxiao West Road and Ketagalan Boulevard.[^55] On April 27, President Ma Ying-jeou, concurrently serving as KMT chair, convened a meeting of KMT-affiliated county and city heads; the conclusion was that Lungmen Unit 1 would not be constructed, only safety-checked, then mothballed after the check, while Unit 2 would be halted entirely. The next day, Premier Jiang Yi-huah announced this decision at a press conference.[^56] On November 24, 2018, Case 16 "Nuclear for Green" was voted on the same day as the nine-in-one elections; turnout was 54.83%, with 5,895,560 yes votes accounting for 59.49% of valid votes, and it passed.[^57] What it repealed was the deadline in Article 95, Paragraph 1 of the Electricity Act — the clause requiring all nuclear power generation equipment to cease operation by 2025. The Taiwan FactCheck Center subsequently consulted Associate Research Fellow Su Yen-tu of Academia Sinica's Institute of Law, who said: "The reasons and assertions stated in the referendum proposal's explanatory statement are provided for voters' reference, and are not the subject of the vote."[^58] The four units of Nuclear Power Plants 2 and 3 were sequentially shut down between 2021 and 2025.[^59]

On December 18, 2021, Case 17 was the Lungmen referendum; this time it was not bundled with an election, but voted on the same day as three other referendum cases; turnout was 41.09%, with 4,262,517 no votes exceeding the 3,804,689 yes votes, and it did not pass.[^60] On August 23, 2025, Case 21 asked whether Nuclear Plant 3 should continue operating after the competent authority confirms no safety concerns; yes votes totaled 4,341,432, no votes 1,511,693, with yes accounting for 74% of valid votes and turnout at 29.53%. Yes votes represented only 21.7% of the total electorate, failing to reach the one-quarter threshold, so the result was not adopted.[^61]

In Hengchun Township, Case 21 produced 4,103 yes votes and 2,658 no votes. The aforementioned Chiang Kuo-liang, speaking that day in his capacity as former president of the Pingtung South Community University, said Hengchun's turnout was only about 30%, and the result could not prove the claim that 70% of local residents support the license extension.[^3] KMT Youth Department Director Lee Chang-chih, at the Youth Lecturers Corps formation press conference in October 2026, looked at the national picture: the last Nuclear Plant 3 referendum saw over 4 million people cast yes votes, with yes at roughly 70%, and yes votes outnumbered no votes in every county and city.[^62]

## Before the Count, Each Side Defines What This Ballot Means

KMT Chair Cheng Li-wen said at the Central Standing Committee meeting on September 16 that for the referendum, please vote "agree" — agree to fully restart nuclear power and increase nuclear power's share of electricity generation in Taiwan.[^65] On September 3, the KMT announced it would hold a hundred referendum campaign rallies going into communities, with the theme "Energy is national security, computing power is national strength, fight for energy for AI, vote agree for development."[^35] The TPP caucus supported this case in its August vote.[^14]

DPP Legislative Yuan Caucus Secretary-General Chuang Jui-hsiung spoke about this referendum on September 3. According to United Daily News, he said the Lai administration has three principles for restarting nuclear power: nuclear safety without worry, nuclear waste solutions, and need for social consensus. Even if the referendum passes, nuclear waste disposal still has hurdle after hurdle to clear. Asked whether passage would mean restarting nuclear power, he said: "I think that's jumping too fast." The report also wrote that he indicated the DPP caucus has no preset position and will take it one hurdle at a time.[^66] In his National Day address on October 10, Lai Ching-te did not mention the referendum or nuclear energy. The energy section stated: "Energy policy cannot be built on slogans; it must be built on safety, professionalism, and responsibility," and "We do not exclude options for ideological reasons, nor will we sacrifice generational sustainability for short-term interests."[^67]

On September 29, 34 civil groups, labor unions, enterprises, and legislators' service offices issued a joint statement calling for expanding the professional composition of the subsequent review of the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant and establishing a cross-ministerial decision-making mechanism with central and local government participation. According to CNA, the statement said if the ruling team treats this referendum result as an important political decision for restarting the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant, or even as the basis for so-called "social consensus," it would be putting the cart before the horse; a national referendum should not serve as political authorization for restarting the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant. The statement also demanded that residents of areas that might bear accident risks be allowed to substantively participate in decision-making.[^68]

Legally, if Case No. 22 passes, under Article 30 of the Referendum Act, for major policies "the president or the competent authority shall take necessary measures to realize the content of the referendum case," and "for major policies enacted or decided by referendum, administrative agencies shall not alter the governance content of such enacted or decided cases within two years."[^10] The proponents' interpretation is written in the explanatory statement: to use this direct popular will to "fully authorize the Legislative Yuan to urge relevant administrative agencies to implement nuclear energy promotion policies and complete related laws and regulations."[^15] The main text does not specify which law to repeal; Article 23 of the Environmental Basic Act — "The government shall formulate plans to gradually achieve the goal of a nuclear-free homeland" — remains on the books.[^69] Attorney Chan Shun-kuei believes the government has already initiated safety inspections and administrative procedures for restarting the No. 3 Nuclear Power Plant; even if the referendum passes, the substantive policy effect is limited, while instead increasing public expenditure and election administration burden. NCCU College of Law Associate Professor Lin Chia-ho, however, believes referendums should not replace the legislature's responsibility.[^17]

From November 12 to 20, the CEC held five opinion presentation sessions on five terrestrial TV stations. By law, the Legislative Yuan represents the pro side, while "relevant government agencies or opponents permitted to establish offices under Article 20 of the Referendum Act represent the con side."[^70]

Voting takes place from 8:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. on November 28, with the CEC scheduled to announce results on December 4.[^15][^71] Passage requires simultaneously satisfying two conditions: agree votes exceed disagree votes, and agree votes must also reach one-quarter of the total eligible voters.[^10] The number of eligible voters will be announced by the CEC before November 24; last year for Case 21, eligible voters numbered 20,002,091, with a threshold of 5,000,523 votes.[^71][^61] Regardless of passage or failure, under Article 32 of the Referendum Act, the competent authority may not re-propose the same matter within two years from the announcement of results.[^10]

## The 3,785 Assemblies in the Fuel Pools

Taipower's restart plan states that the Unit 1 spent fuel pool at the Third Nuclear Power Plant holds 1,879 assemblies, while Unit 2 holds 1,906. Each pool has a design capacity of 2,160 assemblies, and the two pools "retain space for two full fuel cycles' worth of spent fuel assemblies before the dry storage facility becomes operational." No dry storage facility exists on site yet; the plan calls for "planning the construction of a spent fuel dry storage facility within the Third Nuclear Power Plant" to transfer assemblies that have cooled in the pools for years.[^21]

At the April briefing, Lung Chang-wei asked Taipower: "If the Third Nuclear Power Plant continues operating, how will nuclear waste be handled? Will it just stay in Hengchun indefinitely?"[^1] The plan specifies on-site storage. As for a final disposal site, the Ministry of Economic Affairs said in January that the siting act for high-level radioactive waste still has no concrete version.[^41]

At the same briefing, Hsu Yung-hui said a nuclear plant cannot achieve one hundred percent safety. He promised that once all autonomous safety inspections are complete, Taipower would explain to the public how much "residual risk" remains at the Third Nuclear Power Plant.[^1] The safety portion of that explanation has the Nuclear Safety Commission's review process to receive it. Whether society accepts those residual risks — by what metric, measured by whom, and how much is enough — will still need answering after the votes on Case 22 are counted.
