> Taiwan's young adults aged 25–35 are living through an undeclared survival experiment. The median salary sits at NT$38,000, while a Taipei studio apartment starts at NT$14,000. One full-time job isn't enough, so they open delivery apps after work, take freelance gigs on weekends, edit videos late at night. This isn't a "slash trend" — it's a survival strategy forced by structural crisis.

Hsiao-chieh, 28, graduated from a private university with an English degree. By day she works as a social media editor at a mid-sized marketing firm, earning NT$33,000 a month. Every evening at six, she clocks out and switches identities: hops on her scooter, opens the Uber Eats app, delivers until 10 p.m. On weekends she picks up translation jobs on 104 Freelance — a 3,000-word Chinese-to-English piece pays NT$2,500. Three jobs combined bring her monthly income to around NT$52,000. After deducting NT$13,500 rent for an 8-ping (≈26 m²) studio in Taipei's Songshan District, scooter fuel, food, and student loan repayments, she saves less than NT$8,000 a month.

"I'm not lazy," she says. "One salary just doesn't cover living."

This is the portrait of Taiwan's slash generation. Not the glossy "portfolio career" narrative on LinkedIn, but a survival formula hammered out under the pincers of structurally stagnant wages and soaring rents.

## The Numbers Speak: Wages Can't Catch Rents

Let's look at wages first. According to the Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS) 2025 data, the median regular earnings of all employees stood at NT$38,319. Note: this is the _median_ — meaning half of all workers take home less than this each month. The average starting salary for fresh university graduates is about NT$34,000; for non-STEM majors, falling in the NT$28,000–32,000 range is extremely common.

Now look at rents. The average monthly rent for a private studio in Taipei City is NT$14,400; even in New Taipei City it's NT$13,100. By the internationally accepted "rent shouldn't exceed 30% of income" benchmark, renting a studio in Taipei requires a monthly salary of at least NT$43,000 to be considered healthy. But a fresh graduate earning NT$34,000 spends 42% of income on rent alone.

And that's just renting. For buying, Taipei's price-to-income ratio hit 16.36× at end-2024 — meaning a household saving every penny of income without eating or drinking would need over 16 years to afford a home. This figure exceeds London, New York, and Toronto, ranking among the highest globally for major cities.

How much does a master's degree help? Fresh master's graduates (non-tech) average starting salaries of NT$38,000–42,000 — only a few thousand more than bachelor's holders. But Taipei rents don't give discounts for degrees. A humanities master's starting salary in Taipei might barely cover studio rent plus basic living costs. The wage premium from two extra years of study is almost entirely swallowed by rent.

## Delivery Platforms: A Generation's Airbag

Taiwan's delivery rider count surged from 45,000 in 2019 to 145,000 in 2022 — tripling in three years.

Who are these 145,000? 104 Job Bank analyzed 8,402 résumés with delivery experience: 42.9% started riding before age 24, mostly university students and youth under 29. Median monthly earnings: NT$36,000. Median tenure: just 0.9 years — most quit within a year.

Delivery isn't a career destination; it's a transition. An airbag when the day job doesn't pay enough; a buffer when the ideal job isn't found after graduation.

In January 2026, the Legislative Yuan passed the _Delivery Workers' Rights Protection and Delivery Platform Management Act_ on third reading, mandating a minimum per-order fee of NT$45 and an hourly wage no less than 1.25× the minimum wage; it took effect at midnight on July 21, 2026 (see [Delivery Platform Act](/en/society/delivery-platform-law/)). This is a belated institutional patch. Before this, riders languished in a Labor Standards Act gray zone: not formal employment, no labor/health insurance, no occupational injury coverage, no base pay. Platforms could unilaterally cut per-order fees; riders had zero bargaining power.

"Having laws to govern" is progress, but it also reveals something: a livelihood for hundreds of thousands waited nearly seven years for basic legal protection.

## Not Just Delivery: The Full Spectrum of Side Hustles

Delivery is just the lowest barrier to entry. The side-hustle spectrum of Taiwan's youth is far wider than imagined.

**Freelance design and engineering.** 104 Freelance, Tasker, or direct via social media. A young person with UI design skills earns NT$38K at their day job, then builds a landing page at night for NT$15,000–30,000. Software engineers do better: a React frontend project can command NT$50,000+. The upside is high hourly rates; the downside is unstable pipeline — "feast this month, famine next" is the norm.

**Online teaching and knowledge monetization.** Hahow has accumulated over 1,000 online courses with 800,000 members. PressPlay Academy hosts 500+ creators. A hit course can bring millions or even tens of millions in revenue — but that's the pyramid tip. Most course creators earn far less, while production costs are high: filming, editing, marketing, typically three to six months of work.

**Self-media.** YouTube, Instagram, Threads, podcasts. Lowest barrier, most uncertain ceiling. Taiwanese YouTubers typically need 100,000+ subscribers for stable ad revenue. Before that, self-media is more like "a side hustle where you pay with your time."

**Proxy shopping and e-commerce.** Japan/Korea proxy buying, Shopee sellers. Thin margins but fast cash flow. Many young people start with proxy shopping and gradually build a small e-commerce operation.

Per 104 Job Bank's 2025 survey, 39% of office workers have a side job. By generation it's more striking: Gen Z (born ~1997 onward) hits 57%, Millennials 48%. yes123 Job Bank found an even higher figure: 93.2% expressed willingness to become slash workers.

The top reason? 78.8% said "to increase income." Not self-actualization — money isn't enough.

## Freelancers in Convenience Stores

Taiwan has a globally rare sight: rows of people working in convenience stores.

Over 13,000 convenience stores nationwide, nearly all with seating, free Wi-Fi, and power outlets. A NT$45 americano buys an entire afternoon. For freelancers and slash workers with no budget for coworking spaces, 7-ELEVEN and FamilyMart are the cheapest mobile offices.

Cafés too. Taiwan has an exceptionally high density of no-time-limit cafés; Cafe Nomad lists hundreds suitable for work, tagged with Wi-Fi speed, outlet count, noise level. The existence of this directory itself proves the scale of demand: an entire cohort needs a cheap place to sit with a laptop and earn money.

Taiwan ranked 12th globally and 1st in Asia in the 2024 Global Digital Nomad Report. Behind this ranking isn't just foreign remote workers enjoying low costs — a large part of the ecosystem is sustained by local freelancers and slash workers.

## Gray Zones: Reality Outpacing Law

Taiwan's Labor Standards Act doesn't prohibit moonlighting. The Ministry of Labor's stance: workers have the right to do other work outside normal hours; employers cannot punish them for it.

Sounds progressive. Reality is more complex.

Many companies' work rules or employment contracts hide "non-compete" or "exclusive service" clauses banning other paid work. Legally, such clauses are contestable: non-competes require reasonable compensation and a maximum two-year limit. But most young employees don't know this and won't confront their employer. Result: they do side hustles, but secretly.

Another gray zone is tax. Freelance income, delivery earnings, e-commerce revenue — all theoretically must be declared. But vast numbers of micro-transactions — a NT$3,000 design gig, an extra NT$15,000 from a month of deliveries — fly under the reporting radar. Not deliberate evasion; the system simply hasn't caught up to gig economy speed.

The labor protection gap is wider. Taiwan had 804,000 atypical workers in 2024, 6.95% of total employment. That number looks modest, but it only counts those whose _primary_ job is atypical. People with a day job who deliver or freelance at night — their side-hustle identity is invisible in labor statistics. No labor insurance, no occupational injury coverage, no pension contributions. If a rider gets hit while delivering, the day-job employer's labor insurance won't cover it — it's not a commute.

## Structural Problems Won't Be Solved by Individual Effort

A brutal fact: from 2012 to 2023, Taiwan's price-to-income ratio surged 77%, while median wages rose only 14%. This scissors gap cannot be closed by "adding one more job."

104's survey shows 57% of Gen Z have side hustles. This statistic is often packaged as "young people are so driven" or "slash is the new trend." But put it back in context — a generation where over half feel one full-time salary cannot support life — and it's not a trend. It's a warning signal.

Taiwan's AI industry boosted semiconductor exports; 2024 electronic/information product exports grew 52% YoY. GDP looks good; per capita income ranks high in Asia. Yet DGBAS simultaneously noted that the share of employees earning below the average wage hit a historic high of 69.77%. Economic gains are highly concentrated in specific industry chains; most people don't share in them.

Young people see clearly. They don't complain — they open delivery apps. They don't wait for policy — they freelance on weekends. They don't dream of buying homes — they calculate how to survive.

The slash generation's story is ultimately an arithmetic problem: when income growth perpetually lags living costs, you either accept getting poorer, or you split yourself into several people.

Taiwan's youth chose the latter. But this shouldn't be celebrated. It should be treated as a problem to solve.
