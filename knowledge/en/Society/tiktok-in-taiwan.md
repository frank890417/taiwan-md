> **30-Second Overview:** TikTok is more than just a short-video platform for fame; in Taiwan, it serves as a "decentralized" cognitive experiment. Through hyper-personalized pushes, it allows users to unconsciously internalize the political agendas of others into their own worldview. A 2025 survey shows that over 70% of Taiwanese adolescents aged 15 to 18 spend more than two hours daily on TikTok; as they scroll, their favorability toward China is quietly rising, while their negative perceptions of Taiwanese society deepen.

15 seconds—that is all the time TikTok needs to capture a Taiwanese soul. As you swipe the screen, the algorithm begins calculating your pupil dilation, your clicks, and your preference for certain rhythms within milliseconds. This is not mere entertainment; it is a predatory war over Taiwan's attention and identity.

### From Musical.ly to Global Hegemony: Algorithmic Victory and Taiwan's Challenge

The TikTok story began in 2016 when the Chinese company ByteDance launched "Douyin." In 2017, ByteDance acquired the US-popular teen app Musical.ly for $1 billion and integrated it with the international version of Douyin to create TikTok. This merger was not just market expansion, but an algorithmic evolution that allowed for the precise capture of global user preferences[^5].

Unlike Facebook or Instagram, TikTok's core competitiveness lies in its "For You Page" (FYP). It does not rely on your social connections, but on your behavior. It understands your desires better than you do, pushing the next video to you even before you realize what you like. In Taiwan, this highly personalized recommendation mechanism allows specific information to penetrate users' daily lives rapidly and covertly.

### National Security Risks and Data Privacy: Taiwan's Digital Sovereignty Crisis

TikTok's national security risks and data privacy concerns have always been focal points for the Taiwanese government and society. Many nations worry that TikTok could be used to transmit user data to Chinese servers, potentially serving as a tool for the CCP to manipulate public opinion and divide Taiwanese society[^14]. The FBI has warned that TikTok could become a tool for China to influence public opinion during an invasion of Taiwan[^14]. An investigation by the European Commission in 2023 confirmed risks regarding data storage and transmission, leading to bans on TikTok on multiple official devices[^15].

In Taiwan, although the Executive Yuan has prohibited the use of TikTok on public sector devices, its popularity among the general public remains extremely high. Cybersecurity experts point out that a ban limited only to the Executive Yuan is far from sufficient; TikTok can pivot from a video platform to a propaganda weapon at any moment upon request from the Chinese government, posing a substantial threat to Taiwan's digital sovereignty[^14][^22].

### Dangerous Challenges and Youth Well-being: The Digital Trap for Taiwan's Adolescents

Driven by the allure of traffic, TikTok has spawned countless "viral challenges." However, these often carry lethal dangers. Between 2022 and 2023, the "Blackout Challenge" erupted globally, encouraging participants to use ropes or belts to choke themselves until they lost consciousness. This challenge resulted in at least 15 child deaths[^4].

In Taiwan, adolescents have also followed trends like the "scar challenge" or the "instant noodle challenge." Behind these behaviors lie deep peer pressure and a craving for engagement. When the algorithm detects that certain dangerous videos generate high interaction, it unconsciously amplifies their reach, creating a vicious cycle of "the more dangerous, the more popular." Furthermore, the immersive influence of the algorithm on adolescents has caused parental concern regarding long-term impacts on mental health, such as shifts in values and decreased academic focus[^16][^23].

### Invisible Cognitive Warfare: Content Censorship and "Destroying Confidence in Taiwan"

The most controversial aspect of TikTok in Taiwan is its role as a tool for "cognitive warfare." During the 2024 Taiwan presidential election, TikTok was flooded with videos alleging that the Central Election Commission (CEC) engaged in "ballot rigging." Posts by influencers like Alice (愛莉莎莎) garnered millions of views in short periods, triggering a serious crisis of trust in democracy[^2]. The Taiwan FactCheck Center noted that many recent instances of political disinformation originated from TikTok videos, which often repurpose news footage with fabricated narratives to spread across other social media platforms[^2].

Research by Watchout indicates that TikTok's algorithm carries distinct value preferences. Research from the Network Contagion Research Institute (NCRI) in the US confirms that searching for sensitive terms like "Tiananmen" or "Xinjiang" on TikTok yields a much lower proportion of "anti-China" videos compared to YouTube and Instagram[^13]. Conversely, TikTok pushes massive amounts of "unrelated" content—such as beautiful scenery or food in Xinjiang—using "video ocean tactics" (影海戰術) to dilute user attention regarding human rights issues[^13].

Even more alarming is that platform content is alleged to align with the CCP's "main melody," deliberately obscuring China's political realities and being used to produce United Front work targeting Taiwan's youth. This propaganda does not directly shout "China is great"; instead, it uses subtle methods to make young Taiwanese people doubt their own society, government, and democratic institutions, leading them to feel that "Taiwan is terrible"[^17]. This strategy of "destroying confidence in Taiwan" is more pervasive and harmful than direct political propaganda. Yang Shun-ching (楊順清), head of the intelligence analysis group at Taiwan Lab, shared multiple cases noting that many rumors preceding the 2024 presidential election originated on TikTok, demonstrating its massive influence on Taiwan's information environment[^24].

### The Truth Behind the Data: Reshaping Taiwanese Identity

According to the _2025 TikTok User Survey Report_ released by Taiwan Lab, active TikTok users show significantly higher favorability toward China than non-users and are more prone to "skepticism toward the US" (疑美論) and pessimism regarding Taiwan's economic prospects[^1]. Using counterfactual analysis, this report confirms that "active TikTok usage" itself has a causal impact on the political attitudes of the Taiwanese people[^3].

Watchout further cited research showing that nearly half of those who use TikTok for more than three hours a day hold more positive views regarding human rights in China[^12]. This "digital charm offensive" is quietly altering the sense of identity among Taiwan's younger generation. Data even shows that the strength of Taiwanese identity among the 20–24 age group has loosened; they are no longer the demographic with the strongest identification with Taiwan, breaking a long-standing pattern[^9]. This indicates that TikTok influences not only perceptions of China but also indirectly affects young Taiwanese people's sense of their own identity.

### Taiwan's Challenge: The Tug-of-War Between National Security and Free Speech

Currently, the Taiwanese government has only banned TikTok within the Executive Yuan and its subordinate agencies, yet its civilian penetration remains extremely high. While many Western nations have implemented bans, a total ban in Taiwan could involve restrictions on freedom of speech. Finding a balance between maintaining national security and protecting free expression is a primary challenge[^18]. This struggle over regulation and free speech tests both government wisdom and the media literacy of civil society. Cybersecurity experts have called the government's handling of TikTok "half a step too slow," urging more proactive response strategies[^14].

In the era of short-form video, we take away more than just knowledge; we take away a new way of seeing the world. As our fingers continue to scroll down, perhaps we should stop and ask: Is this the world I chose to see, or is it the world that has been chosen for me?
