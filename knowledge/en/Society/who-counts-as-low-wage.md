# Who Counts as Low-Wage: Minimum Wage Guards Base Pay, Low Wages Move into the Year-End Bonus Column

There is an office worker surnamed Lee who has been in the workforce for six years. When she first started out, she worked as a planner at a PR firm; after her probation period she earned NT$28,000 a month. After deducting labor and health insurance, that worked out to NT$160 an hour — less than a part-time student worker. Over these years prices have kept climbing: a portion of takoyaki that used to cost NT$45 now costs NT$70, and add a hand-shaken drink and a single meal equals an hour of her wages.[^1]

Over those same six years, the minimum wage has risen year after year. The figure she earned six years ago has now fallen below the minimum wage. She says that once the minimum wage broke NT$30,000, companies should have raised salaries accordingly; otherwise they are effectively condemning their staff to the minimum-wage tier.[^1]

> **30-Second Summary:** Taiwan's minimum wage has risen for ten consecutive years and now stands at NT$29,500 — already above the low-wage threshold commonly used internationally — so the DGBAS says that by monthly salary calculations, no one in Taiwan is low-wage. But if you calculate based on total annual take-home pay, 1.26 million people are low-wage. The difference lies in the year-end bonus column: base pay is protected by law, while year-end bonuses are decided by the company. Low wages haven't disappeared; they've moved into industries without bonuses.

## Takoyaki Now Costs NT$70, and Her Salary Is About to Be Caught by the Minimum Wage

A rising minimum wage is supposed to be good news. This January, minimum-wage earners automatically saw their pay slips increase by over NT$900, and the Ministry of Labor estimates 2.47 million workers benefited from the hike.[^26] But when the floor catches up to someone six years into their career, the good news becomes something else: six years of seniority, and in the base-salary column they stand on the same line as a fresh hire. She says the company should raise wages accordingly — that is exactly what she means.

In August, at the National Model Worker Commendation Banquet, President Lai Ching-te said the minimum wage "only needs one more increase to exceed NT$30,000"; his logic is that as long as there are profits, wages should be raised for workers.[^2] Breaking NT$30,000 is a raise for minimum-wage earners; for those whose base salary sits not far above the floor, it means being caught up. The same sentence, heard from different positions on the pay slip, becomes two different stories.

The same report features two more people standing at the edge of the line: Ms. Chiu, 24, earns exactly NT$30,000 a month. Living at home, she "can't just pay nothing" — she hands over NT$15,000 each month for rent, utilities, gas, and management fees. She says her salary can't keep up with prices, and life is pretty tough. Ms. Lin, in her twenties, earns NT$32,000. She rents in New Taipei and commutes to Taipei for work. After labor and health insurance deductions, she still has to pay student loans and rent; after work she often buys discounted food at convenience stores.[^1] Their base salaries are both just above the floor. When the floor rises again next year, they will be one step closer to the line.

On September 14, the Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS) released July salary statistics. On Threads, someone posted "Sorry, it's me dragging everyone down again," citing the overall average monthly total salary for all employed workers — over NT$70,000. The poster said they fell far short; over 300,000 people saw that post.[^3] So does Taiwan have a low-wage problem or not? The DGBAS gave its answer that day.

## DGBAS Says Zero People; It Measured the Base Salary Column

That afternoon at 4 p.m., the DGBAS released statistics.[^14] Deputy Director-General of the National Statistics Center Tan Wen-ling said at the press conference: "The low-wage threshold is NT$27,598; those below this figure are low-wage employees. Domestic full-time employees are protected by the minimum wage of NT$29,500, so in terms of monthly salary, our country has no low-wage situation."[^4] Taiwan has no statutory definition of low wage; she borrowed the OECD's yardstick: line up all full-time workers' salaries, and two-thirds of the middle person's salary is the low-wage line.[^5]

Using this yardstick to measure this July's monthly salaries, the line falls at twenty-seven thousand and change. The Minimum Wage Act stipulates that the wage agreed upon by labor and management cannot be lower than the minimum wage.[^6] The floor is nearly two thousand higher than the line; by law, the number of people below the line should be zero. Zero people is how it comes out when measured this way; arithmetically, there is no error.

At the same press conference, she gave another figure. CNA reported her as saying: looking at monthly salary, because of the minimum wage guarantee, in principle no domestic full-time employees would fall below the OECD low-wage line. Estimated from annual total salary, however, 1.26 million people meet the low-wage definition.[^7] Calculated by the money actually received over a full year, one in six people falls below the line. The next day, the opposition parties heard the first figure; the Taiwan People's Party issued a statement saying the DGBAS's published data "should not redefine what constitutes 'low wage' just to whitewash the situation."[^8]

Zero people and 1.26 million are actually talking about the same group of people; the difference lies only in which column of the pay slip is used for measurement. The monthly salary yardstick measures base pay; the annual salary yardstick includes year-end bonuses, performance bonuses, and overtime pay. A person's base pay clears the line, but if they receive no bonuses all year, their annual salary falls below the line.[^7] The people who make the difference live in the second column.

That "dragging everyone down" post was actually criticizing a different number. In the first-half statistics released by the DGBAS in August, 70% of all employees had salaries below the average; this proportion has risen for six consecutive years, exceeding 70% for the first time this year, and gets cited every time it's released.[^9] Tan Wen-ling's response was "below average does not equal low wage"; the average is pulled up by the small number of high earners on the right, so the majority falling below average is the norm for any salary distribution.[^10] Mean, median, and the two-thirds line — three yardsticks measuring the same group of people, but only the last one asks who counts as low-wage.

## The Year-End Column Has No Law Guarding It

Taiwanese people's memory of wages splits into two columns. The base salary column records numbers: 22K, ten consecutive increases, breaking 30K. 22K was a figure left by a subsidy program in 2009, later becoming the synonym for an entire generation's starting salary. [^11] The year-end column records scenes: whether the company pays before Lunar New Year, how many months' worth, the boss's word. The law only reaches the first column.

The base salary column is guarded by the Minimum Wage Act: Article 4 divides the minimum wage into monthly and hourly types, uniform nationwide, regardless of industry or company size. Article 5 states that wages agreed upon by labor and management shall not be lower than it. [^6] The year-end column has no corresponding article. Article 29 of the Labor Standards Act only says companies with surplus should give bonuses or dividends. The Taipei City Department of Labor's FAQ puts it more plainly: "Year-end bonuses given by employers to workers according to custom belong to worker welfare; the conditions and standards for distribution may be agreed upon by labor and management in the labor contract, or stipulated by the employer in work rules." [^12] According to custom. One column is law, one column is custom; one column is pushed by the Legislative Yuan and the Review Committee, one column is decided by the boss.

Someone with a base salary of NT$30,000 earns NT$360,000 over twelve months; with no year-end bonus, the whole year stops at NT$360,000, below the NT$390,000 line. If that same person gets one more month at year-end, it's exactly NT$390,000, standing on the line. One month's bonus decides whether the same person counts as low-wage. The law guards the NT$30,000 figure, but cannot guard that one month.

The width of this column is determined by industry: DGBAS itself has explained that the gap between annual and monthly salary mainly comes from year-end bonuses, performance bonuses, and overtime pay. [^13] Using DGBAS figures from January to July this year across industries, in the finance and insurance industry, over 40% of the money received in a year resides in this column. Industries like accommodation and food services, beauty and hairdressing, have only a little over 10%. Both called "annual salary," they are completely different things in different industries.

The industry with the widest second column also has the highest base salary; the finance and insurance industry's monthly median salary from January to July is just over NT$60,000, the highest in the service sector. The industry with the narrowest second column happens to be the one where base salary is closest to the floor. The two industries with the lowest monthly median salaries from January to July are other services and accommodation and food services, both around NT$31,000–NT$32,000. [^14] With base salary clinging to the floor and no second column at year-end, these people's total annual income falls below the NT$390,000 line.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
> The common saying goes: the minimum wage has already surpassed the OECD low-wage threshold, so Taiwan has no low wages. The order of this statement is reversed. The line is calculated from the median; the floor is pushed up by law. The floor pushing past the line proves the law guarded the base salary column; it does not prove Taiwanese people's total annual income crossed the line. Using the same ruler to measure annual income, 1.26 million people stand below the line. The ruler hasn't changed; it measured the column the law cannot reach. The opposition and DGBAS each hold a number; both numbers are correct; what they're arguing about is actually the upper and lower columns of the same payslip.

## The Year-End at the Hair Salon, and the Year-End at the Bank

The DGBAS's 2024 annual salary table, sliced by industry and age, shows who stands closest to the line. If you have a friend working as an assistant at a hair salon, look at her pay stub: the base salary column is protected by law; the year-end bonus column depends on the boss. In the 2024 annual salary statistics, beauty and hairdressing are grouped with repair of personal and household goods under "Other Service Activities." The median annual salary in this sector is NT$385,000 — the only industry whose median itself falls below NT$390,000.[^15] More than half the people in this line of work stand below the low-wage line.

The table doesn't have your friend's name, but her annual salary is in there. Whether the boss pays a year-end bonus, and how many months' worth — the DGBAS table doesn't show it, but her bankbook does. The bank on the same street falls under "Financial and Insurance Activities," where the median base salary is over 90% higher than in the hairdressing sector, and the year-end bonus column is in a completely different league.[^14]

Those below the line are also concentrated in a few sectors: education (including preschool education), arts and entertainment, and accommodation and food services — the bottom quartile in all these sectors earns below NT$390,000.[^15] By age, it's both ends: people under 25, and people over 65 still working. Those just entering the workforce and those working past retirement age — their total annual earnings sit closest to the line.

Outside that ruler, there's another group of people who don't even have a base salary column. Chang Yung-shun, after graduating from the Department of Silver Industry Management at Chaoyang University of Technology, worked on the front lines as a care service worker for three years. In March 2025, _CommonWealth Magazine_ profiled him: when he first entered the field doing home care services, for three months he never took home more than NT$25,000 in a single month. Home care workers and agencies operate on a piece-rate basis — how many cases they get, how much they collect per case — all are variables. He said: "It takes near-perfect alignment of timing, conditions, and connections to earn more."[^16]

Three months later he gave up home care services and moved to a residential long-term care facility with stable pay. In the same report, a long-term care provider ran a recruitment ad proclaiming "Congratulations to Home Care Workers Earning NT$100,000 Monthly" — which, when calculated, requires working over 200 hours a month, including on national holidays.[^16]

Beyond piece-rate home care workers, there are part-time gig workers ([Taiwan's Slash Generation](/en/society/taiwan-slash-generation-multi-job-economy/)), self-employed workers without employers, and foreign domestic caregivers not covered by the Labor Standards Act — these three groups alone total nearly two million people. They fall under neither the zero-person ruler nor the 1.26-million ruler.[^17] The self-employed alone number 1.26 million, almost exactly matching the number of people below the line, yet they are two completely different groups. For them, the year-end doesn't even allow the question "is there a second column?"

The median monthly salary for part-timers is NT$16,000, with an average hourly wage of NT$231 — slightly above the minimum hourly wage. The OECD's ruler only measures full-time workers; hourly workers have no low-wage line of their own.[^14] Industrial migrant workers in factories are covered by the minimum wage, so their base pay follows the floor — but their "second column" is called overtime pay, and the bonuses they receive in a year can't match a few months of overtime.[^28]

## Snack Shop Owner Says NT$196 Per Hour Can't Find Anyone

![MOL September 26, 2025 Press Release Chart: Minimum (Basic) Wage Increased for Ten Consecutive Years, Monthly Salary from NT$20,008 in 2016 to NT$29,500 in 2026](/article-images/society/mol-minimum-wage-ten-year-chart-2025.webp)

_Figure: MOL Press Release, OGDL-Taiwan-1.0_

This line didn't move for nearly ten years from 1997 to 2007; the same number was printed in the MOL's tables for a decade. Starting in 2016, it rose ten times in a row, a cumulative increase of 47% over ten years.[^18] The people pushing for it are in the Legislative Yuan and the Review Committee; the people paying for it are in the shops.

A snack shop owner surnamed Yu told TVBS in September that there are now too many food and beverage businesses, competition in the labor market is fierce, and offering NT$196 per hour simply cannot find anyone; the actual going rate is NT$220 to NT$230.[^19] Some applicants may not have the skills to match, but still want to ask for the market rate. Snack shops are a high-volume, low-margin business; he said if the minimum wage rises again, the shop can absorb it by earning a little less, and menu prices would probably go up by five or ten dollars.[^19] For him, the floor has long been below the market price. If the minimum wage rises again, what actually moves in the shop are those five or ten dollars on the menu.

At the Review Committee meeting last September, management representative and CNCI Standing Director He Yu stated directly who actually hires at the minimum wage. Industries like semiconductors, ICT, electronic components, and computers don't hire at the basic wage. Those who do are mostly SMEs, sole proprietorships, and traditional industries.[^20] Hair salons and snack shops weren't named, but they are mostly exactly these kinds of sole proprietorships and micro-enterprises.[^19]

Before this year's Review Committee meeting, management's rhetoric shifted. CNA President Hsu Shu-bo argued that "instead of raising the minimum monthly wage across the board, why not consider raising the hourly wage for part-timers," reasoning that every time the monthly wage is adjusted, it mostly affects migrant workers, increasing the burden on enterprises.[^21][^29] The "333 Meeting" on the big enterprise side said a 3% adjustment would be more reasonable; once the minimum wage rises, labor and health insurance premiums rise along with it, accounting for about 20% of personnel costs.[^22]

On the labor union side, Taiwan Labor Front Secretary-General Yang Shu-wei responded: the minimum wage only solves part of the low-wage problem, cannot solve widespread low wages, and creates "wage compression," with the minimum wage adjustment magnitude gradually catching up to middle-income earners' wage growth.[^23] On decoupling hourly and monthly wages, he said monthly and hourly wages have already decoupled to a certain extent, and the gap should not widen further. If enterprises increasingly tend to use hourly-paid flexible labor, it amounts to shifting business risks like order fluctuations and insufficient hours onto workers.[^21]

The numbers for the "catching up" he mentioned are as follows: from 2016 to 2024, the minimum wage rose 37%, while over the same period the annual median salary of all employees rose 18%.[^24] The speed at which the floor is pushed up is twice the speed of that middle person's salary growth. The people paying the wages and the unions are arguing over how fast and how high to push the floor. From start to finish, no one at the table talked about the second column.

## September 24, the Floor Rises Once More

![Ministry of Labor entrance, lintel reads 207 Songjiang Road, revolving door in center, glass sliding doors on both sides, August 2023](/article-images/society/mol-main-entrance-2023.webp)

_Photo: Solomon203, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

On September 24, the day before the Mid-Autumn Festival, the Minimum Wage Review Committee met at the Ministry of Labor. Twenty-one people sat down: seven labor representatives, seven management representatives, four scholars, and three government representatives.[^6] They had only two cells to decide: next year's monthly and hourly minimum wages. The data on the table was the Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS)'s newly revised economic growth rate, nearing 10%, and an inflation rate under 2%; outside estimates predicted it would break NT$30,000.[^25] The law requires the committee to reference the inflation rate; labor productivity, average wage growth, and minimum cost of living are optional references.[^6] There was no third cell on the table. The year-end bonus column has never been within the scope of deliberation; the law never gave it a seat.

That day's meeting ran from 10 a.m. to 5:30 p.m.; the decision was a monthly salary of NT$30,900 and an hourly wage of NT$205 starting January next year, a 4.745% increase, the eleventh consecutive annual hike, pending Executive Yuan approval and announcement.[^30]

At last year's session, labor and management reached consensus in the morning that the inflation rate must be faithfully reflected. Management said economic growth was driven by semiconductors and high-tech, while traditional industries and services remained in difficulty, advocating a minor adjustment. Labor said inflation is felt more keenly by low-income earners; scholar representatives believed the floor should be at least the inflation rate. In the afternoon, scholars proposed a compromise; Minister of Labor Hung Shen-han mediated and set it at 3.18%.[^26] Those 2.47 million beneficiaries were all in the first column; the second column was not in that meeting room.

This time, labor committee members specifically named youth starting salaries and atypical workers, demanding a fair share of the fruits of economic growth. Management committee members said they support reasonable, moderate adjustments, but require complete data.[^25] The two sides want different things, but the same column: youth starting salary is the first column, hourly wage is the first column, data is first-column data. The DGBAS has a webpage called the "Salary Platform" (薪情平台); enter your total annual salary and it tells you where you stand across Taiwan.[^27] Whether you are among those 1.26 million people depends on a full year's figures; the first column has been pushed up, the second column still has to wait until year-end.

On that September 24, the minimum wage moved up once more; the monthly salary stood above NT$30,000 for the first time. The committee decides the first column for all of Taiwan. That Ms. Li, a white-collar worker six years into her career, earned NT$28,000 six years ago — already below the floor; come January next year, the floor moves up another notch. If the company follows with an adjustment, her base-salary column returns to the line. The year-end column — the law has no say; it depends on the boss, and the boss looks at whether the company made money that year. The first column is pushed up by twenty-one people and a law; the second column is decided by the company. She put it clearly: the company should follow with a raise, otherwise she falls into the minimum-wage cohort.[^1] Come January next year, the floor of her base-salary column will move once more; the year-end column, she'll have to wait until before the Lunar New Year to know.

Who counts as low-wage? The answer is in the second column of the pay slip. Today a portion of takoyaki costs NT$70, plus a bubble tea — still just one hour of her wage.

- [Air Conditioning & Energy Poverty](/en/society/energy-poverty-and-cooling) — Another measure of poverty: convert income into electricity bills — can you afford to run the AC in summer?
- [Learning Poverty](/en/society/learning-poverty-in-taiwan) — Also a definitional question: children sitting in classrooms yet learning nothing — how are they counted?
- [Taiwan's Slash Generation](/en/society/taiwan-slash-generation-multi-job-economy) — The side gigs that people with insufficient base salaries pick up after work mostly fall outside the measures discussed in this article
