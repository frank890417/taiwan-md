> **30-Second Overview:**
> The Snow Mountain Tunnel is approximately 12.9 kilometers long, making it Taiwan's longest highway tunnel and one of the world's largest double-bore tunnel complexes, comprising 58 tunnels of various sizes [^10]. Construction began in 1991, taking 15 years and costing about NT$18.5 billion to complete. This excavation history was fraught with fault water surges and collapses; international experts even labeled it an "impossible mission." Ultimately, the Taiwanese engineering team successfully opened the tunnel in 2006 through sheer willpower and innovative construction methods, reducing the travel time from Taipei to Yilan from 2 hours to about 40 minutes.

## December 15, 1997: The Moment That Made Everyone Hold Their Breath

At 8 AM on December 15, 1997, deep within the western line of the Snow Mountain Tunnel, the earth emitted a muffled grinding sound. The full-face tunnel boring machine (TBM), valued at one billion NTD, was trapped in the fractured zone of the Jinying Fault, with water continuously seeping from above [^1].

"Water kept surging in from all directions, and the cracks in the construction segments were getting worse; there were two or three hundred people rescuing people on site," recalled Leng Yao-tung (冷耀東), a Malaysian Chinese expatriate who was then an assistant chief of mechanical and electrical engineering, still showing traces of fear when recounting the disaster [^1]. At that time, high-pressure water mixed with sediment posed a collapse risk to the tunnel structure. Around noon, Leng Yao-tung sensed something was wrong and ordered everyone to evacuate.

In the chaos of the evacuation, he suddenly realized that the 22.8 KVA high-voltage power supply for the TBM had not been shut down. If water contacted the electricity, all more than three hundred people on site would perish. He risked running back into the control room to cut the power. Just as he turned and sprinted away, a deafening collapse echoed from behind. The "mining marvel" that had been highly anticipated was thus buried in the flow of ancient groundwater, becoming one of the most tragic costs in Snow Tunnel history [^1].

This disaster led to the scrapping of the TBM, putting immense pressure on the engineering team, who were forced to switch to traditional **Drill-and-Blast (NATM)** methods and expand working faces (such as the second vertical shaft) to salvage the progress [^12]. This moment encapsulated the core tension of the 15-year construction history of the Snow Mountain Tunnel: geology, technology, willpower, and luck were all pulling at the project's progress simultaneously.

## Pushing Geological Limits: Harder Than Steel Quartzite and Eternal Water Surges

Why was the Snow Mountain Tunnel so difficult to build? The answer lies in the unique and harsh geological structure of the Snow Mountains.

The Snow Tunnel had to pass through 6 major faults, 98 shear zones, and 36 water surge zones [^2]. Among these, the most terrifying for engineers was the "Four-Ridge Quartzite," known as having "steel within rock." This geology boasted a strength of up to 1200–2785 kg/cm², with quartz content reaching 82% and a Mohs hardness between 6 and 7—far exceeding the 5.5 of ordinary rebar [^13]. The section of Four-Ridge Quartzite, spanning about 3,671 meters, caused the TBM, which was originally expected to advance quickly, to move extremely slowly, averaging only over 1 meter per day, and it was even stuck for as long as 885 days [^14].

Even scarier were the "water surges." The water surge at the western entrance of the Snow Tunnel was deep "ancient groundwater" accumulated over eight thousand to twenty thousand years [^3]. Once a water vein was breached, high-pressure water mixed with sediment erupted like a flood. The highest recorded flow rate reached 750 liters per second; this scale of eruption was enough to instantly flood and destroy any machinery [^14].

To cope with these challenges, NT$100 million was spent on preliminary geological surveys before construction, and another NT$87.5 million was invested in supplementary investigations during construction—far exceeding the norm for domestic engineering at the time [^15].

## Engineering Response and Innovative Methods: The Resilience of the Taiwanese People

Faced with international experts being stumped, and even some leaving with comments like "if it can't be solved, we will commit seppuku" [^16], the Taiwanese engineering team demonstrated astonishing resilience and innovation.

They invented five major methods to deal with the harsh geology [^17], including:

1. **Three Sets of Vertical Shaft Expansions:** Three vertical shafts (two main shafts and one gallery shaft) were added in the middle section of the tunnel, drilled vertically from above to the tunnel depth, creating multiple working faces that operated simultaneously, significantly shortening the construction period [^10].
2. **Grouting Water Sealing Technology:** For high-pressure water surges, high-pressure grouting technology was adopted, injecting cement slurry or chemical solutions into rock fissures to form a water barrier and effectively control the seepage [^18].
3. **Pre-deformation Method:** The surrounding rock mass was pre-reinforced before excavation to reduce deformation and collapse after digging.
4. **Geological Pre-investigation:** Geological drilling and seismic methods were used to advance knowledge of the geology ahead, allowing for early adjustment of construction strategies.
5. **Combined TBM and Drill-and-Blast Methods:** In sections of hard Four-Ridge Quartzite where TBM advancement was difficult, traditional drill-and-blast methods were decisively adopted; the TBM was reactivated in more stable strata to flexibly respond to geological changes [^12].

These innovative methods and flexible strategies ultimately allowed the Snow Tunnel project to overcome numerous difficulties.

## Key Figures and Turning Points: From "Impossible" to "Full Line Connection"

The construction of the Snow Tunnel spanned multiple administrations and presidents, with each one bearing the immense pressure of achieving completion. This project tested technology, political commitment, and public patience simultaneously.

On September 16, 2004, President Chen Shui-bian presided over the full line connection ceremony of the Snow Mountain Tunnel. In his speech, he lamented: "The difficulty of the Snow Mountain Tunnel has been listed in _Encyclopædia Britannica_; it is an asset for all the people." [^4] At the time, Director Zheng Wen-lung (鄭文隆) of the National Science Council revealed a small secret before the connection ceremony: to ensure the successful detonation on the day, engineers had already "gotten busy" the day before to ensure the final mile was presented perfectly in front of the President and Vice President [^5].

However, completion did not mean the end. The subsequent improvements to the mechanical, electrical, and traffic control systems took nearly two more years. At that time, Minister of Transportation Lin Ling-san (林陵三) faced extreme political pressure due to repeated delays in inspections, even being mocked by legislators for being "too realistic toward Vice President Chang Ching-kuo" [^6]. But for the engineers, safety was the only metric; they insisted on opening the tunnel only after ensuring absolute safety.

## The Names of 25 Martyrs: The Monument at Shiding Service Area

When we today air-condition and listen to the radio inside the tunnel, crossing the Snow Mountains in just about 40 minutes, few people notice the "North Yilan Highway Engineering Martyr Memorial" standing in a corner of the Shiding Service Area on National Freeway No. 5.

This monument commemorates the 25 workers who tragically died during the construction of the North Yilan Highway (including the Snow Mountain Tunnel). These included 12 local engineers and 13 Thai laborers [^11]. Although there were 25 martyrs in the entire North Yilan Highway project, about 13 of them died directly in the Snow Mountain Tunnel construction [^19]. Among them, some were buried under rock due to collapse, some were swept away by high-pressure water surges, and others died from overwork caused by poor air quality and high temperatures inside the tunnel.

The monument is made from the hardest material in the Snow Mountain Tunnel—the "Four-Ridge Quartzite"—symbolizing the arduousness and challenges of the project. Its design mimics the emblem of the National Science Council and [Guishan Island] (/en/geography/gueishan-island/), and all the names of the martyrs and engineering records are engraved on the stele [^15]. These 25 lives are the heaviest footnote after the Snow Tunnel was completed. The completion of this tunnel relied not only on engineering technology but also on the risks undertaken by countless engineers in the dark underground.

## Yilan After Opening: The Cost and Harvest of a Third Revolution

The Snow Tunnel is referred to as the "Third Revolution" in the history of Yilan development [^1].

- **First Revolution:** Wu Sha settled in Lan 230 years ago, beginning the pioneering history of the Yilan plain.
- **Second Revolution:** The opening of the Taipei-Yilan railway nearly a century ago greatly improved transportation and material circulation.
- **Third Revolution:** The Snow Mountain Tunnel opened on June 16, 2006, officially integrating Yilan into the "one-hour commuting circle" of Taipei [^7].

After the opening, tourism and accommodation industries in Yilan grew significantly, and housing prices soared, but this also brought severe holiday traffic congestion and social impacts such as agricultural land fragmentation. Records from the Yilan Ama Story Museum mention that the tunnel made the people of Yilan "both look forward to and fear harm" [^12].

## Today's Snow Tunnel: Safety, Technology, and Challenges

The Snow Mountain Tunnel was designed with world-class safety standards, including a double-bore directional design to effectively prevent head-on collisions, and advanced mechanical, electrical, and disaster prevention systems [^8] [^9]. Its ventilation system is also a world first—an enhanced longitudinal flow type, equipped with 36 axial main fans and 112 jet fans, ensuring air quality and smoke extraction efficiency inside the tunnel [^10].

However, even after opening, the Snow Tunnel faces challenges. Holiday traffic management remains the biggest pain point, especially during Lunar New Year and consecutive holidays, when northbound traffic often increases by 10-24%, leading to severe congestion [^20]. The Highway Bureau has installed a slow vehicle warning system for this purpose and reminds users through real-time images and broadcasts [^20]. Although the tunnel's inherent safety design is excellent, the "road captain" phenomenon remains a key factor affecting traffic efficiency.

## Conclusion: A Flowing River of History

The Snow Mountain Tunnel is a hole made of steel and concrete, but it is also a flowing history of engineering. With limited resources and international experts giving up, Taiwanese engineers completed this "impossible mission" through unyielding will and innovative thinking.

As Leng Yao-tung said, he once dreamed that the TBM fell into a black hole, and his soul was already bound to the tunnel [^1]. This tunnel carries the most brilliant yet painful memories of Taiwan's engineering community. The next time you drive into the tunnel and see the mile markers flashing on the wall, perhaps you can recall that afternoon in 1997, the figure risking everything to shut down the power in the darkness, and those nameless heroes battling the eternal water surges in the mountainside. Their story is the landscape we must remember when passing through the Snow Tunnel.

When traveling through the Snow Tunnel, please slow down and drive carefully; this is a responsibility to one's own safety and a respect for the blood, sweat, and sacrifice of the 25 martyrs and countless engineers.
