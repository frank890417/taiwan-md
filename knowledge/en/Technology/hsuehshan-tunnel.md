> **30-Second Overview:**
> The Hsuehshan Tunnel spans approximately 12.9 km, making it Taiwan's longest highway tunnel and one of the world's largest twin-bore highway tunnel complexes, comprising 58 tunnels of various sizes [^10]. Construction began in 1991, taking 15 years and costing roughly NT$18.5 billion to complete. This excavation history is filled with fault-zone water inrushes and cave-in disasters, even prompting international experts to label it an "impossible mission." Ultimately, Taiwan's engineering teams, through sheer will and innovative methods, achieved opening in 2006, reducing the Taipei–Yilan drive from 2 hours to about 40 minutes.

## 15 December 1997: The Moment That Nearly Stopped Breath

At 8:00 a.m. on 15 December 1997, deep within the westbound bore of the Hsuehshan Tunnel, the earth emitted a dull, crushing groan. A full-face tunnel boring machine (TBM) worth NT$1 billion was trapped in the shattered zone of the Jinying Fault, with water continuously seeping from above [^1].

"Water kept gushing in from all directions, the segmental lining cracks worsening by the minute; there were two or three hundred people on site attempting rescue," recalled Leng Yao-tung (冷耀東), a Burmese-Chinese overseas compatriot who served as deputy chief of mechanical and electrical engineering at the time; his voice still carries a trace of lingering shock [^1]. At that moment, high-pressure water laden with silt threatened imminent structural collapse of the tunnel. Around noon, Leng sensed something was wrong and ordered everyone to evacuate.

Amid the chaotic evacuation, he suddenly realized the TBM's 22.8 kVA high-voltage power supply had not been shut off; once water contacted electricity, none of the three hundred-plus people on site would survive. He risked his life dashing back to the main control room to cut the power, and at the very moment he turned and sprinted toward the exit, a thunderous collapse roared behind him. That once highly anticipated "excavation god" was thus entombed in the surge of ten-thousand-year-old groundwater, becoming one of the most grievous costs in Hsuehshan Tunnel history [^1].

This disaster wrote off the TBM, placing the engineering team under immense pressure and forcing a switch to the traditional **New Austrian Tunnelling Method (NATM)** along with the opening of additional working faces (such as the No. 2 Vertical Shaft) to turn progress around [^12]. This moment encapsulates the core tension running through the tunnel's 15-year construction saga: geology, technology, willpower, and luck all tugging simultaneously at the schedule.

## Pushing Geological Limits: Quadrilateral Sandstone Harder Than Steel and Ten-Thousand-Year-Old Groundwater

Why was the Hsuehshan Tunnel so difficult to build? The answer lies in the unique and hostile geological structure of the Hsuehshan Range.

The tunnel had to traverse 6 major faults, 98 shear/fracture zones, and 36 water-inrush zones [^2]. Most dreaded by engineers was the "quadrilateral sandstone" (四稜砂岩), dubbed the "steel of the rock world." This formation boasts a strength of 1,200–2,785 kg/cm², 82% quartz content, and a Mohs hardness of 6–7, far exceeding ordinary rebar's 5.5 [^13]. The roughly 3,671-metre stretch of quadrilateral sandstone reduced the TBM — originally expected to advance rapidly — to a crawl averaging just over 1 metre per day, with one standstill lasting as long as 885 days [^14].

Even more terrifying was the "water inrush." The groundwater at the tunnel's west portal is "paleogroundwater" accumulated over 8,000 to 20,000 years [^3]. Once a water vein was breached, high-pressure water mixed with silt would blast out like a flash flood. The highest recorded inrush reached 750 litres per second — a volume sufficient to inundate the tunnel and destroy any machinery in an instant [^14].

To confront these challenges, NT$100 million was invested in pre-construction route geological surveys, with an additional NT$87.5 million for supplementary surveys during construction — far exceeding the norm for domestic projects at the time [^15].

## Engineering Responses and Innovative Methods: The Resilience of Taiwan's People

Faced with international experts throwing up their hands — some even declaring they would "commit seppuku if they couldn't solve it" before quietly departing [^16] — Taiwan's engineering teams displayed remarkable resilience and innovation.

They developed five major proprietary methods [^17] for tackling the adverse geology, including:

1.  **Three Additional Vertical Shafts to Multiply Working Faces**: Three shafts (two main shafts, one pilot-tunnel shaft) were sunk midway along the main tunnel alignment, vertically excavated down to tunnel depth to create multiple simultaneous work faces, drastically shortening the schedule [^10].
2.  **High-Pressure Grouting for Water Control**: Targeting high-pressure inrushes, high-pressure cement or chemical grout was injected into rock fissures to form a water-stop curtain, effectively controlling groundwater [^18].
3.  **Pre-Deformation Method**: Surrounding rock was reinforced before excavation to reduce post-excavation deformation and collapse.
4.  **Advanced Geological Forecasting**: Geological drilling, seismic surveying, and other techniques were used to probe conditions ahead, allowing early adjustment of construction strategy.
5.  **Combined TBM and Drill-and-Blast**: In the hard quadrilateral sandstone where TBM advance stalled, the team decisively switched to conventional drill-and-blast, reactivating TBMs only in more stable strata, flexibly adapting to geological changes [^12].

These innovative methods and flexible strategies ultimately enabled the Hsuehshan Tunnel project to overcome one obstacle after another.

## Key Figures and Turning Points: From "Impossible" to "Full Breakthrough"

Construction of the Hsuehshan Tunnel spanned multiple administrations and premiers, each bearing the immense pressure of delivering an opening. The project tested technical capability, political commitment, and public patience simultaneously.

On 16 September 2004, President Chen Shui-bian presided over the full-breakthrough ceremony. In his remarks he marvelled: "The engineering difficulty of the Hsuehshan Tunnel has been recorded in the _Encyclopædia Britannica_; this is an asset for all our people." [^4] Then-Director-General of the National Freeway Bureau Cheng Wen-lung (鄭文隆) later revealed a behind-the-scenes detail: to ensure the ceremonial blast went smoothly on the day, engineers had "tinkered" the day before, guaranteeing the final stretch would be presented flawlessly before the President and Vice President [^5].

Breakthrough, however, did not mean the end. Subsequent electromechanical and traffic-control system improvements consumed nearly two more years. Then-Minister of Transportation and Communications Lin Ling-san (林陵三) faced intense political pressure over repeated delays in inspection progress, even being taunted by legislators for being "too realistic toward Premier Frank Hsieh" [^6]. But for the engineering staff, safety was the sole benchmark; they insisted on opening only after absolute safety assurance.

## The 25 Names on the Memorial: Shiding Service Area's Monument

Today, as we cruise through the tunnel in air-conditioned comfort, listening to broadcasts and crossing the Hsuehshan Range in about 40 minutes, few notice a corner of the Shiding Service Area on National Freeway No. 5 where the "North Yilan Freeway Construction Fallen Personnel Memorial" stands.

This monument commemorates the 25 workers who perished during construction of the North Yilan Freeway (including the Hsuehshan Tunnel). They include 12 ROC-national engineers and 13 Thai migrant workers [^11]. Although the entire North Yilan Freeway project claimed 25 lives, approximately 13 died directly in the Hsuehshan Tunnel works [^19]. Some were buried by cave-ins, some swept away by high-pressure water inrushes, others succumbed to overwork in the tunnel's foul air and extreme heat.

The monument is built from the tunnel's hardest "quadrilateral sandstone," symbolising the project's severity and challenge; its form echoes the National Freeway Bureau emblem and **Guishan Island** (龜山島) [^15]. The inscription bears every fallen worker's name and a project summary. These 25 lives are the heaviest footnote to the tunnel's opening. Its completion relied on engineering technology, but equally on the countless personnel who shouldered risk in the dark mountain belly.

## Post-Opening Yilan: The Price and Gain of the Third Revolution

The Hsuehshan Tunnel is called the "third revolution" in Yilan's development history [^1].

- **First Revolution**: 230 years ago, Wu Sha (吳沙) entered Lanyang to reclaim land, opening the pioneering history of the Lanyang Plain.
- **Second Revolution**: Nearly a century ago, the Taipei–Yilan railway opened, vastly improving transport and goods flow.
- **Third Revolution**: On 16 June 2006 the Hsuehshan Tunnel opened, formally bringing Yilan into the "Taipei one-hour commuting circle" [^7].

After opening, Yilan's tourism and lodging industries grew dramatically, and housing prices soared, but severe holiday congestion and farmland fragmentation also emerged. Records from the Yilan Ama Story House note the tunnel made Yilan people "both hopeful and afraid of being hurt" [^12].

## Today's Hsuehshan Tunnel: Safety, Technology, and Challenges

The Hsuehshan Tunnel was designed to world-class safety standards, including twin-bore unidirectional design effectively eliminating head-on collisions, and advanced electromechanical and disaster-prevention systems [^8] [^9]. Its ventilation system is a world-first enhanced longitudinal-flow design, equipped with 36 axial main fans and 112 jet fans, ensuring air quality and smoke-extraction efficiency [^10].

Yet, since opening, the tunnel still faces challenges. Holiday traffic management remains the biggest pain point, especially during Lunar New Year and consecutive holidays, when northbound traffic often increases 10–24%, causing severe congestion [^20]. The Freeway Bureau has installed slow-vehicle warning systems and uses real-time video and broadcasts to alert road users [^20]. Despite the tunnel's excellent safety design, the "pace-car convoy" phenomenon remains a key factor affecting throughput efficiency.

## Epilogue: A Flowing River of History

The Hsuehshan Tunnel is a reinforced-concrete cavity, and also a flowing chapter of engineering history. With limited resources and international experts giving up, Taiwan's engineers, through indomitable will and innovative thinking, accomplished this "impossible mission."

As Leng Yao-tung said, he once dreamed the TBM plunged into a black hole; his soul has long been bound to this tunnel [^1]. This tunnel carries the most glorious and most painful memories of Taiwan's engineering community. Next time you drive into the tunnel, watching the kilometre markers flash by on the walls, perhaps you can recall that 1997 afternoon, the figure who risked the darkness to cut the power, and those unsung heroes who battled ten-thousand-year-old groundwater in the mountain's belly. Their story is the most important scenery to remember when travelling through the Hsuehshan Tunnel.

When passing through the Hsuehshan Tunnel, please slow down and obey traffic rules; this is responsibility for your own safety, and respect for the blood, sweat, and dedication of the 25 fallen and countless engineering personnel.
