# Open Source Community and g0v

> **30-second overview:** In 2012, because the government spent a fortune on a content-free advertisement, a group of engineers decided to "fork the government"—changing gov.tw to g0v.tw and redesigning what government should look like. Eight years later, when COVID-19 broke out, these "amateur" programmers built a mask map in 72 hours that let everyone in Taiwan check real-time mask inventory at 13,000 pharmacies. This wasn't a government achievement; it was a citizens' victory.

On a night in October 2012, CL Kao (高嘉良) sat at his computer watching the government's "Economic Dynamism Promotion Plan" advertisement, growing angrier by the minute. The government had spent over NT$40 million producing a promotional video, but the content was so vacuous it made one wonder: would throwing that money into a ditch have been more meaningful?

That night, he made a decision that would change Taiwan: **If the government won't do it right, we'll do it ourselves.**

He took the government's domain name gov.tw and changed the "o" to a "0," creating g0v.tw[^1]. This simple wordplay symbolized a brand-new concept: **fork the government**. Just like open source software—if the original version has problems, you fork a branch and rewrite a better version yourself.

## Forking the Government: A Digital-Era Civic Experiment

g0v isn't about overthrowing the government; it's about running a "parallel" government—using open-source collaboration to reimagine what government services should look like.

**December 2012** saw the zeroth g0v hackathon held at Academia Sinica, with 40-plus participants. The first project was rescuing the _Central Government General Budget_ from PDF hell and turning it into an interactive visualization website.

The government's original budget document was a 500-plus-page PDF, dense with numbers and tables that ordinary people simply couldn't understand. g0v volunteers reorganized the data and built interactive charts—**one click and you could see exactly where the government spent your tax money.**

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> g0v's first project choosing budget visualization was no coincidence. The budget is the core of democratic politics—what the government does with the people's money, the people have a right to know. But traditional budget documents are practically designed to be incomprehensible. g0v used technology to crack this "intentional opacity."

This small experiment proved one thing: **The government not doing something doesn't mean it can't be done. It just means no one had done it yet.**

## The Sunflower Movement: A Showcase of Civic Tech Firepower

On the evening of March 18, 2014, students occupied the Legislative Yuan. The next morning, g0v volunteers appeared on site—not to protest, but to "build infrastructure."

**No one organized it, no one directed it.** g0v community participants spontaneously did the following:

- **Live streaming**: Set up a multi-camera live broadcast system so the whole world could see what was happening inside the chamber
- **Information integration**: Created hackfoldr folders[^2] to collect, organize, and verify all kinds of information from the internet in real time
- **Crowdsourced collaboration**: Built a collaborative editing system so citizens not on site could participate in data gathering and fact-checking
- **International outreach**: Provided real-time multilingual translation so international media could instantly understand the protesters' demands

**24 days of occupation, full HD live streaming throughout, zero downtime.** In 2014 this was an inconceivable technical achievement. Facebook Live didn't exist yet, YouTube streaming wasn't widespread, but g0v volunteers used open-source tools to build a streaming system more stable than professional media outlets.

More importantly, they proved the power of "transparency." Because of the live stream, everyone could see what happened inside the chamber, making it impossible for the government to distort facts. This model of "using technology to monitor government" was later seen abroad as a viable template, with similar civic tech communities emerging in South Korea, Thailand, Japan, and Germany[^3].

## The Mask Map: A 72-Hour Miracle

In early February 2020, COVID-19 erupted in Taiwan. The government announced a mask rationing policy: each person could buy two masks per week. The problem: where to buy? Which pharmacy still had stock?

**February 6**, Digital Minister Audrey Tang (herself a g0v founding member) announced: the government would open mask inventory data for all 13,000 contracted pharmacies nationwide, updated every 30 minutes[^4].

**February 8**, the first mask map went live.
**February 9**, over 100 different versions of mask maps appeared.

This wasn't a government-contracted IT project; it was the result of programmers across Taiwan "voluntarily working overtime." **Everyone wanted to contribute to epidemic prevention, and what programmers could do was write code.**

The most popular versions included:

- **Taiwan Mask Map** by Howard Wu: clean, intuitive map interface
- **Still Have Masks?** by user kiang: integrated pharmacy reviews and business info
- **Where to Buy Masks** by Finjon Kiang: supported voice query function

Within 72 hours, Taiwan had the world's most comprehensive mask inventory query system[^5]. **While people in other countries were still lining up to panic-buy, Taiwanese could already check on their phones how many masks remained at the nearest pharmacy.**

> **⚠️ Contested Viewpoint**
> Some criticized the government for "outsourcing responsibility to volunteers," letting civilians build systems for the government for free. But the g0v community's response was direct: we're not being used by the government; we're actively choosing to give back to society with our expertise. Moreover, the open-source mask maps were more usable, more innovative, and better met user needs than anything the government built itself.

## The Magic of Open-Source Collaboration

g0v's operating model is simple: **no bosses, no employees, no budget, no office.** Just a group of people willing to use technology to solve social problems, plus an open-source collaboration culture.

### Hackathon Culture

A major hackathon ("大松") is held every two months, where participants propose, form teams, and implement on the spot. The process:

1. **Three-minute pitch**: Anyone can take the stage to propose an idea
2. **Free team formation**: Interested people can join projects
3. **On-site implementation**: Start building right there that day
4. **Results sharing**: Present progress in the afternoon

**No one gets rejected, no idea gets shot down.** The only requirement: the project must be open source so others can continue improving it.

### Collaboration Tools

- **Slack channels**: Daily discussion and info sharing
- **GitHub**: Code management and version control[^6]
- **HackMD**: Collaborative documents and meeting notes
- **Trello**: Project management and progress tracking

### Three Core Principles

1. **Open source**: All project code, data, and documentation are public
2. **Decentralized**: No leadership hierarchy; anyone can initiate a project
3. **Implementation first**: "Talk is cheap, show me the code"

> **💡 Did You Know**
> g0v has a tradition: every hackathon prepares "little squirrel" stickers. If it's your first time attending, you get a little squirrel sticker. The design implies: even if you're a newcomer, you're welcome to contribute a little bit of effort, like squirrels gathering acorns—every tiny contribution matters.

## Key Projects and Social Impact

Over eight years, the g0v community has produced hundreds of projects, many directly influencing government policy and social operations.

### Legislative Yuan Transparency

**Legislative Yuan transcripts** used to be text-only, making it hard for ordinary citizens to understand what legislators actually did in the chamber. g0v volunteers built the "Legislative Yuan Transparency" platform, providing:

- **Live streaming**: Live broadcast of Legislative Yuan sessions
- **Speech records**: Speech statistics and content search for each legislator
- **Voting records**: Voting results on important bills
- **Bill tracking**: Complete process from proposal to third reading

The result: **Legislators started caring about their "data."** Attendance rates, interpellation counts, proposal numbers—figures no one used to care about—now had websites automatically tracking them. Elected representatives discovered their every move was being monitored, and behavior began to change.

### vTaiwan: A Digital Democracy Experiment

In 2014, g0v and the government jointly launched the vTaiwan platform[^7], letting citizens participate in the policy-making process. The most famous case was the Uber controversy:

**2015**, Uber's operations in Taiwan sparked taxi driver protests. The traditional approach was unilateral government decision-making, but vTaiwan offered a third path: letting all stakeholders dialogue on an online platform to find a win-win solution.

After months of online discussion and physical workshops, the "Diverse Taxi" policy emerged, protecting traditional taxi drivers' rights while allowing innovative service models to exist.

**This was Taiwan's first use of "digital democracy" to resolve a policy dispute.**

### Driving Open Government

g0v's advocacy directly influenced government policy:

- **2012**: Budget visualization project pushed government to open budget data
- **2013**: Legislative transparency project prompted live streaming of Legislative Yuan sessions
- **2014**: Post-Sunflower Movement, government pledged to amend the _Government Information Disclosure Act_
- **2015**: vTaiwan platform became an official government policy participation channel
- **2016**: Audrey Tang became Digital Minister, bringing g0v experience into government

## International Impact and Connections

g0v's experience hasn't just influenced Taiwan—it has inspired civic tech movements worldwide.

### Code for All Network

g0v is a founding member of the **Code for All** international network[^8], collaborating closely with Japan's Code for Japan, South Korea's Code for Korea, the US's Code for America, and other organizations.

**2019**, g0v summit was held in Taipei, with civic tech communities from 30+ countries gathering to share experiences and technology.

### International Cooperation During the Pandemic

During the 2020 pandemic, g0v's mask map experience was adopted by other countries:

- **Italy**: Roma version mask map
- **Germany**: Berlin version mask map
- **United States**: PPE map (personal protective equipment)
- **South Korea**: 마스크맵 (mask map)

g0v volunteers also proactively assisted other countries in building similar systems, sharing Taiwan's epidemic prevention technology experience with the world.

## Challenges and Future

As an "organization without a boss," g0v faces the same challenges all open-source communities encounter.

### Project Sustainability

Many g0v projects are "spur-of-the-moment" creations lacking long-term maintenance. The mask map was extremely active during the pandemic but gradually fell into disuse afterward. **How to keep good projects running sustainably is g0v's biggest challenge.**

### Contributor Fatigue

Eight years of high-intensity volunteer participation has left some early contributors exhausted. How to attract new blood and make participation more sustainable are issues the community must face.

### Government Relations

g0v's relationship with the government is delicate: both cooperative and supervisory. When the government actively embraces open source and digital democracy, g0v's "adversarial" role becomes blurred. How to maintain independence and critical spirit while cooperating is an ongoing challenge.

### Disinformation and Information Warfare

In the era of information warfare, the principle of openness and transparency can also be abused. How to avoid becoming a conduit for disinformation while staying open is a new challenge.

## An Experiment Still in Progress

In 2012, when CL Kao changed gov.tw to g0v.tw, he just wanted to express dissatisfaction with the government. Twelve years later, g0v has become part of Taiwan's democracy and a major force in the global civic tech movement.

This experiment has proven several things:

1. **Technology can be a tool for civic participation**, not just a means for commercial profit
2. **Openness and transparency matter more than government efficiency**, because transparency brings efficiency
3. **Small anger can change the world**, if you're willing to roll up your sleeves and do it
4. **Forking the government isn't overthrowing it**—it's proving better possibilities exist

In this era of democratic backsliding, g0v reminds us: **Citizens aren't users of government; they're co-creators.** What the government does poorly, we can do ourselves. What the government does well, we can help do better.

This isn't a finished revolution; it's a continuing experiment. Every hackathon, every new project, every line of code answers the same question: In the digital age, what can democracy look like?

The answer is still being written, and every person willing to contribute is an author of that answer.
