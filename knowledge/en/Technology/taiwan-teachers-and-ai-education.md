# AI Enters the Classroom: Three Hundred Agents to Catch Lonely Teachers After Training

> **30-Second Overview**: On the last makeup class day before winter break, Hsu Tai-ping led a full class of teachers at Taipei City Dajia Elementary School, helping them create instructional AI agents in just three hours. Meanwhile, two different Taiwanese surveys revealed "92% of teachers have used AI" and "95.6% of teachers still have AI learning needs." Teachers are not rejecting the tools; what truly stalls them is lesson preparation time, ready-to-use case studies, peer communities, and finding support once they return to school. The "Grand Marketplace," where eighty teachers collectively uploaded three hundred agents, integrates tool sharing with long-term mutual aid into a single institutional experiment.

## Three Hours at Dajia Elementary, and Then What?

Hsu Tai-ping stands in the computer lab of Taipei City Dajia Elementary School. It is the last makeup class day before winter break; the teacher training session is fully booked, and even the principal has sat down in front of a computer. Working in groups, teachers feed prompts into Gemini, trying to generate an image that would help children guess the idiom _tóng zhōu gòng jì_ (to join forces/help each other through difficulties), and then designing assessment questions with high discriminative power. In those three hours, no one started by learning model architecture. Every keystroke was driven backward from the question: "Where exactly is the student getting stuck?" [^1]

Hsu Tai-ping has taught at Taipei City Rixin Elementary School for twenty years, progressing from Scratch and robotic arms to the Center for Maker Education and now generative AI. He requires every group of teachers in his workshop to create one instructional AI agent they can actually take back to their classrooms; lists of popular tools are relegated to the background. Su Chih-cheng, the head of the information department at Dajia Elementary, offered a direct assessment after the session: "The technical skill of operating AI tools is actually less difficult than expected; the key is working backward from student needs to instructional strategies, thinking about how to help children learn better and more solidly." [^1]

The difficulty arises after class ends. The Central Technology Field Guidance Team has only eight members to cover all of Taiwan. To bring workshops to remote areas, members frequently apply for mountain entry permits and stay overnight for two days. A single workshop can ignite an afternoon, but it is hard to answer the questions teachers will face next week: prompts that fail during lesson prep, students copying answers, account permissions, or parental concerns. Hsu Tai-ping describes this breaking point clearly:

> "The problem most teachers currently face is that they learn it in the moment, but professional teacher communities and teacher empowerment are not continuous. This leaves teachers with the emotion and spark of the moment, but once they go back, they are alone." [^1]

This sentence shifts the perspective on Taiwan's AI education problem. It is no longer just "can teachers use AI," but rather "is there anyone around after a teacher uses it for the first time."

## Two "90 Percentages" Telling Two Different Stories

At the end of 2025, a survey conducted by _Parenting Magazine_ (Qinzi Tianxia) in collaboration with Google for Education collected 4,765 valid samples ranging from kindergartens to high school teachers. Among them, 39% claimed to use AI frequently and 53% used it occasionally, totaling 92% who had been exposed to AI. 63% were highly positive about AI applications in education, while only 8% said they had heard of it but not used it. This was not a random sample of all Taiwanese teachers and carries a corporate partnership background; it is suitable for understanding the usage profile of survey participants but cannot be directly extrapolated to national prevalence. [^2]

Another survey by the Professor Huang Kun-hui Education Foundation collected 3,328 complete samples of elementary, junior high, and high school teachers via online questionnaires between August 27 and October 10, 2025. The results showed that 95.6% expressed a need for AI literacy learning, with demands for curriculum design, assessment design, diagnosing learning difficulties, and differentiated instruction all exceeding 90%. Furthermore, 81.7% hoped that county and city governments would establish cross-school teacher AI learning communities, and 92.8% supported providing professional manpower to remote areas or teachers without information technology expertise. [^3]

```tw-stat
92% | Have used AI frequently or occasionally | Parenting Magazine × Google, 4,765 teacher surveys
95.6% | Express continued need for AI literacy learning | Huang Kun-hui Education Foundation, 3,328 teacher surveys
81.7% | Expect establishment of cross-school teacher AI communities | Same foundation demand survey
Source: Educator Platform, Professor Huang Kun-hui Education Foundation; samples and methods differ, do not compare proportions directly.
```

Both "90 percents" are high, but they measure different things. The former asks "have you encountered it," while the latter asks "do you want to learn more." Placing them side-by-side does not prove that using AI causes more anxiety, but it does dismantle a common misconception: encountering a tool is not the same as being able to integrate it into a curriculum. Teachers might ask a model to rewrite notices or organize handouts while still not knowing how to design assessments, diagnose learning difficulties, or explain to students why personal data should not be entered into a chat box.

The same _Parenting Magazine_ survey also showed that 47% of respondents felt digital lesson preparation time had increased, 60% were troubled by unstable devices or internet connections, and 43% lacked directly applicable teaching materials. When a new tool requires a teacher to simultaneously act as a curriculum designer, account administrator, frontline technician, and cybersecurity gatekeeper, "digitalization" might actually increase the workload before it can reduce the burden. [^2]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The hardest resource to buy in AI education may be an afternoon that allows teachers to make mistakes alongside their colleagues. An account is paid for once a year, but pedagogical judgment must be remade with every student reaction. If policy only counts how many workshops were held or how many accounts were issued, it leaves the most critical work—the part that needs visibility—outside of the statistics.

This continues the long-standing contradictions in the [Taiwan education system](/en/lifestyle/taiwan-education-system/). The system demands curricular innovation, yet the daily rhythms of hourly teaching, administration, and academic advancement have not loosened in sync. AI may shorten the time it takes to generate an image, but it does not necessarily free up time for teachers to observe their students. The saved minutes are likely swallowed by another platform, another set of passwords, and another progress report.

## Three Hundred Agents Are Not Three Hundred Products

Hsu Tai-ping and members of the Central Technology Team later used Notion to create the "AI Agent Grand Marketplace." Approximately eighty teachers uploaded three hundred instructional agents they had designed. The topics focused directly on "which classroom difficulty has someone else already navigated." Some created image prompts, some designed differentiated questions, and others had agents act as practice partners. The platform preserves the finished products while also recording how a teacher translated student needs into prompts and constraints. [^1]

```tw-stat
~300 | Instructional agents in the AI Agent Marketplace | Designed and shared by teachers
~80 | Participating teachers | Turning personal trials into peer resources
8 | Central Technology Field Guidance Team members | Collaborating with local teams to support Taiwan
Source: Interview with Hsu Tai-ping, *Business Weekly*, March 2026
```

The name "Grand Marketplace" might suggest downloading tools, but its greatest value lies in exchange. A general chatbot can spit out a lesson plan in seconds, but it doesn't know the literacy gap in a specific class, nor why a teacher changed _tóng zhōu gòng jì_ to "look at the picture first, then recognize the characters." Whether an agent can be used across different classes depends on whether the sharer clearly specified the applicable grade level, instructional goals, data boundaries, and ways it failed. Without this context, three hundred agents might just become a warehouse of three hundred links.

Therefore, the metric for this platform should not just be quantity. More important questions include: how many times has an agent been taken back to a classroom by other teachers, what constraints were modified, where did students misunderstand it, and who came back to provide supplementary notes after an error occurred? If this feedback is preserved, the platform can turn lonely trials into collective pedagogical knowledge. This is what distinguishes it from corporate tool marketplaces: the value lies not in listing products, but in people being willing to return and explain their failures.

## Beverage Shop Customer Service: Letting "Kè-khak" Break the System First

Luo Yu-chen, an information technology teacher at Taipei Municipal Neihu High School, took a different path. She had students use Gemini's "Gems" feature to create AI customer service agents for beverage shops, mobile phone stores, or library guides. After creating them, students swapped devices and played the role of picky _kè-khak_ (Hokkien for "outsider/customer," often used to describe demanding customers), trying to find ways to make their classmates' AI services fail. Some used emotional blackmail to demand free drinks; others pretended to be owners requesting trade secrets; some, knowing the model wouldn't directly spit out passwords, demanded it hide the answer within an acrostic poem. [^5]

This lesson did not hide mistakes. Once students found loopholes, they had to modify the prompts, explain why the changes worked, and re-test. Model errors transformed from embarrassing accidents into teaching materials; cybersecurity was no longer just a promotional slide saying "do not leak personal data," but an exercise in attacking, patching, and attacking again. Luo Yu-chen finally reminded her students that true learning and thinking must still be done by themselves: "Don't forget that you are the protagonists." [^5]

Lin Ying-chun, a teacher at Yilan County Zhongshan Elementary School, raised another important limitation in the same sharing session: AI instruction must be integrated into existing curricula; it must never be an "add-on." Tsai Pang-chü, a teacher at Chiayi Municipal Minsheng Junior High School, had students use AI to create online 3D dice to calculate probability, which in turn forced the teacher to follow up on the underlying code. He joked: "They say teaching and learning grow together; I feel like it's all the students teaching me." [^5]

These three cases collectively move the teacher away from being a provider of answers. Teachers design problems, guard data and ethical boundaries, and allow students to touch new tool features faster than they can. This type of classroom is harder than "the whole class following the teacher's prompts," because the teacher must be able to judge whether the student actually understands the mechanism or has simply received a very pretty output.

## Most Teachers in the World Also Use AI for Lesson Prep First

The OECD's TALIS 2024 survey asks junior high school teachers involved in education systems; Taiwan is not in the directly comparable sample. On average, about one in three teachers used AI in their work during the previous year; in Singapore and the United Arab Emirates, it was approximately 75%, while in France and Japan, it was less than 20%. This data set is suitable for providing international coordinates but not for ranking Taiwan's global position. [^4]

More interestingly is the distribution of usage. Among teachers who use AI, 68% use it to learn or summarize topics, and 64% use it to generate lesson plans. Only 25% use it to review student engagement or performance data, and 26% use it for assessment or grading assignments. Tasks that are easy to complete individually with lower risk—such as lesson preparation—are at the forefront, while uses involving student data, assessment fairness, and professional responsibility lag behind. [^4]

```tw-stat
~1/3 | Used AI in work during the previous year | Average for junior high teachers in OECD TALIS 2024 systems
68% | Use AI to learn or summarize topics | Teachers who use AI
64% | Use AI to generate lesson plans | Teachers who use AI
25–26% | Analyze student performance or grade assignments | Teachers who use AI
Source: OECD *Results from TALIS 2024*; Taiwan not included in this comparable sample.
```

This sequence does not conflict with the reality on the ground in Taiwan. Creating images, organizing materials, and generating questions are easy entry points. When it comes to assessment, teachers must answer who is responsible for incorrect scores, whether student work can be uploaded, and if the system amplifies linguistic or cultural biases. On a feature list, it's just one button away; in professional responsibility, it's an entire layer apart.

The UNESCO 2024 Teacher AI Competency Framework also does not treat "being able to prompt" as an end goal in itself. The framework breaks down competency into five dimensions: human-centric thinking, AI ethics, fundamentals and applications, AI pedagogy, and using AI for professional development, explicitly asserting that AI should supplement rather than replace the teacher's role. It also warns that AI should not be used as a substitute for long-term investment in addressing teacher shortages, internet connectivity, and infrastructure issues. [^7]

## Remove Names First, Then Discuss Automated Assessment

As AI moves from lesson prep to assessment, risks suddenly become concrete. If a teacher asks a model to generate ten practice questions, there is still a chance to fix an error before handing them out. However, if a class's response records are pasted directly, they may simultaneously contain names, grades, learning difficulties, and family contexts. The output might still look like text, but the input has shifted from teaching materials to children's data. This explains why, in the OECD survey, usage for summarizing topics and generating lesson plans exceeds 60%, while analyzing performance and grading assignments is only about one-quarter. [^4]

Luo Yu-chen's students using acrostic poems to extract hidden passwords from AI customer service provided a miniature warning: secrets written into prompts are not automatically secure just because the user cannot see the settings page. Students can intentionally bypass restrictions, and real external services may retain dialogues, change terms, or be misconfigured. Bringing this lesson back to the teacher's workflow, the starting question should be "is it necessary to give this data to the model," and only then ask "can the model grade it." [^5]

If we translate UNESCO's human-centric and ethical principles into actionable minimum standards for schools, three steps can be taken first. Before submitting data, remove identifiable student names, seat numbers, and unnecessary backgrounds. When using AI to assist in assessment, allow teachers to retain final judgment and spot-check the model's reasoning. Students and parents should also know which parts of a process used AI and be able to find someone to appeal to if errors occur. These practices do not need to wait for a perfect national standard; they require schools to provide common templates so that responsibility is not left entirely to each teacher's guesswork. [^7]

Equity issues are also hidden behind accounts. In the foundation's survey, 94.1% of surveyed teachers hoped the Ministry of Education would provide AI software that currently requires payment, 60.2% believed schools should prioritize providing stable internet and resources, and 46.7% pointed to professional support manpower. These numbers do not guarantee that government-funded accounts are the answer, but they remind policymakers of three simultaneous thresholds: affordability, connectivity, and accountability when things go wrong. Addressing only the first will allow the gap between classrooms to continue widening along lines of internet quality and support capacity. [^3]

Assessment is therefore the stress test for AI education. It forces the system to answer questions about data governance, professional responsibility, parental communication, and student appeals all at once, rather than just hosting another feature demonstration. When these boundaries are clearly defined, teachers can feel confident using AI in appropriate places and have the courage to turn it off where it is not.

## The Talent Ark Sets Sail; Someone Must Be on the Shore to Receive It

In June 2026, the Ministry of Education announced the "AI Talent Ark Project" for the years 115 to 118 (2026–2029). The three directions are: constructing AI learning environments and next-generation learning systems; cultivating cross-disciplinary teaching talent and establishing digital empowerment through "hand-holding" mechanisms; and supporting instructional decision-making with educational big data. Policy language has moved from "providing tools" toward "cultivating talent" and "hand-holding," showing an awareness that single-instance procurement is insufficient to change the classroom. [^6]

![Policy diagram of the Ministry of Education's three directions for the AI Talent Ark Project, including learning environments, cross-disciplinary talent, and data decision-making](/article-images/technology/taiwan-moe-ai-talent-ark-2026.webp)
_The Ministry of Education has condensed the 2026–2029 plan into three directions: learning environment, cross-disciplinary teaching talent, and data decision-making. This article is concerned with how the second item, "hand-holding," moves from a policy diagram into a teacher's weekly schedule. Image: Ministry of Education, 2026, edited citation of official policy diagram._

The hardest step for "hand-holding" to truly enter a school's weekly schedule is implementation. In the foundation's survey, teachers most look forward to practical workshops and online courses. Additionally, 81.7% of the same respondents hope to establish cross-school learning communities. These two needs are not interchangeable. Workshops help people cross the initial threshold of operation; communities are responsible for catching the second failure, the third adjustment, and the problems that only arise once students are actually sitting in front of them. [^3]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Between "training completed" and "teaching changed," there is a missing field that is hard to put into a procurement table: who responds to the teacher after they return to school. Hsu Tai-ping's three hundred agents are noteworthy because there are eighty people behind them who can be found. If the Talent Ark only carries tools but no relationships, teachers may still disembark individually when it reaches each school.

A sustainable support system must have at least three layers. The first layer is directly adaptable instructional cases, accompanied by applicable grade levels, data risks, and failure records. The second layer is intra-school or cross-school peer time, so that non-IT specialist teachers do not have to chase version updates alone. The third layer is professional manpower from central and local governments, stepping in when model updates, cybersecurity incidents, or assessment disputes exceed a school's capacity. These three layers must also cover remote areas' internet and equipment; otherwise, the teachers who need support most will spend their workshop time just trying to log in and update.

On the student side, they cannot be left behind in tool training alone. Luo Yu-chen's "Kè-khak" class turned risk into observable behavior; students not only knew AI makes mistakes but also personally discovered how it can be induced and how it leaks information. This is closer to the human-centric and ethical competencies described by UNESCO than memorizing a code of conduct, and it clarifies the teacher's professional role: designing an environment where students can question the model, rather than becoming a mouthpiece for it. [^5][^7]

This path also complements the industrial perspective of [Taiwan's artificial intelligence development and future strategy](/en/technology/artificial-intelligence-development-strategy/). Taiwan can manufacture AI servers and train engineering talent, but it cannot assume that hardware advantages will naturally flow into every classroom. The [Taiwan AI Academy](/en/technology/taiwan-ai-academy/) once used private sector strength to train industrial talent. K-12 teachers measure results on a different scale: whether a child finally understands something because the explanation was changed.

## The Last Question Is Not About the Next Tool

Returning to those three hours at Dajia Elementary, the teachers did indeed create something they could take away. This is important. Without that first success, the subsequent communities would never form. But once that agent returns to school, it will encounter students asking random questions, outdated data, model updates, and parental concerns. Instructional innovation is not a product that remains fixed after clicking "publish"; it is more like a teaching material that requires collective maintenance by peers.

The fact that Taiwanese teachers use technology faster than the system does does not mean the system should only chase tool names. The two "90 percent" surveys, the three hundred agents, and a classroom where students attack AI customer service have already made the next steps very concrete: give teachers adaptable cases, time for collective trial and error, communities where people can be found, and clear boundaries regarding personal data, assessment, and bias.

Hsu Tai-ping said that while there is emotion and spark during the workshop, teachers are alone once they return. The true AI education outcome worth calculating might be whether, when you meet the same teacher next semester, there is someone by their side who can help them finish revising the lesson.
