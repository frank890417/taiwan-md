# Threads in Taiwan: From "Hello, I'll eat a little" to an Egyptian Student ID—How a News Feed Became a Digital Night Market

On December 27, 2025, a traveler found a student ID belonging to a National Taiwan University (NTU) Department of Economics and Management student near the Great Sphinx in Egypt. He left a message on Threads: "Heading to Cairo Airport soon; can do an in-person handoff or help bring it back to Taiwan." As of the final fact-check for this article, this post seeking help had accumulated 3.8 million views, 179,000 likes, and 26,300 shares. [^1]

It didn't explain how far Egypt is from Taipei, nor did it frame itself as a charitable mission. It simply placed a card, a location, and a person about to board a flight onto the news feed. A few hours later, Taiwanese netizens began reposting, tagging, and speculating about the owner. An unspoken tacit agreement of "helping to spread the word" (幫擴), which exists in no terms of service, was summoned into being.

> **30-Second Overview:** Threads in Taiwan is more than just a text-based battlefield during elections or a marketing window for brands to jump on trends. It functions more like a digital night market that is always open: some post "nonsense" posts to test the waters, some ask strangers to help find lost items, and some comment "Hello, I'll eat a little" on food photos posted by Japanese or Korean travelers. Meta advertising tools show that 6.65 million Threads users can be reached in Taiwan, but this is not the number of active users; what truly deserves understanding is how this platform packs low-pressure chatting, local mutual aid, meme translation, and public stances into a single news feed. [^2]

## Don't Rush to Call It Number One: What Does 6.65 Million Actually Mean?

Threads is highly visible in Taiwan, yet it is often oversimplified by the phrase "the world's favorite app." In June 2026, Meta announced that Threads had reached 500 million monthly active users (MAU). Meanwhile, DataReportal estimated—based on Meta's advertising tools—that by the end of 2025, the platform could reach 6.65 million users in Taiwan, representing approximately 28.8% of Taiwan's total population and 29.8% of internet users. [^2] [^3]

These two figures cannot be directly subtracted or divided from one another, nor can they be used to create a national ranking. The former is the global MAU announced by Meta; the latter is the reachable audience within an advertising system. DataReportal specifically notes that ad reach is not the same as MAU or DAU (daily active users), and it may be affected by multiple accounts, location data, and platform data corrections. By reading these metrics correctly, one can see where the true interest in the Taiwan phenomenon lies: it isn't that "everyone has moved there," but rather that a large group of people is willing to leave short sentences and engage with strangers here, allowing content to meet before existing social connections do. [^3]

### 21% Traffic is Not 21% of Users: Four Numbers Answering Four Different Questions

The claim that "Taiwan contributes over 20% of global traffic to Threads" sounds like even stronger evidence, but it also needs to be deconstructed. In its June 2026 `threads.net` web traffic dashboard, Semrush listed Taiwan as a country-level traffic source accounting for approximately **21.24%**. This shows that Taiwan has a high share of website browsing as measured by that specific tool, but it is not the number of accounts announced by Meta, Taiwan's MAU, or App usage hours; furthermore, it cannot be used to infer that "one in every five Threads users in the world is in Taiwan." [^55] The scope of web analysis services, cookies, devices, and the ratio of App vs. web traffic all influence these results. It is circumstantial evidence for understanding usage intensity or traffic concentration, not a census.

## Why "Cui," and Not Just Another Twitter/X?

Understanding the popularity of Threads in Taiwan as "Taiwanese people abandoning X" is inaccurate. A more realistic description would be: Taiwan never had a Twitter/X that was used by the majority of people for daily activities. When _MIT Technology Review_ interviewed scholars and users in 2024, it noted that at most about 1% to 5% of Taiwanese people use Twitter/X regularly. Notable exceptions include K-pop circles, which preserve idol information, and the LGBT community, which uses it as a subcultural social space. [^5] For most people, the issue with X's real-time text logic is not a lack of appeal, but rather that it never became a mainstream lifestyle habit.

Threads entered through a different gateway. It does not require users to first understand X's culture, follow foreign journalists, or build a following within unfamiliar English or Japanese message streams. Users can create a profile using their existing Instagram or Facebook accounts, and their personal information and links are carried over. [^15] This design of "opening an extra door from a place already in use" reduces the friction of a cold start, ensuring that first-time posters do not have to find an entire new community as they would when arriving at X.

The differences between platforms are more like a division of labor rather than a competition of winners and losers: A survey by MIC, Institute for Information Industry, shows that among social media users aged 18–24, the proportion of frequent users is 78% for Instagram, 44% for Threads, and 45.9% for Dcard. Those aged 35–44 use PTT more frequently, while Facebook's usage rate peaks at 79.4% among the 45–54 age group. [^4] These figures do not mean a specific age group can only use one platform; instead, they illustrate that Taiwanese users constantly manage different relationships across different spaces: one place for photos, one for seeing acquaintances, one for asking anonymous questions, one for following real-time news, and another to say something that just popped into their head to strangers.

| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Twitter/X  | Real-time global information, subcultures, cross-linguistic communities              | Users are concentrated in specific interest and subcultural circles                    | Brings text-based public dialogue into a broader Traditional Chinese lifestyle context                 |

| Dcard, PTT | Topic discussions, experience sharing, anonymous or bulletin-board style communities | Clearer board structures and subcultural rules                                         | Uses algorithmic feeds to temporarily bring together people who wouldn't normally visit the same board |
| Threads    | Life whispers, interaction, Q&A, memes, and public discussion                        | Situated between acquaintance-based socializing and a stranger's plaza                 | Makes "I'll say something first and see who picks it up" a low-threshold habit                         |

Therefore, the key to Threads' success in Taiwan is not its feature list, but rather that the context aligns perfectly: Facebook's social relationships are too complete, Instagram's visual requirements are too high, Dcard and PTT each have their own board cultures, and X lacks a mass base. Threads allows an unedited text, a single reply, or a casual photo to reach beyond one's original circle of followers. The _MIT_ report also captured this juxtaposition: political and lifestyle posts coexist, while "nonsense posts" allow users to gain unimaginable attention through humorous personal trivialities. [^5]

However, being "easier to be seen" is not a permanent promise. It depends on algorithms, policies, account status, and whether the platform remains willing to push content in front of strangers. Choosing Threads is not like buying a plaza; it is more like temporarily renting a table in a night market set up by a private company, where the rules can be changed at any time.

## Anime, VTubers, and X: Some Communities' Home Bases Haven't Moved

If one only looks at daily topics in Taiwan, it is easy to mistakenly assume that all text-based communities have "moved to Threads." However, anime fans, derivative creators, and the VTuber community provide a different answer: **Platform migration is not an entire group moving from A to B, but rather different purposes remaining in different places.** For many who need to follow new works, find illustrators, track fan activities, participate in birthday/debut/livestream hashtags, repost official information, or connect with Japanese and English-speaking circles, X (formerly Twitter) often remains a more important public index and daily gathering point. Threads can serve as an extension for Taiwanese lifestyle posts, short-form interactions, and reaching different audiences, but it does not necessarily inherit existing follower relationships, hashtag databases, and international timelines.

This is not a conclusion that "X is better than Threads," nor does it mean anime fans and VTubers do not use Threads. The difference lies in the community workflow. Fan studies have pointed out that Twitter's public hashtags, retweets, and mention mechanisms allow enthusiasts to collaborate and accumulate reputation and information visibility. Hashtags are not just decorations; they are a set of retrieval paths maintained over the long term by both acquaintances and strangers. [^44] Work from PTS (Public Television Service) in 2024 regarding the VTuber community also recorded that VTuber-related accounts operate simultaneously on Discord and X, showing that livestreams, core communities, and public promotion are often split across different platforms. [^45]

| :--------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Following new works, finding creators and derivative works | X accounts, hashtags, retweet networks, and multilingual timelines   | Daily Taiwanese interaction, lifestyle updates, and chatting with existing Instagram audiences | Years of accumulated followers, hashtags, and work links cannot be automatically migrated                                               |

| Character and creator persona                              | Public accounts, work accounts, anonymous or semi-anonymous accounts | Low-pressure "shitposting," daily life, or local Taiwanese memes/jokes                         | Creators must manage different audiences, boundaries for derivative works, and visibility; it cannot be summarized by a single platform |

Therefore, when someone says "anime fans and VTubers still frequent Twitter more," a better way to understand it is: they are preserving a **path that can already be identified through works, hashtags, and international enthusiasts.** Especially for budding VTubers or independent illustrators, a public account is not just a promotional column, but a searchable, taggable, and referable portfolio. Moving these relationships involves more than just reinstalling an app; the cost is re-teaching the audience where to find someone, what terms to use to find events, and how to determine if an account belongs to the person themselves or a fan community.

This also makes the metaphor of Threads as a "giant open LINE group" more accurate. Threads excels at letting Taiwanese people immediately speak about their immediate lives, news, food, and small memes. For certain subculture communities, X is more like an international corridor, allowing the same hashtag to continuously link works and events together. The two can overlap without necessarily replacing one another. Treating the home bases of different communities as a competitive ranking actually obscures the fact that creators are constantly performing multi-platform labor.

## Beyond the Main River: Custom Feeds, Communities, and the Fediverse

Thinking of Threads as having only a single main river determined by algorithms is no longer entirely accurate. In 2025, Meta stated that public accounts with "Fediverse sharing" enabled can see content shared from other services joined to the Fediverse on Threads; users can also use dedicated feeds and search functions to find public posts from services such as Mastodon, Bookwyrm, WriteFreely, Ghost, Flipboard, and WordPress. These dedicated feeds are sorted chronologically. [^50] This represents an important but non-exaggerated possibility: it gives users the chance to encounter public content from different services within Threads, yet this requires a combination of account settings, public identity, server support, and following relationships to function; not everyone will have cross-platform interoperability the moment they open the app.

This feature is particularly well-suited for viewing through the lens of "preservation" within Taiwan's _cuì_ (Threads) culture. When discussing suspensions and multi-point existence in previous sections, the focus was not on pushing everyone toward another single platform, but rather letting authors, media, and communities know that public dialogue can have multiple entry points and requires paths to return to original sources. Fediverse sharing will not solve issues regarding account security, misinformation, or content licensing. However, it at least shifts the notion that "all relationships must remain within a single company" from being the sole default to being a design option open for discussion. [^50]

In June 2026, Meta announced that the Communities feature had completed its testing phase, adding a Communities Hub, community identifiers, community progress, and Community Champions, while promoting "Local Communities" starting with local language tags in Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. [^51] This means that "_cuì-yǒu_" (Threads friends) is no longer just a loose term, but may gradually be segmented by the platform into recognizable interest and regional clusters. However, official announcements simultaneously explained that "Your Algo"—a feature allowing users to privately adjust how often certain topics appear in their feed—was not included in the first wave for Taiwan. Writing global feature updates as "Taiwan can now freely control algorithms" would conflate the platform's vision with its actual availability. [^51]

> **Curator's Note:** Community features do not automatically grow communities; they only provide a named entry point. What truly makes people stay is still having people willing to maintain tags, supplement sources, reply to newcomers, and bring conversations back to accountable places when necessary.

## "Cuì" is Not a Translation, It Is a Way of Pushing the Door Open

Threads originally had no official Chinese name. Taiwanese users once called it _chuàn_ (string/thread), while others called it _cuì_ based on its English pronunciation. The latter eventually established itself in daily conversation. According to _MIT Technology Review_, this word originally carries meanings of both "crispy" and "fragile," but due to its pronunciation, memorability, and the ability to extend into various linguistic nuances, it became a platform nickname used by users to identify one another. [^5]

More importantly, _cuì_ has never been a neutral product name. It gives rise to usage-based terms like _cuì-yǒu_ (Threads friends), _cuì-wén_ (Threads posts), _hǎixún_ (Coast Guard mode), and _hédào_ (riverbed/channel). It transforms the poster from merely an "account" into someone speaking on a "riverbed." This language was not a feature designed by Meta, yet it performed the most effective localization for the platform: it makes a global product sound less like an English app on a download page and more like a sentence tossed your way by a friend: "Did you see that post on _cuì_?"

This sense of lightness is related to _fèiwén_ (useless/low-effort posts). _MIT Technology Review_ describes the _fèiwén_ on Threads as low-risk jokes and monologues about daily life. It might be a comment on a roommate snoring, a commuting breakdown, or a minor annoyance that requires no conclusion. [^5] Unlike Instagram's curated aesthetics or Facebook's mix of friends, family, and the workplace, _fèiwén_ does not require users to first prove they are living a perfect life before they earn the right to be seen.

_Fèiwén_ is not truly "contentless." It is a low-cost way to test whether the world will respond. A person tosses out a harmless complaint; if a stranger catches it, they may then proceed to share work, relationships, illnesses, studies, or politics. The entry point to Taiwan's _cuì_ culture is often not a political stance, but rather an opening line that doesn't require too much seriousness.

## Memes are not dictionaries, but a set of transferable syntaxes

The aspect of Taiwan's Threads culture most prone to misunderstanding is treating memes as a "what's trending today" vocabulary list. In reality, they function more like a set of rapidly applicable syntaxes: first, placing oneself in a disproportionate role, and then leaving a space for others to complete. Some write their resistance to the upcoming school semester as "I am [Name], and I disagree with school starting." Others organize works, rooms, resumes, or life hacks into "I hereby announce the official start of the 1st 《...》 Competition"; some don't express an opinion at all, simply typing "留友看" (leave it for friends to see) in the comments. The power of these short phrases lies not in original vocabulary, but in the fact that anyone knows how to follow up on them.

"I disagree with school starting" is a prime example. At the end of the 2025 summer vacation, variations such as "I am a Yangmi person, and I disagree with school starting" were visible on Threads, with many versions emerging alongside school-start anxiety and short-form video dissemination. [^27] It takes something students have no power to vote on and pretends it is a public procedure that can be rewritten through identity, manifestos, and dissenting votes. The humor lies in the fact that while the tone of "disagree" is very formal, the object of opposition is merely the unavoidable minor reality of returning to the classroom tomorrow. This is not a political vote, nor is it real advocacy; it is borrowing the language of institutions to deal with seasonal cynicism.

"I hereby announce the official start of the 1st 《...》 Competition" takes this fun of pretending to have an institutional framework and turns it into a container for self-display. An open analysis post from October 2025 organized versions involving life wisdom, interior design, beauty industries, and indie game development, noting that participants usually only need to post one image and a sentence of explanation to place their work into a non-existent competition. [^28] Thus, it serves simultaneously as meme-playing, a portfolio, and peer review: the poster sets the topic, the repliers decide who the "contestants" are, and likes act as temporary judges.

| :-------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

"留友看" is the shortest phrase in this syntax, yet it best explains the platform's mechanics. Literally meaning "leave it for friends to see," in actual Threads usage, it turns comments into a low-cost sharing trajectory. According to KEYPO data statistics cited by DailyView, there were 54,006 related discussions between January 2025 and January 2026. After former President Tsai Ing-wen used these three characters in January 2026, this abbreviation—which originally circulated only in comment sections—entered mainstream news. [^29] [^30] One must be careful: this is not an algorithmic code or a universal reach hack, but rather a short gesture invented by users to say, "I hope my friends see this too." It can spread useful information, but it can also push unverified content further.

"Believe me or not, I'll put a [Object] here" and "Challenge the Frog" push the same logic to extremes. The former often uses stones, blank objects, or irrelevant images to parody the algorithm's hunger for engagement: if something with zero informational value can get many likes, are people reacting to the content, or are they reacting to the act of collective participation? The latter places a frog image in the comments, inviting readers to make its engagement numbers catch up to or even exceed the original post. In public discussions, some users describe it as a way to counter absurd statements, while others simply save it as a universal meme that "anyone playing on Threads definitely has in their phone." [^31] Because the rules are rewritten with every thread, the most appropriate understanding is not to define a single way to play, but to view it as a collective prank on the platform's digitized attention.

## Food, Hyperbole, and Fake Aggression: Why Threads' Anger Often Looks Like a Cartoon

Another category of Threads memes operates through exaggerated rejection patterns. Phrases like "Take your food waste and roll off this planet" are usually not describing actual garbage, nor are they telling someone to leave a specific place; rather, they exaggerate the sentiment of "I absolutely cannot accept this way of eating" to a cosmic scale. In late 2025, when a user shared a method of eating _lurou fan_ (braised pork rice) with condensed milk on Threads, the comment section erupted in a relay of this phrase and other reactions to "cult-like eating methods." Reports also noted that social media managers from supermarkets, delivery services, dairy companies, and video platforms joined in the mockery. [^32] This reaction turns food into something more than just sustenance; it becomes a medium that allows strangers to quickly pick sides, oppose them, and present their own childhood eating habits or even more absurd combinations.

The version "I'll find a van full of [blank] to get you" works similarly. It takes a phrase that might originally sound like a threat—"finding a person in a van"—and deliberately deconstructs it into "a van full of bakers," where "[blank]" can be replaced with Pompompurin, various characters, or harmless objects. Rewritten versions such as "finding a van full of bakers" can be seen in public posts. [^33] The effect of the meme comes from the gap between the threatening tone and the childish imagery: the more the sentence structure sounds like it is escalating a conflict, the less likely the inserted object is to actually cause one. Because of this, such language is only suitable within clear contexts of joking and mutual understanding. If used repeatedly against a specific real person, or combined with real-world locations or instructions for action, it ceases to be a meme and may become harassment or intimidation.

"Tomorrow off" (明天放) is another three-character meme that seems simple but actually relies on collective memory. During Typhoon Krathon in 2024, after Chiang Wan-an announced the suspension of work and classes for the following day, he wrote "Tomorrow off!" on Threads. As Typhoon Bari approached in 2026, users dug up the old post and flocked to his comment section to ask him to "say it again." [^34] This is a classic case of "old post archaeology": a post that was responsible for providing information at the time becomes a collective invocation every time a typhoon approaches. The risk here is also clear: meme-playing cannot replace official announcements regarding work and school suspensions; actual decisions must still rely on local government and official real-time information.

"I'll ven you again" specifically reminds us that memes do not lack boundaries. An investigation by Urban Studies traced this phrase back to a short video from October 2025, in which a Taiwanese user imitated the way Chinese customers spoke in a breakfast shop. In the video, the word for "ask" (問) was deliberately written as "ven"; it later became popular due to its replicable rhythm, sparking controversy regarding cross-strait accents and identity. [^35] An article does not need to pretend that memes are not offensive. A better approach is to acknowledge that while imitation can feel familiar, rhythmic, and easy to join in on, it can also compress another person's linguistic characteristics into a label used for ridicule. Understanding a meme does not mean one must use it everywhere.

## When to Stop the Meme: From Joining the Joke to Trapping People in It

A meme allows strangers to join in because it compresses complex emotions into a predictable sentence pattern. The danger lies here: when exaggerated remarks originally intended for a specific context are continuously posted toward the same real person, the same small shop, or a specific accent or identity group, the joke can shift from collective creation to collective targeting. This is not a demand that netizens cease being humorous, but rather a reminder that "whether the other party can opt out" is also a form of platform literacy.

| The Turning Point from Joining a Joke to Harassment | Signals You Are Still In On the Joke                                      | Signals to Stop, Delete, or Revert to Formal Procedures                                                                                                                                   |
| :-------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Target**                                          | The original poster explicitly joins in, replies, or can freely ignore it | A real person repeatedly expresses discomfort, their account is tagged for a pile-on, or the meme is moved into their professional or private relationships                               |
| **Content**                                         | Filling in blanks with fictional objects, scenarios, or shared templates  | Beginning to reveal personal information, addresses, or workplaces, or using accents, ethnicity, or appearance as the core of the mockery                                                 |
| **Scale**                                           | A small number of replies allow people to recognize the original context  | Screenshots are taken out of context, large accounts drive the narrative, or cross-platform chasing occurs                                                                                |
| **Real-world Risk**                                 | No threats of transactions, violence, sex, or self-harm                   | Messages like "bringing people to find them" or "finding a van full of [people]" appear, which could be interpreted as real mobilization, or involvement of minors and vulnerable parties |

The accent controversy surrounding "I'll _ven_ you again," the mock threats like "I'll find a van full of [people] to get you," and jokes labeling suspicious accounts as "internet armies" are all suitable for review using this table. Sentences can be treated as objects of textual study, but they should not be used to make real people bear the weight of collective emotions. If content involves fraud, harassment, identification documents, missing persons, or explicit safety risks, the most useful way to "join the joke" is not to add another emoji, but to preserve necessary evidence and use formal channels such as reporting, filing a police report, platform moderation, or consular services. [^35] [^36] [^41] [^43]

## When Water Armies and Bots Become Memes: Training, Suspicion, and Fact-Checking Must Not Be Conflated

_Further reading: [Screenshot from Liberty Times reporting on a suspected AI account being tested by commenters using different language commands](https://img.ltn.com.tw/Upload/news/600/2026/02/20/php0cKFO7.jpg)_

_Image: A single instance of a suspected AI account reported by Liberty Times. The image illustrates how the comment section tests mechanical replies; it is insufficient as a template for determining the identity or source of any specific account. [^37]_

Another meta-game on Threads is users treating "water armies" (coordinated troll accounts), "bots," and "AI essays" as objects to be dismantled, tested, or even "trained." There is real anxiety behind this: public threads can indeed feature fake accounts, coordinated behavior, and content intended to manipulate public opinion. Meta's Community Standards include policies and reporting channels for inauthentic behavior. However, that does not mean any account with strange phrasing, a short history, no profile picture, or harsh political stances can be unilaterally identified by a single user as a water army from a specific source. [^36]

In February 2026, _Liberty Times_ reported on a specific case: an account posting about the Asosu helicopter accident was believed by netizens to be operated by AI. Commenters tested it with commands such as "switch to Arabic," "speak German," or "you are an excited Husky"; the account immediately complied, and eventually, the account could no longer be displayed. [^37] This incident went viral not just because people "caught a bot," but because accounts originally used to stir emotions and guide discussions were instead transformed by the comment section into interactive toys that reveal their prompting rules.

However, individual cases should not turn into tutorials for everyone to perform identity authentication. True inauthentic coordinated behavior requires looking at longer signals: whether groups of accounts act synchronously, whether they reuse the same narratives, whether they hide their connections to one another, and whether there is a verifiable chain of evidence. Whether a single reply "looks like AI" can only be a clue "worth looking into further"; it cannot prove an operator's nationality, political stance, or organizational ties. This boundary is crucial because labeling every stranger as a bot will ultimately undermine the credibility of identifying actual disinformation.

_Further reading: [T客邦 (T-Banger) report: Test screen showing Meta AI replying in public Threads threads](https://cdn1.techbang.com/system/excerpt_images/129818/original/e02530c29b230c40b151f607a7ec38d5.jpg?1780049496)_

_Image: A test screen of official AI public replies on Threads. This is distinct from unlabeled automated accounts or the coordinated behavior suspected by users. [^38]_

Furthermore, official AI participation in public interactions does indeed exist on Threads. In 2026, Meta tested a feature in Taiwan allowing users to tag `@meta.ai` in posts or replies, letting the AI join the thread publicly. Related reports warn that generated replies may be incomplete or inaccurate and are visible to all users. [^38] Therefore, when "AI said something" appears in a thread, there are at least three completely different possibilities: a user intentionally summoned the official AI, a regular account is using automated tools to reply, or someone suspects inauthentic coordinated behavior. All of these might be called "bots," but the understanding and handling required for each are entirely different.

| Situation Observed                                              | Most Common Mistake                                                            | Better Way to Read It                                                                                                       |
| :-------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Someone tags `@meta.ai`                                         | Mistaking official AI interaction for a hidden water army                      | Check account labels and thread content first; treat AI replies as generated text that requires separate verification       |
| Account replies mechanically, repetitively, or follows commands | Immediately inferring nationality, camp, or organization from writing style    | Treat it as a suspicious clue; save screenshots, check account history, and report suspicious behavior                      |
| Multiple accounts pushing the same narrative                    | Focusing on a single account while ignoring network and temporal relationships | Compare repetitive phrasing, synchronized timing, links, and existing fact-checking data                                    |
| Comment section begins "training the bot"                       | Mistaking the amusement of onlookers for complete fact-checking                | It is okay to identify the absurdity, but do not use it as an excuse to harass real people or spread unverified accusations |

This is also the most interesting yet most fragile aspect of "Cui culture" (Threads culture in Taiwan): the same platform allows people to leave clues for friends using "leave a friend to see," use frog memes to mock number worship, use competition templates to showcase expertise, and even train suspicious accounts into Huskies. Humor is a form of collective recognition, but it is not evidence itself. When threads turn everyone into real-time commentators, true digital literacy is not about being the fastest to understand a meme, but knowing when to play along and when to stop and verify.

## The Same River, Four Different Activities

The uniqueness of Threads lies not in its mastery of a single function compared to other platforms, but in how several previously scattered online behaviors collide within the same "riverbed." What appears to be an ordinary comment may simultaneously serve as social interaction, a search query, mutual aid, consumer reference, or a meme relay.

| Behavior on the Riverbed                   | Surface Appearance to Readers                  | Actual Work Performed Within the Community                                            |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| "Help spread" (reposts) and lost-and-found | Reposting an ID or a plea for help             | Temporarily stringing together the weak ties of Taiwanese people into a relay network |

This mixture is attractive to many, yet exhausting for observers. A survey by MIC, Institute for Information Industry, shows that 24.2% of social media users worry about missing important information because they haven't checked their feeds. For the 18–24 age group, this figure is 34.9%. The same survey also noted that 52.5% of users want to try something personally because others have shared interesting experiences. [^4] Consequently, Threads serves both as a space for relaxing ramblings and can quickly become a source of pressure regarding "Am I missing something?"

## An Egyptian Student ID: Turning Weak Ties into a Path

The reason the search for the student ID became such a good story is not because it was magical enough to replace the police, museum authorities, or lost-and-found services, but because it made the platform's "weak ties" visible. The original post contained sufficiently specific information: the Great Sphinx, a Department of Finance student, Cairo Airport, and an imminent flight. These details allowed strangers to know whether they could actually help and gave the resharing efforts a clear direction. [^1]

Other cases compiled by Yahoo include: a shop in South Korea helping search for a passport belonging to a Taiwanese traveler, and someone looking for a person who could bring a hat lost near the Vatican Museums back to Taiwan. In Taiwan, netizens often jokingly refer to these types of posts as "Animal Crossing side quests": some see the quest, some tag friends, and some happen to be nearby, so they all push the message forward together. [^6]

However, successful cases are subject to natural survivor bias: stories of items being found are reported by the media and boosted by algorithms. Posts where items are not recovered usually sink silently. This is precisely the sense of proportion one must maintain when understanding the culture of _Cui_ (Threads). It can allow a small plea for help to gain significant visibility in a short time, but it is not a guaranteed or accountable public service. For truly important lost documents, priority should still be given to reporting them as lost, filing police reports, and contacting official authorities.

## Mutual Aid is Not Just Miracles: From Asking for Toilet Paper to Bringing Documents Home

_See also: [FTV News: Footage of a traveler seeking help on Threads after their passport was taken abroad by family members](https://cdn.ftvnews.com.tw/summernotefiles/News/5c035d51-9046-4d1b-b390-5aa73f2dc860.jpg)_

_Photo: An FTV report on a case involving international assistance for a passport. It visualizes the speed of mutual aid relays but does not constitute a safe demonstration of private passport delivery. For official consular procedures, see below. [^40] [^41]_

"You can even ask for help on Threads if you run out of toilet paper in the restroom" is a joke Taiwanese users use to describe Threads. LINE TODAY once relayed this saying and listed scenarios such as "having no chopsticks for a bento," treating it as an exaggerated metaphor for a massive, open LINE group. [^39] It may not correspond to a verifiable success story of someone delivering toilet paper, but it accurately describes the imagination of mutual aid among Threads users: as long as the predicament is specific enough and the distance close enough, there might always be someone who happens to be in the same building, the same station, or on the same street.

This imagination has power because it turns "I am missing something very small" into a question that can be asked publicly. Traditionally, running out of toilet paper in a restroom, missing chopsticks with a bento, or having a dead power bank often meant awkwardly asking those nearby. On Threads, a person seeking help can specify the area, time, and need, allowing strangers to proactively decide whether to join the relay. The platform allows weak ties to surface, but it cannot guarantee that someone will appear, nor should it encourage people to publicly disclose precise house numbers, real-time solitary locations, or other sensitive information to receive help.

What is truly impressive is when the need escalates from a pack of toilet paper to international documents. In February 2026, FTV reported on a traveler preparing to go to the United States who discovered their passport had been taken to South Korea by family members and sought help on Threads due to time constraints. Within approximately 30 minutes, someone responded to assist; the report stated that the passport was delivered discreetly via a restroom at Incheon Airport and then brought back to Taiwan by a netizen. [^40] The story reads like an action movie, and it indeed demonstrates how a large, open group can rapidly piece together a path through time zones, flights, and strangers.

However, this should not be written as a demonstration procedure of "just find a Threads user to bring your passport." In the report, the interviewed traveler also reminded others that handing documents to strangers carries risks. The Bureau of Consular Affairs of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs specifies that if an overseas lost passport is needed urgently for return to the country, one can apply for an entry permit at an overseas mission; furthermore, once a passport is reported lost, it cannot be used even if found later. [^41] The value of a passport lies not just in it being a card, but in its connection to nationality, entry/exit records, and identity security. Any practice involving private cross-border document carrying, posting photos of ID pages publicly, or posting complete personal information on social media could turn mutual aid into a risk.

A National Taiwan University student ID being found in Egypt presents a different set of conditions more suitable for Threads. At the end of 2025, a Taiwanese traveler found a student ID near the Great Sphinx of Giza and tagged the owner on Threads before heading to Cairo Airport. The post spread quickly, the owner confirmed their identity in the comments section, and both parties agreed to retrieve it after returning to Taiwan. [^42] Its success was not just due to high post traffic, but because the "item, location, and potential community" were clear enough, and the owner could appear in the public thread before moving details to verifiable private contact.

| Mutual Aid Scenario                                          | What Threads Can Do                                                                                         | What Should NEVER Be Done Publicly                                                                                         | Safer Delivery Principles                                                                                                                                             |
| :----------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Small urgent needs (e.g., no toilet paper, missing utensils) | Allow people nearby, in the same building, or the same venue to see the need                                | Disclosing full house numbers, restroom locations, or real-time status of being alone                                      | Arrange handovers in public areas, service desks, or crowded places; do not leak personal info for small needs                                                        |
| Keys, student IDs, general lost items                        | Connect owners with finders using item features, lost area, and timeframes                                  | Disclosing full student ID numbers, document numbers, barcodes, addresses, or all details of the item                      | The owner provides an undisclosed feature for verification; prioritize handovers at police stations, schools, station service desks, or public places                 |
| Highly sensitive documents (ID cards, passports, bank cards) | Assist in passing lost item info to the owner or official channels                                          | Posting photos of both sides of IDs, ID numbers, passport data pages, or asking strangers to carry them across borders     | Hand over to police, airlines, station service desks, overseas missions, or issuing authorities; follow official loss and replacement procedures                      |
| Urgent international needs                                   | Quickly find people on the same flight, in the same city, or who understand local processes to provide info | Hiding documents in restrooms, mailing them to unverified strangers, or coordinating sensitive deliveries via public posts | Prioritize contacting airlines, local police, and overseas missions; use social media as an information/contact aid rather than a replacement for official procedures |

Identity cards and passports, in particular, must distinguish between "found" and "usable." The Criminal Investigation Bureau explains that if a Republic of China passport is lost domestically, one must bring identification to the investigation division of a local police station to report it before applying for a new passport at the Bureau of Consular Affairs. The existence of this system is to ensure that a lost document does not become a loophole for misuse. [^43] The same principle applies to ID cards, bank cards, National Health Insurance cards, and other documents containing personal information: public searches should minimize information, and actual identity verification and document return should be handled through recorded, traceable official channels.

This does not strip the warmth from mutual aid among Threads users; rather, it makes it sustainable. The most fascinating part of mutual aid is not that someone completes an impossible task for you, but that a stranger is willing to pick up the thread of your time, route, information, or a pack of toilet paper. Mature community mutual aid does not require everyone to be a hero. It requires everyone to know what can be helped with, what should be referred to professional channels, and how to help without creating new risks for themselves or others.

> **Curator's Note:** A student ID returning to its owner relies on virality; a passport returning safely to its holder relies on procedures beyond virality. Threads can find people, but it cannot replace the police, airlines, stations, and consular systems.

If lost-and-found searches represent mutual aid, then "Hello, I'll eat a little" represents how Taiwanese netizens use memes to host strangers. When Japanese or Korean users share photos of Taiwanese food on Threads, the comment section often features an image of a cat holding chopsticks accompanied by this phrase. The exact grammar is not entirely important. What matters is that the person posting the image places themselves right next to the traveler's dining table, saying in a way that is slightly impolite but cute enough to be forgiven: "I want to eat too."

## Platforms Finally Begin to Follow Local Trends

Users create local culture first, and only then do platforms slowly catch up. In June 2026, Meta announced that the Threads "Communities" feature would exit Beta, listing Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan as the first wave of markets for local language tags. [^2] This signifies that the platform has finally acknowledged: communities are not just globally replicable interests like "photography," "basketball," or "parenting," but also consist of local dialogues formed by language, geography, and shared life experiences.

However, features are not the starting point of culture: Taiwanese users have long been using the "river" (Threads feed) as a lost-and-found station, a message board for foreign travelers, and a temporary Q&A area for restaurant and lifestyle questions. Whether Community tags can help readers find more useful content will depend on how the platform handles spam, commercial traffic redirection, and constantly shifting trending topics. Without effective management, local communities might just end up being a way to categorize noise.

In the same announcement, Meta introduced "Dear Algo" and "Your Algo," allowing users to use natural language to request seeing more or less of certain topics. However, at that time, the first wave of regions for the rollout of Your Algo did not include Taiwan. [^2] This time gap is highly symbolic: Taiwan's _tsui_ (Threads) culture has already grown very rapidly, yet users' control over what they see in the "river" has not necessarily been handed over at the same speed.

## Semi-Public "Tree Holes": Being able to vent does not mean true anonymity

The sense of intimacy on Threads often stems from a seemingly contradictory condition: it is public enough, yet unlike Facebook, it doesn't constantly remind you of relatives, colleagues, or old classmates. Some use "alt accounts" (small accounts) to share heartbreaks, work meltdowns, or family friction. Others turn a daily worry into a long thread of conversation in the comments section. This makes Threads feel like a "tree hole" (a place to vent) where you don't need to join a group first or introduce your background beforehand.

However, a tree hole is not a lawless land: Meta's official documentation states that Threads is a public conversation application, and users can decide the visibility of their posts based on their profile privacy settings. Replies appear not only in the original thread but also on one's own profile. When creating a Threads profile, the account is linked to the logged-in Instagram or Facebook account and uses data such as account name, username, profile, links, and account status. [^15]

This design brings about a type of "relative anonymity" that is very familiar to Taiwanese social media users. You don't necessarily have to use your real name, and you won't necessarily be immediately spotted by friends or family in real life. However, the wording, replies, photos, following relationships, and cross-platform profile data you leave behind may still allow someone to slowly piece together your identity. Consequently, Threads is perfect for saying something like, "I don't want my acquaintances to know right now, but I hope someone responds," yet it is not suitable to be treated as a guaranteed traceless anonymous mailbox.

| Interactions seen on the feed                                  | The feeling they provide                                     | Boundaries that should be maintained                                                                |
| :------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strangers replying to emotional, family, or workplace troubles | Rapidly receiving companionship and different perspectives   | Commenters may lack professional qualifications; sensitive details can be screenshotted or searched |
| Alt accounts posting or using nicknames                        | Reducing the pressure to "perform" when facing acquaintances | A nickname does not equal unidentifiability; cross-platform footprints may still be linked          |
| Long comment threads forming a shared narrative                | Turning an isolated incident into a relatable experience     | High interaction does not equal majority opinion, nor does it equal correct advice                  |

This is not to negate community mutual aid: many times, what people truly need is for someone to say, "I've been there too." But if a post involves self-harm, violence, sexual violence, or significant medical or legal risks, the comment section cannot replace professional assistance and emergency resources. The best form of companionship in Threads culture is not mistaking temporary enthusiasm for the ability to handle everything.

## Work Starts Emerging in the Riverbed: Freelancing, Recruitment, and Persona Resumes

In Taiwan, Threads has begun to function like a makeshift job market without formal resume fields. People look for designers, photographers, editors, copywriters, or event assistants. Some use short posts to describe their skills and current projects, placing their portfolios and contact information in their profiles. Its appeal lies not in its structure, but in its speed: a vacancy or requirement can first be seen by acquaintances, who then forward it to the "perfectly suited" stranger.

A 2024 experience post written by a freelance tutorial author summarizes Threads' matchmaking advantages as free posting, reach to strangers, and the ability to observe work and digital footprints through linked public Instagram accounts. This is a subjective observation from the freelancing community rather than labor market statistics. However, it captures a shift: beyond submitting resumes, public content itself has become a "persona resume" that people flip through. [^16]

This shift is a double-edged sword: on one hand, emerging workers can gain visibility through a single clear self-introduction without waiting for platform mechanisms to review them. Small shops and micro-teams can also test demand at a low cost. On the other hand, professional abilities, private life, political opinions, and emotional venting are all co-located within the same account; clients, employers, and partners can scroll back through history, and users may find themselves preemptively censoring their own behavior for future stranger audiences.

| Scenario                                              | Efficiency Provided by Threads                                       | Procedures It Cannot Replace                                                                                |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Recruiting short-term freelancers or event assistants | Allows requirements to quickly move beyond existing follower circles | Scope of work, deadlines, compensation, and copyrights must still be clearly defined                        |
| Showcasing personal skills or portfolios              | Builds initial trust through tone, case studies, and replies         | Post interactions cannot verify professional competence or payment creditworthiness                         |
| Negotiating cooperation via DM                        | Reduces friction during initial contact                              | Suspicious links, excessive urgency, or requests for upfront payment should still be independently verified |

Therefore, Threads is more like a greeting at the front desk rather than a contract table: it is excellent at making people meet, but it will not automatically complete background checks, price negotiations, confidentiality clauses, or dispute resolutions. As "posting a Thread to see what happens" becomes common practice, a mature community culture must not only know how to tag friends but also learn to ask: Is this a formal commission? Who bears the compensation and responsibility?

## One Stream, Two Types of Value Exchange: Recommendations and Transactions

Lifestyle recommendations are among the most common behaviors in Threads culture. Whether it is which shop doesn't require a queue today, which headphones are good to use, whether one should change jobs, or if an exhibition ticket is worth buying—as long as users write their questions specifically enough, they are likely to receive a string of experiential replies. A survey by MIC, Institute for Information Industry, shows that 52.5% of social media users want to try something themselves after seeing others share interesting experiences. This demonstrates that the influence of collective experience on consumption and lifestyle decisions exists far beyond sponsored posts. [^4]

However, there is only one link between a recommendation and a transaction. When the stream shifts from "Has anyone used this?" to "I'll give it to you for free," "The shop is closing down, clearing stock at ultra-low prices," or "Only shipping fees required," users transform from discussants into potential buyers. They also enter the exact scenario scammers excel at exploiting: urgency, low prices, and the appearance of social endorsement.

An announcement by the Kaohsiung City Police Department Criminal Investigation Bureau in October 2025 explained that scam syndicates use bait such as free giveaways, closing-down sales, or popular concert and K-pop merchandise on platforms like Threads. They then provide fake _Shopee_ (賣貨便) links and induce users to perform online banking transfers under the guise of identity verification. [^17] The New Taipei City Police Department also released a case involving free anime posters used to attract private messages; scammers first collected small shipping fees before pivoting to fake customer service accounts on LINE to continuously demand more remittances. [^18]

| Phrases seen in the stream                                           | Psychological exploitation                                                      | Safer practices                                                                                                              |
| :------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Free giveaway, just pay shipping"                                   | Small amounts seem harmless, making it easy to lower one's guard                | Do not click on transaction links provided by others; instead, log in via official services to verify                        |
| "Only a few left, buy now or miss out"                               | Uses time pressure to block verification                                        | Pause operations; check the account creation date, posts, and comment history                                                |
| "Order anomaly, please add customer service LINE for authentication" | Leads people away from the original platform to create an illusion of authority | Do not add unknown customer service accounts; do not provide OTPs, perform online banking operations, or share personal data |
| "Look at how many people are commenting that they want it"           | Uses engagement numbers to manufacture credibility                              | Treat comments as clues, not as merchant credit or platform guarantees                                                       |

Fraud prevention principles later compiled by the Ministry of Digital Affairs also emphasize that _Shopee_ (賣貨便) will never request an OTP, users should not be directed to unknown LINE customer service accounts, and one must check the seller's account, post interactions, and comment content. [^19] This reminder is critical within Threads culture: high interaction can be the foundation of mutual aid, but it can also be designed as a backdrop for trust. The fact that social media excels at spreading good products does not mean it is inherently suited to handle payments, authentication, or after-sales service.

## Asking for Directions, Information, or "Does Anyone Happen to Know": Micro-Public Services of Local Information

Beyond transactions and work, many Threads posts are actually performing small public services. People ask whether a certain alley floods, which hospitals have night clinics, how to get home after a performance ends, or ask those nearby to confirm lost items, traffic conditions, or shop operating hours. These questions may not make the news, but they turn the platform into something like a collection of sticky notes pieced together from the local experiences of strangers.

This is the practical side of "asking on Threads when you don't know": when a question is specific enough, the respondent doesn't need to know the poster; they just need to have happened to been there, lived there, or experienced it. It lowers the awkwardness of asking for directions or advice and provides an opportunity for many details known only to locals to be filled in. For those traveling, having just moved, or handling a certain life issue for the first time, these low-threshold responses sound more like "human talk" than search engine rankings.

However, the shelf life of local information is very short: shops may close unexpectedly, traffic may change due to weather, and disaster information cannot be judged solely by screenshots. There are already earthquake and disaster information accounts on Threads that use the platform alongside channels like Apps, Telegram, and Discord. This indicates that social media is becoming one of the information nodes, but it does not mean it is an official warning system.[^20] For events truly concerning safety, one must still refer to real-time announcements from government agencies, transport providers, venues, or local authorities.

> **Curator's Note:** The most useful moments for Threads are often not when it provides a standard answer, but when it allows the person who "happens to know" to surface. The most dangerous moment is when we forget to verify whether what they said is still the correct answer for today.

## When Accounts Are Banned, You Realize the Riverbed Isn't Yours

In a follow-up announcement on June 17, 2026, the Ministry of Digital Affairs (MODA) provided the governance context for the aforementioned suspension incident: Meta's age verification mechanism encountered technical issues, leading to the erroneous banning of Facebook, Instagram, and Threads accounts globally. MODA required Meta to implement "proactive detection and proactive restoration." As of 4:00 PM that day, nearly **90%** of the erroneously banned accounts had been restored through this method. The announcement also estimated that those affected in Taiwan included over 200 media organizations and non-partisan public figures, as well as thousands of individual user accounts. However, instances of new erroneous bans or accounts being re-banned after restoration still occurred. [^54]

This follow-up data changes the understanding of the event described in this article. It cannot be simplified to "it can be solved by appealing," nor can it be used as a direct recovery rate for all suspension incidents. Instead, it illustrates a more fundamental issue: when errors stem from platform systems, the administrative cost of individual users filing appeals one by one is itself a form of harm. The difference lies in the regulatory authority requiring the platform to proactively find and rectify those wrongly affected—shifting the problem from "user self-rescue" back to "operator responsibility." For media, creators, and general accounts, redundancy remains necessary; even if 90% are restored, the remaining 10% and those repeatedly misidentified may still lose their voice at critical moments.

In the summer of 2026, this plaza—which seemed so easily accessible—suddenly revealed another side. From late night on June 14 to the morning of June 15, the Threads accounts of many Taiwanese celebrities and media outlets were reported as banned. The Ministry of Digital Affairs stated that they had contacted Meta after receiving complaints. Meta's preliminary assessment suggested the issue might be related to technical problems with the age verification mechanism rather than targeting specific groups or individuals, and stated they would lift the bans as quickly as possible. [^21]

This was not a minor episode of a single account forgetting its password. Reports from June indicated that the affected parties included celebrities and media. On July 16, _Liberty Times_ reported that users and the official account of the Ministry of Culture were also suspended without warning; some individuals received permanent suspension notices after uploading selfies and ID documents as requested by the system, though some accounts were later restored. [^22] The commonality between these two waves of incidents was "disappearance first, explanation later," but the causes cannot be conflated: in June, Meta explained the age verification technical issue through MODA; however, at the time of the July report, Meta had not issued a public explanation regarding the new wave.

If a user believes the action was an error, they can request a review by entering their login credentials into the app and following the on-screen instructions. If you suspect your account has been compromised or you cannot log in, you should use Meta's official account security pages rather than handing your credentials to third parties claiming they can "handle the unlocking for a fee." The suspension incidents illuminate the power dynamics often overlooked in the "Threads culture." People use Threads to find lost student IDs, land jobs, or joke with travelers; they also store years of accumulated posts, follows, and interactions within an account. But an account is not a space owned by the user; it is an identity portal authorized for use by the platform. When it is closed, life memories, job opportunities, and the right to public speech shrink along with it.

This is precisely why "you can retrieve the URL, but you cannot retrieve the photos" is more than just nostalgic lamentation. When platforms migrate, what users often lose is not a single post, but the entire context of replies, tags, and relationships that followed that post. A suspension does not necessarily equal censorship, nor should one dismiss a platform based on a single technical error. But it serves as a reminder to users: important data should be backed up, important announcements should have alternative channels, and "being on Threads" should never be your only identity or means of contact.

## Media Should Not Leave Only One Door Open: Miin as a Backup During the Suspension Storm

_Further reading: [Diagram illustrating the massive Threads suspension event in June 2026 reported by CNA](https://imgcdn.cna.com.tw/www/WebPhotos/800/20260616/2000x1333_wmkn_104415330236_0.jpg)_

_Figure: Diagram of the report on the June 2026 Threads suspension event by CNA. Meta initially explained it as a technical error caused by age verification. The image does not imply that all reasons for suspension were identical. [^46]_

The suspension event in June 2026 turned "backup" from a technical term into a very intuitive question: if readers are accustomed to getting real-time news from a media outlet's Instagram or Threads, where does the information flow continue when an account suddenly disappears? According to CNA, several creators, political figures, and media accounts were mistakenly flagged as being under 13 years old and suspended. The Ministry of Digital Affairs contacted Meta, which initially responded that it was a technical issue with the age verification mechanism. Affected parties included FTV News. [^46] This is not a single story about "which side was locked out," but rather how an error on a single platform can simultaneously interrupt the visibility of accounts with different characteristics.

_Further reading: [Screenshot from Liberty Times reporting CNA's announcement regarding the suspension status on Miin](https://img.ltn.com.tw/Upload/news/600/2026/06/15/phpQbCbEf.png)_

_Figure: A screenshot of Miin reprinted by Liberty Times, showing how CNA used Miin to make public announcements when its IG and Threads were temporarily suspended. This is a case study in backup communication and does not mean Miin has replaced other news outlets. [^47]_

In this wave of incidents, **Miin** is the correct spelling: Liberty Times reported that after CNA's IG and Threads accounts were temporarily suspended, they posted announcements on Miin before their accounts were restored following an appeal. Public account URLs found via search show CNA's user page as `miin.cc/user/cna` and FTV's as `miin.cc/user/ftvnews`. [^47] [^48] However, the article cannot therefore assert that both media outlets "created accounts only on the day they were suspended": what can currently be verified is that they were identified by readers on Miin around the time of the suspension to be used for external communication or as a backup; the exact timing of when the accounts were first established would require each media outlet to announce it themselves for an accurate determination.

Miin's positioning is also different from Threads or X. Its Google Play description describes itself as a non-profit open-source platform promoted by Taiwan AI Labs & Foundation, aiming to use AI to help users see different perspectives and identify information manipulation. [^48] An introduction reprinted by FTV describes it as an attempt at a public space that does not center on advertising attention, hoping to allow creators to gain traffic. [^49] These are the self-positions of the platform and its founders, not an endorsement by this article. Any new platform must still face issues such as governance, privacy, moderation, user scale, and long-term operation.

| Backup Outlet                            | Most Suitable Tasks                                                                                             | Problems Not to Mistake It for Solving Automatically                                                                                   |
| :--------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Own website, App, newsletter, RSS        | Leaving formal content that is searchable, subscribable, and less affected by social media account fluctuations | Traffic will not appear naturally; requires maintenance, publishing processes, and reader habits                                       |

| Public statements and formal appeals     | Leaving a traceable record during suspensions, fake accounts, or major errors                                   | An appeal does not guarantee immediate restoration, nor can it replace multi-point links established in advance                        |

The multi-platform presence of media is essentially two versions of the same thing as VTubers dividing their work across X, Discord, YouTube, and Threads: **relationships should not be deposited behind only one door.** For news organizations, this concerns whether readers can continue to find a credible outlet during errors or suspensions. For creators and communities, it concerns whether works, files, and contacts can be preserved when a platform changes its rules. The emergence of Miin has made this issue visible, but the real answer is not "choosing another single platform," but rather establishing a multi-point presence that can verify one another.

## Bluebird is not the entire riverbed, but it illustrates what a riverbed can do

Politics provides an important backdrop for the rise of Threads in Taiwan, yet it should not swallow this entire article about "Threads culture" (脆文化). The 2024 elections and the Bluebird Movement (青鳥運動) did indeed bring a massive influx of users to the platform. The Taiwan FactCheck Center has also observed that once major political events fade, high-traffic content often returns to daily whispers and trivial matters of life. [^7]

A study published in 2026 used the Meta Content Library to collect 62,321 Threads posts and 21,572 images related to the Bluebird Movement between May 2024 and June 2025. The research indicates that political mobilization on Threads is no longer just about text dissemination; it also relies on cute animal imagery, memes, AI-generated images, and active user resharing. [^9]

This serves as a reminder that the "lightness" of Threads culture does not mean an absence of politics, but rather that politics must learn to pass through that lightness. Political messages wrap themselves in the guise of cute illustrations, everyday language, and instant jokes. This increases accessibility beyond the threshold of participation, but it also raises the cost of judgment. Readers must ask two things simultaneously: Is this meme funny? And what is it trying to make me believe?

## When "A-bian Baba" Enters the Fray: Politicians Can Also Become Co-authors of Memes

Politicians on Threads are not just subjects of commentary; they can also become co-authors of meme dialogues. On May 10, 2026, Chen Shui-bian's verified account replied to a now-invisible post, writing: "A-bian 'Baba' refers to the final digits 88 of the account, no need to overthink it!?"[^24] This sentence contains no policy discourse or formal statement. It takes a nickname that appeared to be an extension created by netizens and pulls it back to the specific detail of the account suffix "88," using a rhetorical question to make the explanation sound like playing along with a joke.

The key point of this case is not judging whether the meme is "funny," but rather the shift in the role of political figures. In the past, political memes were often one-way adaptations by netizens regarding public figures. On Threads, the person involved can reply, supplement, joke around, or even upload an image with the preset expectation that it will be screenshotted and reposted. Consequently, memes are no longer just a form of mockery; they also become a language for testing social distance.

However, this sense of proximity cannot replace public accountability. While a meme can make a politician appear more like a regular user in the comment section, it cannot substitute for policy data, media questioning, or institutional responsibility. A good way to understand posts like "A-bian Baba" is to see both layers simultaneously: it is indeed a self-response using the familiar tone of the platform, but it also serves as a reminder to readers not to misinterpret approachable interaction as complete public argumentation.

## "Hai-xun" is Not Just Passing By; It's Turning Official Accounts into Comment Section Regulars

In the culture of Threads, _hai-xun_ (patrolling) does not refer to how many posts an account has published itself. Instead, it refers to official accounts proactively leaving their own pages to join conversations under trending threads from users or other brands. Marketing observations from Yahoo/FoodNEXT describe this practice as allowing a brand to join a conversation in the posture of a commenter rather than an advertiser. Examples cited include Kinmen Kaoliang Liquor, FamilyMart, Huayuan Oyster Omelet, Jianan Goat Milk, Fullon Hotels, and various department stores. [^25] This is observational data rather than conversion rate research, but it accurately captures the core of _hai-xun_: a brand must learn to act like "someone who just happens to be there" before it is even their turn to speak.

Specifically, a FamilyMart social media manager once saw a user arranging slices of cake into a circle to make a birthday cake and responded when reposting: "We also sell actual round cakes, please be advised." They also once responded to a question about the indentations in convenience store microwaveable meal trays by jokingly concocting a mock-serious explanation involving the term "outgoing." Yahyo Hicha was dubbed the "King of _Hai-xun_" on Threads by DailyView: in a thread discussing the trend of adding "okay?" (好不好) to the end of sentences, they replied "Milk tea with corn, okay?", forcefully pivoting a sentence pattern unrelated to beverages into a new product promotion. [^26]

| Type of _Hai-xun_     | How Commenting Works                                                                       | Visible Examples                                                                   | Effects and Risks for Brands                                                                                                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Meme-jacking          | Catch the user's sentence pattern or meme first, then insert the product                   | Yahyo Hicha responding to daily threads with "Milk tea with corn, okay?"           | Easy to screenshot and spread; can feel like an intrusive ad if forced too hard                                                   |
| Responsive            | Provide a character-driven reply to existing user creativity or troubles                   | FamilyMart responding to sliced cakes arranged as a round cake                     | Makes the brand seem like it is listening; actual customer complaints still require service rather than just jokes                |
| Summoning             | Consistently appearing in certain types of topics, prompting users to tag the brand        | Jianan Goat Milk managers are often expected to "patrol" into view                 | A beloved persona may lead users to question exactly who is doing the replying                                                    |
| Knowledge Translation | Using a lighthearted tone to introduce cultural relics, exhibitions, or public information | National Palace Museum Southern Branch interacting with relics via short sentences | Can reduce the distance of information; when involving health, safety, or disasters, memes must not replace accurate explanations |

The reason _hai-xun_ is particularly effective on Threads is that the comment itself becomes content. When users see an official account suddenly appear—not just replying to the original poster, but also tagging friends, taking screenshots, and giving the social media manager nicknames—a single comment can expand into an entirely new thread. Brands no longer just buy advertising space; they borrow the existing narratives of strangers to become a recognizable supporting character in that story.

However, "official accounts acting like real people" is both a charm and a pressure. The Jianan Goat Milk incident has already demonstrated this: when netizens view every instance of _hai-xun_ as the personal labor of a specific social media manager, once the collaboration team, visual design, or internal division of labor is exposed, it triggers questions regarding authenticity, attribution, and labor ownership. [^11] Therefore, the key to successful _hai-xun_ is not appearing as often as possible, but whether a brand can clarify: who is replying, when to be humorous, and when to provide a direct answer to a question.

> **Curator's Note:** The best _hai-xun_ is not "the brand hijacking the comments," but rather the user's original topic remaining more fun or useful even after the brand joins in. If every reply is merely a discount code or a forced product plug, the flow will quickly identify it as noise.

## The original three cases were just three stalls in this night market

Chen Shui-bian publicly stated on Threads that his account is personally managed and has been replying to netizens himself. Meanwhile, "Yang Bian" (the goat persona) of Chanan Goat Milk went viral due to high-frequency interaction, only to face follow-up questions regarding who was actually speaking amidst controversies over agency management. [^10] [^11] Both cases are worth preserving because they transform "humanity" from an abstract branding term into a question that users directly inquire about.

Another category includes memes like the four-panel "Worst Politician" polls. Users have publicly questioned why certain posts that appear to be polls come with default options favoring a specific camp, only for the comment section to create another option by saying "Click here for Lai Ching-te." [^12] This is also quintessential Threads: an image that looks like a survey might actually just be a ritual of expression, weaving together designed questions, comment-section additions, and a relay of likes.

However, none of these should constitute the entirety of understanding Taiwan's "Threads culture" (Taiwan Cui). Politicians, brand accounts, and high-conflict polls are easily reported because they have names, drama, and clear stances. What truly sustains the daily flow of the feed are often nameless users: those asking how to cook a certain dish, those looking for someone on behalf of a friend, those leaving a photo of a cat on a traveler's food picture, and those who just want to say "I'm so tired today" late at night.

## Bustle Does Not Equal Reliability, and Truth Is Not Without Intermediaries

The disadvantages of Threads are grown directly from its advantages. While algorithms allow strangers to meet, they also accelerate emotional, short, and sharp content. High interaction can help a student ID find its owner, but it can also make unmethodical voting polls look like public opinion. Meta's policy on political content is as follows: the platform does not proactively recommend political content from accounts you do not follow, but users can still see posts from accounts they do follow and can adjust their relevant preferences. [^13]

The Taiwan FactCheck Center reminds us that all social media platforms, including Threads, carry risks of misinformation and information manipulation. When facing social media trends, the most important thing is neither to treat all content as fake nor to take comment counts as truth; rather, it is to question the source, the original context, and the evidence. [^7] This habit is useful for political information, and equally useful for restaurant recommendations, lost-and-found inquiries, product testing, and any content that seems to be "what everyone is saying."

A more fundamental risk is memory: today's riverbed may be bustling, but tomorrow's posts might be deleted, suspended, or buried by the algorithm. Taiwan's online communities have migrated from BBS, Wretch, Facebook, and LINE; every migration leaves behind URLs that can still be opened, but also loses a portion of photos, comments, and context. [^14] Threads is not the end of this history. It is simply, at this moment, another way for Taiwanese people to gather strangers around a table.

## The Lifecycle of a Post: From Being Seen to Being Handed Over to Systems

If we imagine Taiwan's Threads as a night market, then a post is not finished once it is published; instead, it passes from hand to hand between different stalls. It might begin with being seen via algorithmic recommendations or friend replies; next, it is "caught" through comments, reposts, and the practice of "leaving it for friends to see"; then, it is amplified by brand "coast guard" monitoring, media republication, or becoming a meme template; finally, it either sinks into obscurity or loses visibility due to misinformation, copyright infringement, suspensions, or account deletions. This lifecycle connects the cases mentioned earlier in this article: lost-and-found searches and requests for toilet paper need "speed," while competitions and food memes need to be "catchable," and controversies over bans and fake accounts serve as a reminder that both "speed" and being "catchable" also create risks.

| Stage                         | What General Users Are Doing                                                                     | Typical Case                                                                           | The Most Critical Skill Needed                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Being Seen                    | Encountering information via follows, algorithms, friend comments, or trending threads           | Food photos from Japanese/Korean travelers, local Q&A, public events                   | First, distinguish whether this is personal experience, a retelling, a joke, or news                                           |
| Catching                      | Joining in through replies, images, catchphrases, tagging, or reposting                          | "Leaving it for friends to see," the frog challenge, "I disagree with school starting" | Understanding the preceding context; not using isolated snippets to force others into the original intent                      |
| Amplification                 | Spreading via brand monitoring, media reports, celebrity involvement, or templating              | Brand social media managers, A-bian "Baba," "Off tomorrow"                             | Distinguishing between high engagement and high credibility; not substituting likes for judgment                               |
| Handover                      | Moving information from crowd attention to accountable channels                                  | Searching for student IDs, lost passports, scams, or reporting                         | Masking personal data, preserving necessary evidence, and handing it over to police, stations, consular services, or platforms |
| Preservation or Disappearance | Screenshots, backups, takedowns, suspensions, or account removals changing subsequent visibility | Unwarned suspensions, digging up old posts, meme recycling                             | Preserving context and sources; not treating isolated screenshots as permanent, complete evidence                              |

This framework also answers why some people feel Threads is "noisy" yet cannot leave it. It compresses tasks that would originally require searching, asking friends, browsing forums, reading news, or contacting customer service into a single, replyable, and visible stream. This compression increases serendipity and speed, but pushes the responsibility for trust, privacy, identity, and factual judgment onto every participant. The miracles of lost-and-found searches, brand personalities, political memes, and account suspension incidents mentioned earlier are all results of this same compression effect in different scenarios. [^16] [^22] [^23]

> **Curator's Note:** What is truly worth preserving in Threads culture is not a single catchphrase or a screenshot, but the context of "who caught it, when, and why." Without context, mutual aid turns into rumors, memes turn into accusations, and images lose their original voice.

## Conclusion: A Table, Not a Consensus

The most fascinating aspect of Taiwan's Threads culture is not that it makes everyone agree, but that it temporarily pulls people who would never otherwise sit together to the same table. The student ID from Egypt, food photos from Japanese/Korean travelers, images of blue birds, late-night replies from goat milk social media managers, and a piece of "nonsense" post that requires no conclusion—all can brush past one another within a few swipes.

This digital night market is certainly noisy and prone to "fires": but as long as we remember to distinguish between lost-and-found searches and official services, memes and polls, and real replies versus designed personas, it will be more than just another platform swallowed by traffic. What it leaves behind is a very Taiwanese form of public practice: first catching a stranger's word, and then deciding whether or not to help carry it further.
